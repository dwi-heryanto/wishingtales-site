Mole had been at the spring cleaning since breakfast, and the spring cleaning had been at him. The little brush, the big brush, the pail of water going grey, the dust sheets over the chairs, the smell of soap and old soot. Every year it came around, and every year it made him want to lie down on the floor and stay there.

He threw the brush across the room. It hit the wall and dropped behind the clock. He stood in the middle of the parlour with his paws dirty and his heart hammering, and then he was up the tunnel, scratching at the earth, pushing through the dark, and out. The grass above him was wet. The air smelled of green things.

He rolled in it. He ran along the hedgerow with no hat and no coat and no plan at all, and then the ground fell away and there was water.

It was wide and brown and moving. It had been moving all winter, he supposed, without him. It went past him and away, and the sound of it filled his ears until he could not hear his own breathing. Mole stood on the bank with mud on his feet and his mouth open.

A boat came around the bend. It was small and green and it sat low in the water, and in it sat a brown animal with a whisker or two out of place and a look of great contentment.

"Hullo," said the animal. "You all right?"

"I've never seen it before," said Mole.

"Seen what?"

"The river."

The animal looked at the water as if checking that it was still there. "Well," he said. "It's the only one we've got. Hop in."

Mole looked at the boat. It did not look like a thing that wanted him in it.

"I don't know how," he said.

"You don't have to know how to sit down," said the animal. "I'm Rat. Come on, before the current takes me off without you."

Mole put one foot in the boat and the boat moved, and he put the other foot in and the boat moved the other way, and then he was sitting and the bank was already sliding past. The water lapped at the wood. It was the best sound he had ever heard.

***

Rat rowed for a while with long easy strokes, and Mole watched his shoulders and his wrists and the way the oars went in and came out without a splash.

"You want a go?" said Rat.

"I'll tip us over."

"Probably," said Rat. "But it's a warm day and the river's shallow here. Go on."

They changed places carefully, and Mole took the oars. They were heavier than he expected. He pulled, and the left oar dug in deep while the right one waved at the sky, and the boat turned sideways and began to drift with the current.

"Both together," said Rat. "Not one and then the other. Together."

Mole pulled both together. The boat shot forward and then stopped hard, and the water came over the side in a grey curl and soaked his legs.

"Better," said Rat. "That was almost a stroke."

Mole tried again. The oars went in crooked, and the boat rolled, and he felt the whole thing tilt under him like a loose stone. He grabbed at the side. The side was not there anymore. The river was there instead, and it was cold and it was in his ears and his mouth, and for a moment he was sure the world had ended.

Then a paw closed on his collar and hauled, and he came up coughing and spitting and blinking at the sky.

Rat was holding him with one arm and the boat with the other, and the boat was upside down and drifting away.

"There," said Rat. "That's the first lesson finished."

Mole coughed. "I've drowned."

"You haven't," said Rat. "You've only got wet. It's different."

He towed Mole to the bank, and they sat in the sun with the water running off them. The upside-down boat bumped gently against the roots.

"I'm sorry about your boat," said Mole.

"Boats are for tipping over," said Rat. "If they weren't, nobody would learn anything."

Mole looked at him. Then he laughed, because the sun was warm and the river was still going and he was alive, and Rat laughed too, and they sat there on the bank like two wet stones while the afternoon came on.

The afternoon unrolled along the water. Rat righted the boat and they climbed back in, and this time Mole kept his oars low and his eyes on Rat's shoulders. The river carried them past beds of yellow flags and under a bridge where the swallows had built their cups of mud. Light came down through the leaves in coins that slid across the water.

"You're getting the feel of it," Rat said. "Don't think about the oars. Think about where you want to go, and the oars will find out."

Mole tried that, and for a while the boat went straight, and he felt a small, quiet pride that he did not say out loud. They drifted past a heron who pretended not to see them. A family of coots made a great fuss and scattered. A willow trailed its fingers in the stream. Rat told him the names of things as they passed: the water crowfoot, the kingfisher's hole, the place where the eels gathered in autumn. Mole listened and forgot that he had ever been cleaning.

When the sun began to lean toward the west, Rat turned the boat toward a gap in the bank where a little landing stage sat among the reeds. "Come and eat," he said. "You've earned it."

Rat's home was a burrow in the bank, low and round and warm, with a fire already laid and a kettle that knew its business. There were pictures on the walls and a clock that ticked like a slow heart, and the smell of onions and good bread. Mole sat in a chair that had clearly been waiting for him and watched Rat move about the kitchen, and he thought he had never been anywhere so comfortable. They ate stew with dumplings, and Rat talked about the river and the people on it, and Mole ate until he could not eat more.

"You'll sleep well tonight," said Rat. "The river does that to a person."

Mole nodded, and the fire cracked, and the afternoon went quietly out of the window.

***

Evening came down the river like a hand over a lamp. The water turned the colour of pewter, and the trees on the far bank went dark and soft, and the birds went home. Rat rowed slowly, because there was no reason to hurry, and Mole sat in the stern and watched the first star come out. The air was cooler now, and it smelled of wet earth and cut grass and something else that Mole could not name at first. Then he knew. It was his own house.

It came to him on a little turn of the wind, a smell of dust and old wood and the particular mustiness of a place that has been shut up too long. And under that, faint but unmistakable, the smell of home. Mole's paw tightened on the gunwale. He did not say anything. He did not even turn his head. But something in him went still, and the river went on past, and the star above the trees seemed very far away. Rat rowed twice more, and then the oars stopped.

"You've gone quiet," said Rat.

"I'm all right," said Mole.

"You're not," said Rat. "You've smelled something. I know that face."

Mole looked at the water. "It's my house," he said. "My own house. I left it this morning in the middle of cleaning. I just walked out and never went back. And now I can smell it from here, and it's cold, and there's nobody in it."

Rat said nothing for a moment. Then he shipped the oars and let the boat drift, and the current turned them, slow and gentle, until the bow pointed back the way they had come. "Sit still," he said. "We're going home."

The current did the work for a while, and neither of them spoke. The river ran dark and easy under the boat, and the banks slid past like something remembered rather than seen. Mole sat with his paws in his lap and his heart going in a way he did not quite understand. He had wanted to be on the water all day, and now he was going back, and he could not tell whether he was glad or sorry.

"You'll have to guide me," said Rat at last. "I don't know this stretch after dark, and I don't fancy running us into a root."

Mole lifted his head. "It's not far," he said. "Past the willow that leans over. Then the bank goes soft, and there's a hole under the alder roots. That's the door."

"Under the roots," said Rat. "Very fine. You'll be wanting a lamp."

"I don't have a lamp," said Mole. "I never got one. I was going to, after the cleaning."

Rat made a small sound that might have been a laugh or might have been something else. He took up the oars again and rowed them in close to the bank, where the water moved slower and the reeds stood up black against the last of the light. The bow touched mud. Mole climbed out first and held the line while Rat stepped ashore, and for a moment they both stood still, listening to the small sounds of the night: a fish turning, a bird settling, the endless soft talk of the river.

"Well," said Rat. "Show me."

The hole was smaller than Mole remembered. He had to stoop to get through the door, and then he was inside, and the smell of it closed around him like a hand. Dust and cold ash and the particular mustiness of a place that has been shut up. His little table. His little chair. A saucer with a rind of cheese on it that had gone hard and grey. The hearth was full of old white ash, and the window was furred with cobwebs, and everything was exactly where he had left it, which was somehow the worst part.

"Right," said Rat, from the doorway. "First thing is light. Have you got a candle?"

"There's one somewhere," said Mole. "By the bed, I think."

He found it, and Rat struck a match on the doorpost - he had matches in his pocket, of course he did - and the little room jumped into being around them, small and shabby and real. Rat looked at it without any expression that Mole could read. Then he rolled up his sleeves.

"You take the dustpan," he said. "I'll do the hearth. We'll have this place fit for company before you can say jackdaw."

"I don't have company," said Mole.

"You have me," said Rat. "Get the dustpan."

They worked. Rat swept the hearth and laid a new fire with the dry sticks stacked by the door. Mole went over the shelves with a rag, and the dust came off in grey sheets, and under the dust the wood was warm and brown and his own. He had forgotten how much he liked the grain of it. He had forgotten the little carving on the mantel, a bird he had made himself one winter, which looked more like a fish, but he had kept it anyway. He polished it with his thumb and set it straight.

The fire caught. Light moved along the walls and made the room twice as big. Rat sat back on his heels and looked around with a kind of satisfaction.

"There," he said. "That's a home. You had me worried for a while. I thought you'd brought me to a hole in the ground."

"It is a hole in the ground," said Mole.

"So is mine," said Rat. "So is everyone's, if you go deep enough. The trick is the fire, and the chair, and someone to talk to."

Mole stood in the middle of his own room and did not know what to say. The fire crackled. Outside, the river went on with its long, patient business, and the night came down over the bank like a blanket, and for the first time all day Mole felt his own weight in the world.

***

They had barely settled when the singing started.

It came from up the bank, thin and high and slightly out of tune, and it grew nearer as it came. Mole looked at Rat, who had gone very still with his head cocked, the way he did when he was listening to something he liked.

"Carols," said Rat. "Field mice. They do the rounds this time of year. I'd forgotten it was tonight."

"They'll see the light," said Mole. "They'll knock."

"They always knock," said Rat. "It's the whole point of them."

The singing stopped at the door, and there was a small shuffling, and then a knock - three quick taps, polite but firm. Mole opened the door. On the step stood six or seven field mice, the tallest of them no higher than his knee, all of them wrapped in little coats and scarves, their breath making clouds in the cold. The one in front held a lantern made from a hollowed acorn. They looked up at Mole with bright, serious faces.

"Good evening," said the front mouse. "We're singing for the houses on the bank. May we?"

Mole looked back at Rat, who was already on his feet and pulling the good chair closer to the fire.

"You may," said Mole.

So they sang. It was a song about the river freezing and the river waking, and the stars coming out one by one, and a door left open for someone who was late. Their voices were small and true, and the lantern light moved on the walls, and Mole sat down on the floor by the hearth because his chair was taken, and found that he did not mind at all. Rat hummed along under his breath, and the fire leaned toward them, and the cold stayed outside where it belonged.

When they finished, the front mouse bowed, and Mole gave them the hard rind of cheese, which they accepted with great dignity, and they trooped off up the bank singing the next verse, their lantern bobbing away into the dark.

Mole closed the door. The room was warm and small and his. Rat had already found the kettle and was filling it from the jug by the window, and the fire had settled into a steady red glow.

"You'll stay," said Mole. It was not quite a question.

"I'll stay," said Rat. "The river can do without me till morning."

They drank their tea. The fire made its small noises. Somewhere above the bank the field mice were still singing, very faint now, a thread of sound in the dark. Mole sat in his own chair in his own house with his friend across from him, and the river went on past the door, and that was the whole world, and it was enough.
