The rain had stopped by evening, and the woodcutter was far from his village, deeper in the jungle than he liked. He had hoped to reach the clearing before dark, but the storm had slowed him, and now the path was a guess between roots. He carried his axe over one shoulder and a bundle of kindling under his arm, and he was thinking only of his own fire when he heard the crying.

It was a thin sound, almost lost under the drip of leaves. He stopped and turned his head. A child. He was sure of it. He set the kindling down and followed the sound to a low rock shelf, where the earth had worn itself into a den. The smell of wolf was strong there, and bones lay about the entrance, but the crying came from inside, and the woodcutter had never been a man to walk past a thing like that.

He crouched and looked in. A she-wolf lay on her side in the dark, and against her belly, tucked among three grey pups, was a naked baby with a fist in its mouth. The wolf lifted her head and watched him. Her eyes were yellow and calm, and she did not growl. The woodcutter thought of his own child, who had died in the spring, and he understood that he could not take this one. The wolves had found it first. He backed away, and the crying followed him a little, and then stopped.

"Keep him, then," he said aloud, though no one was there to hear. "He is better off with you than with me."

He walked home without the kindling. The wolves did not follow.

So the boy stayed. The she-wolf licked him clean and let him nurse with her pups, and when he grew too old for milk she brought him meat she had chewed soft. She called him by a name that meant Frog, because he had no fur on his body and moved in a way that made her laugh. He learned to walk on all fours before he learned to stand, and he learned the smells of the jungle before he learned any words at all.

His brothers were the three grey pups. They slept in a heap, and when one of them wandered too far, the boy was the one who brought him back. The she-wolf watched them all with the same patient eye, and if she loved the hairless one a little more, she never said so.

***

By the time the boy could run, he knew that the jungle had a shape, and that the shape had a hole in it. The hole was a tiger named Shere Khan, and he was lame. He had been born with a bad foot, and though he could still kill, he could not chase, so he had learned to take what others left and to call it his. He hunted near the village, where the men were slow and the cattle were slower, and he had grown fat and certain.

One evening, when the boy was playing with a bone outside the den, the tiger came. He did not come quietly. He limped along the path with a heavy, dragging step, and he stopped at the mouth of the cave and looked in with one eye, as if the den were a door he had every right to open.

"The cub," he said. His voice was like a stone rolled in mud. "Bring out the man-cub. He is mine. I claim him."

The she-wolf rose and stood between the tiger and her pups. Her lips pulled back, but she did not snarl yet. "He is not yours," she said. "He was left to me. He is my son."

"He is a man," said Shere Khan. "Men are mine. I have eaten them all my life, and this one is no different. He will grow into a hunter. He will grow into a killer of wolves. Give him to me now, and I will go."

The boy had never seen the tiger before, and he did not understand the words, but he understood the tone. He pressed himself against his mother's leg and watched the big shape in the entrance.

The she-wolf did not move. "You are lame," she said, "and you are greedy. You have taken calves from the village because they cannot run. But this den is not the village, and I am not a calf. Go away, Shere Khan."

The tiger showed his teeth. It was not a smile. "You are bold for a wolf with a house full of children," he said. "I will not argue with a mother. But there is a council. The pack meets at the rock when the moon is full, and there I will speak. If the pack gives him to me, you will not stop me."

"Then we will see what the pack says," said the she-wolf.

Shere Khan turned and limped back into the trees. The boy listened to the dragging step until it faded, and then he looked up at his mother. She nosed him back toward the den and lay down across the entrance, and though she closed her eyes, her ears stayed up all night.

The boy did not sleep either. He lay between his brothers and listened to the jungle, which had never seemed so large, and he thought about the word the tiger had used. Council. He did not know what it meant yet, but he knew it was a thing that would come, and that when it came, it would come for him.

The council rock stood above the river where the water ran quick and loud. Moonlight lay white on the stone, and the pack gathered in a ring, tails low, eyes bright. The boy knelt in the middle, small against the grey, and his mother sat behind him with her chin on his shoulder.

A shadow moved at the edge of the circle. Shere Khan came forward, dragging his lame leg, and the wolves drew back to let him pass. He looked at the boy the way a cat looks at something it means to eat.

"This is mine," said the tiger. "I have waited long enough. Give him to me and I will go."

A growl rolled around the ring. Akela, the old leader, stood on a ledge and spoke in a voice that carried.

"The Law says the pack may keep a cub if two speak for it," said Akela. "Who speaks?"

Baloo the bear came out of the dark, slow and heavy. He sat down beside the boy and yawned.

"I speak for him," said Baloo. "I will teach him the Law. He is no cub of ours, but he is not meat either."

Shere Khan showed his teeth. "A bear and a boy. That is a poor bargain."

Then Bagheera stepped into the light. The panther moved without a sound, and where he walked, the grass did not bend. He stopped between the tiger and the boy.

"I also speak," said Bagheera. "And I pay." He turned to the pack. "I have a bull, fat and young, killed this evening and left by the stream. He is yours if you take the boy into the pack."

A murmur went through the wolves. Meat was meat, and the winter had been thin. Akela looked at the bull lying in the shadows, then at the boy.

"The price is good," said Akela. "The boy stays. Shere Khan, you have heard the pack."

The tiger's eyes went yellow. He looked at the boy for a long moment, and then he turned and limped away without a word. But before the dark took him, he looked back once, and the boy felt that look like a hand on his neck.

The pack ate the bull. The boy ate with them, and Bagheera sat close and said nothing, and that was how he became one of the wolves.

***

Years passed the way water passes a stone, and the boy grew. Baloo taught him the Law in the mornings, when the mist lay on the ground and the birds were still waking. The bear would cuff him when he got it wrong and laugh when he got it right, and the lessons went on until the boy could name every track and every call and every leaf that meant danger.

"Say it again," said Baloo. "What do you do when the snake speaks?"

"I stand still," said the boy. "I do not run. I wait."

"Good. And when the tiger comes?"

"I do not run either."

Baloo grunted. "That is the right answer and the wrong one. A tiger is not a lesson. But you will learn."

Bagheera took him running at night. The panther ran fast and low, and the boy learned to move in the dark and to climb and to swim and to sit still for so long that he forgot he was a boy. They hunted together, and Bagheera never said he was proud, but sometimes he let the boy eat first.

Shere Khan did not forget. He stayed away from the pack, but the jungle is small, and sometimes the boy would find a kill he had not made, or hear a dragging step behind him and turn to find nothing. The tiger waited. That was what tigers did.

And the pack began to change. The old wolves who remembered the bull grew slow and stiff. The young wolves had not been there. They saw the boy and did not see a wolf. They saw something that walked on two legs and smelled of smoke.

One evening, the boy sat with Bagheera on a branch above the river, and the panther spoke quietly.

"They are talking about you," said Bagheera. "At the next council, they will say you are not one of us."

The boy looked down at the water. "Akela will speak for me."

"Akela is old," said Bagheera. "And Shere Khan has been busy. He does not need to hunt you. He only needs to wait until the pack hunts you for him."

The boy was silent. He had learned the Law, and the Law said the pack was his family. But families change, and the jungle had never seemed so large as it did that night, with the moon rising and the river running and the tiger somewhere in the dark, patient as a stone.

The council rock stood above the river, worn smooth by generations of paws. The pack gathered in the dark, eyes shining like small moons. Akela sat apart, his grey muzzle resting on his paws. He was old, and everyone knew it. Shere Khan did not come. He did not need to. His voice drifted from the shadows beyond the circle, low and patient.

"The man-cub is not one of you," said the tiger. "He never was. Give him to me, and I will leave you in peace."

The wolves shifted. A young hunter, barely more than a cub himself, spoke first. "He smells of smoke. He walks on two legs. He is not our brother."

Another took it up. "The Law says the pack is one. But he is not pack."

Akela raised his head. "I have led you for many seasons. I have never asked you to break the Law. The boy was bought with a bull. He is one of us."

"You are old," said the young wolf. "Your time is done."

The circle tightened. The boy stood at the edge, his back to the rock, and felt the cold come up through his feet. Bagheera was not there. Baloo was not there. He was alone among his own, and their eyes had changed.

Then a shape dropped from the branch above, silent as a shadow. Bagheera stood between the boy and the pack, his fur bristling.

"The Law is the Law," said the panther. "But the Law also says a cub may speak for himself when he is grown. Let him speak."

The wolves paused. The boy stepped forward. His voice was steady, though his hands trembled.

"I have run with you. I have eaten with you. I have kept the Law. If you will not have me, then I will go. But I will not go to Shere Khan."

He turned and walked out of the circle. No one stopped him. The tiger's laugh followed him into the dark.

Bagheera found him by the river, sitting with his feet in the water. "You did well," said the panther. "But you cannot stay. Shere Khan will hunt you now. You must go to the village."

"The village?" The boy looked up. "They will not have me either."

"They will," said Bagheera. "They will see a boy, not a wolf. And there is something there you need. Fire."

The boy remembered the red flower. He had seen it once, in a pot outside a hut, and the men had fed it with dry wood and it had eaten and grown and given light. He had been afraid of it then. Now he was not.

"Go now," said Bagheera. "Before the moon sets. I will watch the pack."

The boy stood. He touched the panther's head once, then turned and ran. He ran through the grass and the trees, past the places he knew, and he did not look back. The village was a cluster of lights in the distance, and he went toward them, and the night closed behind him.

***

The village was quiet when he reached it. Dogs barked once, then fell silent. He crept between the huts until he found the one with the red flower burning in a clay pot by the door. A woman sat inside, rocking a child. He waited until she slept, then took a branch from the fire. The flame clung to the wood, small and bright, and he carried it away like a treasure.

He walked back through the jungle, and the fire walked with him. The animals saw him and fled. The fire was a thing they did not understand, and it made him something they did not understand either.

Shere Khan found him at the edge of the clearing. The tiger was lame, but he was still huge, and his eyes were yellow and cold.

"You," said the tiger. "You come to me at last."

"I come to send you away," said the boy. He held out the burning branch. "This is the red flower. You have seen it. You know what it does."

The tiger stepped back. The fire crackled between them, and the boy's face was lit from below, and he did not look like a boy anymore.

"Go," said the boy. "Go and never come back."

The tiger snarled, but the fire was stronger than his hunger. He turned and limped into the dark, and the boy watched until he was gone.

Then the boy sat down on the grass, and the fire burned low, and he was alone.

Bagheera came to him in the morning. "It is done," said the panther. "The pack will not follow him. They are afraid of you now."

"I do not want them to be afraid," said the boy.

"It does not matter what you want," said Bagheera. "You have fire. You are not a wolf. You are a man."

The boy looked at his hands. They were brown and scarred and they had done things no wolf could do. He stood up.

"I will go to the village," he said. "But first I will take his skin. It is mine by right."

Bagheera said nothing. The boy went to the place where the tiger had fallen, and he took the striped skin, and he carried it back to the council rock. The wolves were there, and they saw him coming, and they parted to let him through. He laid the skin on the stone.

"This is what I leave you," he said. "Remember me."

Akela came forward, old and slow. "You were always one of us," said the wolf. "Even when we forgot."

The boy knelt and put his arms around the old wolf's neck. Then he stood, and he looked at Bagheera, and at Baloo, who had come at last and stood blinking in the sun.

"I will come back," said the boy. "When I am a man, I will come back."

He walked away, and the jungle closed behind him, and the village was waiting. He did not look back, because the Law said a wolf does not look back, and he had been a wolf once. But he carried the jungle with him, in his heart, and he always would.
