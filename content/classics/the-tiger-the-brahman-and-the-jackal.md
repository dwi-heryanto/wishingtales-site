The path through the sal forest was old and narrow. Woodcutters had worn it smooth, and so had the hooves of goats. Each morning the teacher walked it with his bundle of books under one arm. He was not a rich man. His sandals had been mended twice. His coat had been turned inside out so many times that the seams had given up pretending. But he walked with his chin up. A man who has taught thirty years of children to read has earned a certain posture.

On this particular morning, the forest was quieter than usual. The cicadas had not started yet. The light came down in long, dusty bars. He was thinking about a boy named Dev. Dev could not remember the difference between the two marks that made a long vowel and a short one. The teacher was composing a sentence in his head to explain it kindly when a voice came out of the ground.

"Teacher. Teacher, wait."

The voice was deep and rough, like a millstone turning slowly. The teacher stopped and looked around. Nothing lay on the path ahead but a lizard and a fallen branch. Then he looked to the left, where the undergrowth was thick. There, half-buried in the ferns, sat an iron cage.

It was a big cage, the kind a rajah might use to move a dangerous animal from one place to another. The bars were as thick as a man's thumb. The door was bolted with a lock the size of a fist. Inside, pressed against the bars, was a tiger.

The tiger was enormous. Its coat was the colour of late-season wheat, and its stripes were black as a moonless night. Its eyes were yellow and patient. They followed the teacher as he stepped closer.

"You are a teacher," the tiger said. "I can tell by the books."

"I am," the teacher said. He kept his voice steady, though his heart had begun to knock. "And you are a tiger in a cage. How did this happen?"

"A hunter," the tiger said. "He dug a pit and covered it with leaves. I fell in, and he hauled me up in this cage, and he went to fetch a cart to carry me to the city. He has been gone since yesterday noon. I am thirsty, teacher. I am very thirsty."

The teacher looked at the lock. It was a simple lock, the kind that opens with a key or a strong twist. The hunter had left the key in it, perhaps thinking no one would be fool enough to use it.

"If I let you out," the teacher said slowly, "you will eat me."

"No," the tiger said. "I swear it. On my mother's stripes, I swear it. I will not eat you. I will walk away into the forest and you will never see me again. But if you leave me here, I will die of thirst before the hunter comes back. Then you will have to explain to your students why you walked past a dying creature and did nothing."

The teacher considered this. He thought about Dev and the long vowel. He thought about the look on a child's face when a lesson is understood at last. He thought about how heavy a book is and how light a kindness is.

"You are a very convincing tiger," he said.

"I am a very thirsty tiger," the tiger said. "That is different."

The teacher set down his books on a flat stone and approached the cage. The tiger watched him without moving. The lock was warm from the sun. The teacher took hold of it with both hands and turned.

***

The lock opened with a click that seemed very loud in the quiet forest. The teacher pulled the bolt back. The door swung outward on hinges that had not been oiled in some time. They groaned like a man waking from a bad dream.

The tiger did not move at first. It sat on its haunches in the cage and looked at the open door as if it were a word it had not expected to read. Then it rose, slowly, and stepped out onto the path.

The teacher stepped back. He was not a small man, but the tiger stood taller at the shoulder than his waist. When it shook itself, dust came off its coat in a cloud that smelled of old straw and iron.

"There," the teacher said, and his voice was a little higher than he wanted it to be. "You are free. Go on, then. The forest is that way."

The tiger did not go. It stood on the path and looked at the teacher with its yellow eyes. Then it sat down again, right there in the middle of the path, as if it had all the time in the world.

"I am hungry," the tiger said.

"I am sure you are," the teacher said. "There are deer in the forest. There are wild pigs. Go and find them."

"I have been in a cage for a day and a night," the tiger said. "I am not in a condition to chase deer. You, on the other hand, are right here."

The teacher's mouth went dry. "You promised," he said. "You swore on your mother's stripes."

"I did," the tiger agreed. "But I was thirsty then, and now I am hungry. A promise made in a cage is not the same as a promise made in the open air. Any teacher knows that."

The teacher took another step back. His heel found a root, and he had to put a hand out to keep from falling. "This is not right," he said. "This is not how the world should work. I let you out. I was kind to you."

"Kindness," the tiger said, and it yawned, showing a mouth full of teeth like white knives. "You are a teacher. Ask the world what it thinks of kindness. Go on. Ask the tree, ask the buffalo, ask the road. They will tell you what I am telling you."

The teacher looked at the tiger, and the tiger looked back, patient and certain. The books lay on the flat stone where he had left them. The sun was still coming down in long, dusty bars, and somewhere in the forest a cicada had started to sing at last.

"I will ask them," the teacher said. "And when they say you are wrong, you will let me go."

The tiger flicked its tail. "Ask them," it said.

***

The teacher walked to the tree first, because it was nearest, and because it was the oldest thing he knew. It stood at the edge of the clearing with its roots spread out like the fingers of a hand pressed flat against the earth. Its bark was ridged and grey. High up, its branches held a roof of leaves that let the light through in coins. He had taught beneath this tree for nine seasons. He put his palm against the trunk, and the bark was warm from the sun.

"You have seen a great deal," he said to the tree. "You were here before the village was here. Tell me plainly: when a creature is shown kindness, does it not owe kindness back?"

The leaves moved, though there was no wind. A sound came out of the trunk, low and slow, like a rope drawn over wood. "I give shade," the tree said. "I give shade to the ox and the goat and the man with the axe. Do they thank me? The man with the axe comes back with his axe. He cuts my branches for his fire and he does not look up at the leaves he is taking. The goat eats my bark. The ox leans on me until my roots ache. Kindness goes out from me every day, and nothing comes back but the axe."

"That is not the same," the teacher said. His voice was dry. "I am not asking about shade. I am asking about a promise."

"Promises," the tree said, and the sound of it was almost a sigh. "I have heard promises under my branches. Men make them and men forget them, and the tree remembers both. Ask the buffalo. She has carried more promises on her back than I have leaves."

The teacher stepped away from the trunk. His hand left a print in the dust of the bark, and he looked at it for a moment before he turned. Behind him, the tiger had not moved. It sat in the long grass with its tail curled around its feet, and it watched him go with the calm of something that has already eaten.

***

The buffalo was standing in the shallows of the river, knee-deep, with her head down and her horns catching the light. She was old and grey about the muzzle, and her hide hung loose on her bones. Flies moved around her in a slow cloud. The teacher came down the bank and stood on the mud and asked his question again. The buffalo lifted her head and chewed and looked at him.

"I have pulled a plough since I was three years old," she said. "I pulled it through wet and dry. I pulled it when my shoulder was sore and when my feet were cracked. I gave milk and I gave calves and I gave the strength of my back. And when my teeth went and my milk went, they put a rope on me and led me to the river and left me here. So do not ask me about kindness, teacher. I know what it is worth. It is worth what a rope is worth when the rope is taken off."

The teacher looked at the water moving past her legs. "Then you think the tiger is right," he said.

"I think the tiger is a tiger," the buffalo said. "Ask the road. The road has been walked on longer than any of us."

He climbed the bank again and found the road where it ran past the edge of the forest, pale and dusty and worn into ruts by carts and feet and the hooves of animals going to market. He stood in the middle of it and spoke to the dust. "Road," he said, "you carry everyone. Do you not remember who was kind to you?"

The road was quiet for a long moment. Then a voice came up out of the dust, thin and dry, the way a stone sounds when it is dragged. "Everyone walks on me," the road said. "The man with the load and the man with the whip and the child with no shoes. They take the way I give them and they never look down. I have been kind since before your village had a name, and I have been walked on every day of it. That is the way of the world. The tiger knows it. You are the only one who does not."

The teacher stood in the road and said nothing. The dust settled on his feet. He thought of his books on the flat stone, and the careful lessons in them, and how none of them had prepared him for a road that talked back. He turned and walked slowly to the clearing. The tiger was waiting, and the sun was lower now, and the cicada had stopped.

"Well?" the tiger said.

The teacher looked at the cage, and at the open door, and at the ground where his books lay. "They all say the same," he said quietly. "They say the world does not pay kindness back."

The tiger yawned again. "Then we understand each other," it said. "Come here. I am hungry, and you have been kind, and kindness is worth what it is worth."

***

Something moved at the edge of the clearing, unhurried, the way a leaf turns.

The jackal came out of the ferns with its nose down, following a smell only it could justify. It was a small animal, dusty and thin, with one ear that had been chewed and had healed crooked. It stopped when it saw the tiger. Then it saw the teacher. Then it sat down and scratched behind the crooked ear as if it had interrupted nothing more interesting than a conversation about weather.

The tiger watched it the way a cat watches a bird that has not yet decided to be afraid. "Go away," it said. "This is a private matter."

"Is it," said the jackal. It yawned. "I only came for the shade. The road is hot and the ferns are cool and I am not particular about company." It looked at the cage, and then at the tiger, and then at the cage again. Its head tilted. "Though I will say, I am confused."

The teacher had not moved. His books lay on the ground where he had dropped them, and his hands hung at his sides. He was looking at the jackal as a drowning man looks at a branch.

"Confused," the tiger said flatly. "About what."

"About the arrangement," said the jackal. "I heard you talking. I heard the word cage and the word door and the word kind, and I am trying to put them in an order that makes sense, and I cannot do it." It scratched again, slowly, as if the problem were located somewhere under its ear. "You were in the cage. That much I have."

"I was," said the tiger. "And then this man opened it."

"That is the part I lose," said the jackal. "You were in the cage. This man opened it. And now you are out and you are going to eat him. That is the story, as I have it. Is that the story?"

"That is the story," said the tiger. "It is not a difficult story."

"No," said the jackal. "No, it is not difficult. It is only that I cannot see it." It rose and walked a little closer, its paws making no sound on the packed earth. "Because look at this cage. Look at it. It is a small cage. It is a hunter's cage, built for a dog or a goat or a boy with thin shoulders. And look at you." It stopped, and looked the tiger up and down with an expression of polite, genuine difficulty. "You are not a small animal. I do not say this to insult you. I say it because it is the thing my eyes tell me."

The tiger's tail moved once, slowly, side to side.

"I am a tiger," it said.

"You are," the jackal agreed. "And that is the cage. So you see my trouble. A tiger that big, in a cage that small — I cannot put those two facts in the same thought. My head will not hold them."

"It held me," said the tiger. "I was in it. For a day and a night. I was in it when this man came down the road."

"Then I am wrong," said the jackal pleasantly. "I am often wrong. But I would rather be shown than told. Could you not show me? It would take a moment, and then I would understand the story, and I could go back to the ferns and stop being a nuisance."

The tiger looked at the teacher, who had not spoken and who was standing very still with his eyes on the ground.

"You are a nuisance," the tiger said. "But I am also tired of being called a liar by an animal with one ear."

***

It walked to the open door and turned sideways, and folded itself in. It was a strange thing to watch, the way water goes into a jar. First the head, then the shoulders, then the long back, the striped flank scraping the iron. It had to crouch. It had to put its great paws together and draw them in. It had to lower its head, which a tiger does not like to do.

"There," it said, from inside. Its voice was muffled and close, and it filled the cage the way a fist fills a glove. "There. Are you satisfied? Do you see it now?"

"I see the cage," said the jackal. It had walked around to the door. "I see the cage, and I see a tiger in it, and — no, I am sorry, I still do not see how you got in. Perhaps if the door were closed, it would look more like the story. The story has the door closed, at the start, before the man comes."

"Then close it," said the tiger, "and look, and be quick about it, because I am hungry and the light is going."

The jackal put its shoulder to the door. It was heavy, and it took a moment, and the hinges complained. Then it swung shut with a sound like a dropped pot, and the bolt dropped with it, and the tiger was in the cage, and the door was closed.

There was a silence in the clearing. The tiger's eyes moved from the jackal to the bolt to the jackal again. Something went out of them, some heat, and what was left was only a large animal in a small space, looking out between the bars.

"Open it," it said.

"No," said the jackal. It said it without any particular pleasure. "I do not think I will. You had the story right, as far as you went. It is only that I could not see how a tiger that big got into a cage that small, and now I have seen it, and now I know, and now the door is shut. That is the whole of it."

The teacher bent and gathered his books. His hands were shaking, and he had to do it twice. He looked at the jackal, and the jackal was already turning away, back toward the ferns, back toward whatever smell had brought it there.

"Wait," the teacher said. "You — I should thank you. I should —"

But the ferns closed, and the crooked ear was gone. There was only the sound of something small moving through the undergrowth without hurrying, as if it had never been in the clearing at all.

The teacher stood a moment with his books against his chest. Then he walked home along the dusty road, and the road said nothing, and the sun went down. Behind him in the dark the tiger began, very quietly, to complain.
