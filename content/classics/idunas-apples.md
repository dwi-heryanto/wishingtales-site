Loki liked an audience. That was the trouble with Loki, and everyone in Asgard knew it. So when a stranger in a coat of eagle feathers settled on the ridge above the orchard and asked him what the gods ate to stay so young, Loki sat down on a warm stone and told him everything.

"Apples," he said, and waved a hand at the trees. "Not these. Iduna keeps the real ones in a casket. One bite and a god forgets he ever had a wrinkle."

The stranger's hood tipped up. Under it, an eye the color of old bronze looked at Loki a long moment.

"And she keeps them where?"

"With her," said Loki. "She is careful, and she does not share." He stretched. "I have been in this orchard since dawn, friend, and my bag is empty, and my legs are tired. Fly me home and I will tell no more tales."

The eagle-feathered coat rustled, and the stranger laughed, a low sound like a gate swinging.

"Home?" he said. "I walked this far to find someone who knows the apples. I will not fly you home for nothing. Bring me the casket. Bring it out past the wall and set it down, and I will carry you wherever you like."

Loki's mouth went dry. He looked at the casket in his mind, small and golden, sitting in Iduna's cool room with the shutters closed.

"She will not let it go," he said.

"Then think of something," said the stranger. "You are clever. Everyone says so."

And he spread his arms, and the feathers stretched wide, and the shadow of him fell over Loki and the whole orchard and the ridge beyond. Loki stood in that shadow and thought, and his thoughts were not good ones.

***

Iduna was in her garden the next morning, sorting the last of the summer seeds into paper twists, when Loki came along the path with his hands behind his back and his face arranged into something like worry.

"Iduna," he said. "I found a tree. Down past the wall, in the little wood by the stream. The apples on it are the color of sunlight, and one of them has a leaf shaped like a star."

She looked up. "There are no apple trees past the wall."

"There is now," said Loki, and he smiled. "You should bring the casket. If the fruit is as good as it looks, we could put one in with your store, and the gods would have two kinds of youth instead of one."

Iduna hesitated. She was not foolish, and she had carried that casket a long way. But the morning was bright, and Loki's voice was warm, and she had been alone in her garden for a week.

"A leaf shaped like a star?" she said.

"Like a star," said Loki.

She wrapped the casket in her shawl and came with him. They went out through the gate, down the slope, and into the little wood by the stream. No tree stood there. Iduna turned, and Loki was already backing away, and the sky went dark.

A shape came down out of the light with its wings folded and its claws open. It took the casket. It took Iduna too, shawl and all, and rose. The stream below them shrank to a thread, and the wall of Asgard to a grey line, and the last thing Loki heard was Iduna calling his name like a question he could not answer.

He stood in the empty wood for a long time, holding a branch he had picked up and did not remember picking up.

Nobody noticed the first day. On the second day, Odin came down from his high seat and his beard was the colour of frost on a roof. "Look at my hands," he said, and held them out. The knuckles were big as walnuts, and the skin hung loose. By the third day the great hall smelled of camphor and old wool. Thor's hair was grey as ash, and he sat by the fire with a blanket on his knees, and Freyja's gold necklace had gone dull as a copper coin left in the rain. "Where is Iduna?" asked Frigg, and nobody could answer. Every god in Asgard was growing old all at once, and no one knew why.

Loki kept to the edges of the hall. He brought water. He mended a chair. He was very helpful, and very quiet, and his hands would not stay still. But Asgard has a hundred eyes, and each one had been watching him for a long time. They watched him drop a cup. They watched him not eat. They watched him startle at a crow. "Loki," said Odin at last, "come here." A hundred eyes turned. "I only told him about the apples," Loki said, and his voice went thin. "I only said they were good." The hall was silent. "He took her," Loki said. "He took her to Jarnvid. And I did not stop him." Odin did not shout. He only looked, and that was worse. Then Freyja stood up, stiff and old, and said: "Then you will bring her back."

***

Freyja's falcon skin hung on a peg in her cold room upstairs. It was grey and soft and smelled of high wind. "You will fly fast," she said, "and you will fly straight, and you will not be clever about it. Cleverness is what lost her." Loki put it on. His arms went wide and feathered, and the room grew small and far beneath him. He went out over the wall and north, over the water, over the black pines of Jarnvid, where the trees grow crooked and the path forgets itself.

He found her in a stone hall with no fire. Iduna sat against the wall with the casket on her knees, thin and tired, and her hair was going grey at the temples. "You," she said, without surprise. "I came to carry you home," said Loki. "Can you fly?" He shook his head. "Then how?" He thought. "I can make you small," he said, "and carry you between my claws. But you must hold still, and you must trust me, and I know what that costs." Iduna looked at him a long moment. Then she opened the casket, took out one apple, and set it in her sleeve. She shut the lid. "Do it," she said. And Loki turned her into a small brown nut, and gripped it gently, and the casket with her, and rose into the dark.

Cold air hit him like a wall. Loki climbed, and the casket dragged at his grip, and below him the crooked trees of Jarnvid shrank to a dark moss. Then a sound came up from the stone hall, a great clap of wings, and the giant rose after him in his eagle feathers, wide as a barn roof.

"Fly, little falcon!" the eagle called, almost kindly. "I only want what is mine!"

Loki did not answer. He beat his wings hard and kept the nut tight between his claws. Over the pine tops he went, and the eagle came behind, one wingbeat back, then half a wingbeat, and the wind of those feathers pushed him forward like a hand.

The wall of Asgard showed at last, a grey line on the edge of the sky. Loki's wings burned. His breath came in small torn pieces. Still the eagle gained.

On the wall, the gods were watching. Heimdall saw them first and shouted, and old grey faces turned up, and hands that had been slow for weeks found their strength.

"Light the wall!" Freyja cried. "Pile it high and light it!"

They heaped the braziers and the dry wood of the watchfires, and the fire leapt up along the stones, gold and roaring. Loki dropped low over the flames and shot through the gap. The eagle followed, screaming, and his wide wings caught the blaze.

Feathers flared. The eagle tumbled, beating at the air, and fell away from the wall in a shower of sparks. He fell a long way, and the sound of him was lost in the wind.

***

Loki struck the wall and rolled, and the nut slipped from his claws. It bounced, and stopped, and there was Iduna, on her knees on the stones, with the casket beside her and one apple still in her sleeve.

She stood up slowly. Her hair was grey at the temples, and her hands shook, but she opened the casket and held it out.

"Eat," she said. "All of you. Quickly."

The gods came crowding, and each took an apple, and the years fell off them like water. Grey hair darkened, wrinkles smoothed, and old backs straightened. Freyja laughed out loud and looked at her own hands as if they were new.

"You brought her home," she said to Loki. He sat against the wall, breathing hard, and said nothing at all.

Later, in the dwarf smithy under the hill, the hammers rang. Loki paid his debts there: a laugh for the gods, and gold spun fine as thread for Sif's new hair. The dwarfs worked, and Loki carried the shining strands up the long stair to her, and she thanked him.

Iduna came to the door of the smithy before he left. She set a small brown nut in his hand and closed his fingers over it.

"For the next one you carry home," she said.

Loki looked at it a while. Then he put it in his pocket, and went up into the light.
