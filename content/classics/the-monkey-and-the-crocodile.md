The rose-apple tree leaned out over the water so far that on still mornings its lowest leaves touched their own reflection. The monkey had lived in it longer than he could remember. He knew every branch the way a person knows the rooms of a house. He knew which limb creaked, and which crotch held rainwater after a storm. He knew which high fork gave the widest view of the bend where the river turned brown and slow.

Most mornings he ate alone. Then one morning a crocodile hauled himself onto the sandbar below and lay there blinking in the light. He was not hunting and not hiding. He was just resting with his jaw half open.

The monkey watched him for a long while from a middle branch. At last he called down, "You look tired. Did the current fight you all night?"

The crocodile lifted his head. "It did. Twice it pushed me sideways into the reeds. I am too old to argue with a river."

"Then don't argue. Eat something." The monkey plucked a rose-apple, leaned out, and let it fall. It struck the sandbar and rolled to the crocodile's foot.

The crocodile sniffed it, then took it gently, the way a creature takes something it does not quite trust. He chewed. His eyes went wide and then narrow with pleasure. "What is that?"

"Rose-apple. The tree makes more than I can eat. The wind knocks them down and the ants get them first."

"The ants are fools," said the crocodile.

After that he came every morning. The monkey would shake the branch and the fruit would patter down like rain on a roof. The crocodile would eat until he sighed and rolled onto his side. They talked about small things: the heron who thought too well of himself, the way the water smelled before rain, the fish who jumped at nothing. The monkey told jokes and the crocodile laughed with his whole long body, which made the sand shake.

Some mornings the crocodile brought news from upstream. Some mornings he brought nothing but himself, and the monkey found he liked the second kind better. A friendship had grown there without either of them naming it, the way the moss had grown on the north side of the trunk.

***

One evening the crocodile swam home with a bundle of rose-apples wrapped in a leaf. He held them carefully in his teeth so the current would not steal them. His wife was waiting in the warm shallows by the reeds, and she smelled the fruit before she saw it.

"What have you brought?" she asked.

He set the leaf down and unfolded it. "From the tree I told you about. The monkey shakes them loose for me."

She ate one. Then another. She ate the third slowly, with her eyes closed, and when she opened them they had a different light in them.

"This is the sweetest thing that has ever been in my mouth," she said. "Sweeter than fish. Sweeter than anything."

"He is generous with them," said the crocodile, pleased.

His wife turned the last fruit over in her claws. "If the fruit he eats is this sweet," she said, "think what his heart must taste like."

The crocodile went still. "His heart."

"Bring it to me." She said it plainly, as she might ask for a fish. "Invite him to dinner. Carry him across. Do it in the deep water where he cannot swim away."

"He is my friend," said the crocodile.

"And I am your wife." She set the fruit down and did not eat it. "I have given you my years and my nest and my silence when you came home late. I am asking for one thing, and you are telling me about a friend."

The crocodile lay in the shallows a long time that night. He thought of the monkey's voice calling down through the leaves, easy and warm. He thought of his wife's eyes when she had eaten the fruit. Love pulled him one way and love pulled him the other. He discovered that being torn does not make a creature gentle. It makes him tired, and then it makes him decide.

By morning he had decided. He did not like what he had decided, but he had decided it. He told himself that a wife is owed more than a friend. He told himself that the monkey would never know. He told himself that the water was deep and quick and would do most of the work.

He swam downstream with his jaw set, rehearsing the invitation in his head.

***

Morning came up the river grey and then gold. The crocodile came with it, cruising slow along the bank as if nothing in the world had changed. The monkey was already awake, swinging low on a branch that dipped toward the water. When he saw the long dark shape break the surface he called out before he thought about it.

"You are late today. I have been saving the best ones."

"I am here now," the crocodile said. His voice came out flat, and he heard it, and he tried again warmer. "The current was against me."

The monkey dropped three rose apples into the water, one after another. He watched the crocodile take them without hurry. Everything was as it always was. That was the strange part, the crocodile thought. The tree, the fruit, the small brown hands throwing. It should have been harder than this to ruin a good thing.

"I have been thinking," the crocodile said. "My wife has never met you. She hears me talk of the tree and the fruit and the monkey in it, and she has begun to feel left out of something good."

"Then bring her," the monkey said. "There is fruit enough for a whole family."

"She does not swim well, and she will not come so far from the nest. But she has asked me to invite you. Dinner, at our home, on the far bank. She has been preparing all week."

The monkey went still on the branch. The crocodile felt his own heart knock once against his ribs and hated himself for it. Then the monkey laughed, delighted, the way he laughed at everything.

"Me? Dinner? I have never been anywhere but this tree."

"Then come. I will carry you. It is nothing, a short ride, and the water is calm this morning."

The monkey looked at the far bank, where the trees stood dark and unknown. Then he looked at the crocodile's broad patient back. He had known this creature for a year. He had fed him from his own hand. There was no fear in him anywhere, and the crocodile saw that, and it was worse than any anger.

"Well," the monkey said, and dropped lightly to the lowest branch, and from there to the water's edge. "If your wife is cooking, I should not keep her waiting."

He stepped onto the crocodile's back and settled himself between the ridged plates, holding on with his feet. The crocodile pushed off from the bank into the current, and the tree began to slide away behind them.

***

The river was wider than it looked from the tree. Halfway across, the banks had shrunk to two thin green lines. The water ran deep and dark beneath them, cold enough that the monkey could feel it through the crocodile's hide.

"Your wife must be a fine cook," the monkey said, "to live so far from company."

The crocodile did not answer at once. He swam on, steady, and the far bank did not seem to come any closer.

"There is something I should tell you," he said at last.

The monkey's hands tightened, very slightly, on the ridged plates. "Tell me, then."

"It is not dinner. Or it is, but not the way I said." The crocodile's voice was low and careful, as if he were carrying something heavy and did not want to drop it. "My wife ate the fruit you sent. She said it was the sweetest thing that ever crossed her tongue. And then she said that a fruit so sweet must come from a heart sweeter still. She asked me for it. She asked me for your heart, and she would not be turned."

The water slid past. Somewhere behind them a bird called twice and stopped.

"And you said yes," the monkey said.

"I said yes." The crocodile's tail moved once, slow and powerful, holding them in place against the current. "I told myself a wife is owed more than a friend. I have been telling myself that all night. It has not gotten any truer."

The monkey looked down at the black water and the long jaw below him. He understood exactly how far he was from any branch. The cold came up through his feet. He made himself breathe, slow, the way he breathed when a branch cracked under him and he had one heartbeat to choose.

"Well," he said, and his voice came out easy, almost amused. "You should have said so at the start. It would have saved us both a swim."

The crocodile's head turned, just slightly, uncertain.

"I mean it," the monkey said. "But you have made one mistake, and it is a bad one, and you will be sorry for it when your wife hears."

The crocodile's jaw hung open a moment. "A mistake," he said. "What mistake?"

The monkey shifted his weight on the broad, ridged back, as though settling in for a story. "My heart," he said. "You want to carry it home to your wife. But I don't keep it with me."

The crocodile blinked. The current nudged them sideways. "Everyone keeps their heart with them," he said, slowly, the way a creature speaks when the ground has moved under him. "It is in the chest. I have heard it beat."

"Have you?" The monkey tipped his head. "What you heard was only the small one I carry for ordinary days. The real heart, the sweet one, the one that makes the fruit taste as it does, I keep in the hollow of the rose-apple tree. Safe. It would be foolish to swim around with a thing like that inside me. Any crocodile could take it." He said this last part lightly, and let it sit.

Water lapped at the crocodile's flanks. He was thinking, and the monkey could see him thinking, and could see the moment the thought found a comfortable shape. "Then we must go back," the crocodile said. "You will fetch it, and we will go on to dinner."

"Of course," said the monkey. "Turn around."

The crocodile turned. His tail swept a wide arc, and the bank swung back into view, the tree standing over it with its leaves catching the early light. The monkey did not look at the tree. He looked at the water, and at his own hands, and kept his voice in the easy place. "You know," he said, "I have never been to your home. Is it far?"

"Not far," said the crocodile. "Past the sandbar, under the roots." He swam steadily. The bank grew nearer. "My wife will be pleased. She has been asking."

"Wives ask," the monkey agreed. "It is how they love us." The water shallowed. Pebbles showed through it, brown and green. The crocodile's belly scraped the bottom, and he stopped. The monkey stepped off onto the warm mud of the shore. His legs were cold to the knee. He did not run. He walked to the tree, put one hand on the bark, and only then let out a long breath.

***

The monkey climbed. He went up past the low fork, past the place where the branches thinned, up to the highest limb that would hold him. There he sat, and looked down.

The crocodile waited in the shallows. "Have you got it?" he called. "The heart?"

The monkey rested his chin on his knees. "No," he said. "I have not got it. There is no heart in the tree. There is no sweet heart anywhere but in my chest, and it is beating very fast, and it is staying where it is."

The crocodile was silent a long while. The river went on past him. "You lied to me," he said at last, and his voice was not angry. It was the voice of someone doing arithmetic and not liking the answer.

"I did," said the monkey. "You lied first. You invited me to dinner with a lie in your mouth. I only answered in the same language."

"My wife," the crocodile began, and stopped.

"Your wife wants a heart that is not hers," the monkey said. "Go home and tell her the tree had none. Tell her the monkey was too clever, or too lucky, or too quick. Tell her whatever will let you sleep. But do not come back for me, and do not bring her."

The crocodile's tail moved once, then was still. "We were friends," he said.

"We were," said the monkey. "I fed you fruit every morning for a year, and I was glad to. That was real. It is still real. But a friend who carries you into deep water to take what is inside you is not a friend to carry, and I will not pretend otherwise."

A breeze came off the river and turned the leaves. The monkey looked down at the long, patient shape in the shallows, and something in him ached, and he let it ache. "Go home," he said. "And next time you come, bring the monkey his heart. I will keep it here, and you may visit it, and we will see what kind of friends we can be after this."

The crocodile turned and slid back into the deep water. He did not look up. The monkey watched until the ridged back was only a wake, and then nothing. The river went on the way rivers do, as if nothing had happened in it at all. He climbed a little higher, found a branch that faced the far shore, and sat there in the sun, breathing, alive, and alone with the heart he had kept.
