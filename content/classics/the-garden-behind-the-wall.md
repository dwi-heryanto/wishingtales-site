The train left Mary standing on a platform so small it seemed an afterthought, a strip of gravel between the moor and the sky. A man in a brown coat waited with a pony cart. He did not smile. He did not ask her name. He lifted her trunk as if it were a sack of turnips and nodded at the seat.

"Hold on," he said. "The road is rough."

Mary held on. The road was rough. It climbed through gorse and heather, past grey stones that leaned like tired old men, and the wind never stopped. It pulled at her hat and pushed at her back. The moor went on and on, brown and purple and green, until she could not tell where the ground ended and the sky began.

"Is it always like this?" she asked.

"Like what?"

"Windy."

The man thought about it. "In spring it's worse," he said. "That's when it's got something to say."

Mary did not ask what. She did not care. She had been sent here because there was nowhere else, and she had decided before the train even stopped that she would hate it. She hated the wind. She hated the heather. She hated the man's silence and the pony's slow plod. She had hated India too, in the end, after the fever took everyone and left her behind. Hating was the one thing she was good at.

The house appeared all at once, as if the moor had shrugged and let it fall. It was long and low and grey, with more windows than any house needed and a door wide enough for a carriage. The man helped her down and left her in the hall with her trunk. The hall smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone. A clock ticked somewhere, slow and disapproving.

A woman came down the stairs. She was old, with a face like a closed fist, and she wore a ring of keys at her waist.

"You'll be Mary," she said.

"Yes."

"I'm Mrs. Medlock. I keep the house." She looked Mary up and down. "You're thinner than your photograph."

Mary said nothing. She had learned that silence made adults uncomfortable, and she liked making adults uncomfortable.

"Your uncle won't see you," Mrs. Medlock said. "He doesn't see anyone. You'll have your meals in the nursery and you'll keep out of the way. The house is big. Don't go poking into rooms that aren't yours."

"I won't."

"See that you don't." Mrs. Medlock turned and started up the stairs. "Come along. I'll show you where you sleep."

Mary followed. The stairs creaked under her boots. At the top, a long corridor stretched away into shadow, doors on either side, all closed.

"What's through there?" Mary asked, pointing to a door at the far end.

Mrs. Medlock stopped. "Nothing that concerns you."

"I only asked."

"And I only answered." Mrs. Medlock opened a door to the left. "This is the nursery. Stay in it."

The room was warm enough, with a bed and a window that looked out on the moor. Mary stood at the glass and watched the wind comb the heather. She did not cry. She had not cried since India. But something in her chest pulled tight, like a knot she could not untie, and she pressed her forehead to the cold pane and stayed there until the light went grey.

***

Morning came with rain, then stopped, then came again. Mary ate bread and milk alone in the nursery. Mrs. Medlock did not come back. A maid brought a tray at noon and left without speaking. The house creaked and settled around her like a living thing, and by afternoon Mary could not stand it any longer.

She put on her coat and went outside.

The grounds were a tangle. Paths that had once been gravel were half-swallowed by moss. Hedges grew into each other. There was a kitchen garden with dead stalks and a fountain with no water, and beyond that a lawn that sloped down toward a wall.

The wall was high and covered in ivy, so old and thick that Mary could not see the stones beneath. It ran the length of the property and then turned a corner and kept going. She walked along it, dragging her hand through the wet leaves. The ivy smelled green and bitter. Somewhere behind the wall, a bird sang.

Mary stopped. The song came again, three notes and a trill, clear as a bell in the wet air. She peered into the ivy but saw nothing. She took a step closer. The song stopped. Then a small brown bird hopped out onto a branch that stuck out from the wall, cocked its head, and looked at her.

"Go away," Mary said.

The robin did not go away. It fluffed its feathers and sang again, as if it had decided she was worth singing to.

Mary frowned. "I said go away."

The robin hopped down to a lower branch, closer now. Its eye was bright and black and entirely unafraid. Mary had never had a creature look at her like that. Most people looked at her the way Mrs. Medlock did, as if she were a problem to be solved or avoided. The robin looked at her as if she were simply there, and that was enough.

She sat down on a stone at the foot of the wall. The robin watched her. The rain had stopped for good now, and the sky was pale and wide. Mary pulled her knees up to her chest and stayed very still.

After a while, the robin flew down to the ground. It pecked at the earth near the base of the wall, where the ivy had been cut back and the soil was dark and turned. It pecked again, then looked up at Mary, then pecked again.

"What?" Mary said.

The robin scratched at the dirt. Something in the turned earth caught the light, a small dull gleam, half-buried. Mary leaned forward. The robin hopped aside, as if it had shown her what it came to show, and waited.

Mary knelt in the wet grass and pushed her fingers into the turned earth. It was cold and loose, easy to dig, as if someone had worked it recently, though the wall behind it had been shut up for years. The robin stayed close, hopping from one root to another, watching her with its head tilted. Her nails scraped against something hard. She dug deeper and closed her hand around it. A key. Long and heavy and dark with rust, the kind of key that belonged to a lock nobody had used in a very long time. She rubbed the dirt off with her thumb. The metal was rough, pitted, and cold enough to sting. It looked useless. It looked like something a person would throw away. Mary put it in her pocket anyway. "You meant me to find it," she said to the robin. The bird did not answer, of course. It only flicked its wings and flew up to the top of the wall, where it sat and preened, entirely satisfied with itself. Mary stood and brushed the mud from her hands. The wind came off the moor and pushed at her coat. She had never owned anything that felt as important as the key in her pocket. It was a small weight, no heavier than a stone, and yet she could feel it with every step as she walked back toward the house.

***

That night she lay awake in the dark and turned the key over in her fingers under the blanket. The house made its usual sounds — beams shifting, wind pressing at the windows, something creaking far below in the kitchens. She had learned to ignore those sounds. She had learned to ignore most things. But the key would not let her sleep. In the morning she went out early, before Mrs. Medlock could find her something to do, and walked the whole length of the garden wall. It was long, longer than she had thought, running from the kitchen garden down toward the orchard and then on into a tangle of bare trees she had never bothered to explore. The ivy grew thickest there. It hung in great ropes over the stone, so heavy in places that the wall seemed to be wearing a coat. Mary walked slowly, dragging her fingers along the leaves, pressing where the ivy was thinnest. Nothing. No gap, no handle, no sign of a door at all. She stopped and looked back the way she had come. The robin sat on a branch above her, watching. "You showed me the key," she said. "Now show me the door." The bird hopped to a lower branch, then to the ground, then up onto the ivy itself. It clung there for a moment, then fluttered sideways along the wall and landed again, further on, where the ivy grew in a thick curtain almost to the ground. Mary followed. She pushed her hands into the leaves and felt stone behind them, cold and rough. She pushed harder. The ivy gave, just a little, and behind it her fingers found an edge. Not the smooth face of the wall. An edge. She pulled at the ivy, tearing it away in handfuls, and the edge became a frame, and the frame became a door. It was low and narrow, set deep into the wall, and the ivy had grown over it so completely that no one would ever have known it was there. Mary stood back and looked at it. Her hands were scratched and dirty, her breath came fast, and her heart was beating hard against her ribs. The door was old and dark, bound with iron, and there was a keyhole in the middle of it, rusted and choked with dirt. She took the key from her pocket and held it up. It was the right size. It was the right shape. She had found the door, and now there was only the lock between her and whatever lay on the other side.

The lock was a small dark mouth in the iron, and the key was a small dark tongue. Mary knelt in the wet grass and fitted them together. At first the key would not go in. Dirt and rust had packed the hole tight, and the tip of the key scraped against it and stopped. She pulled it out and looked at it. Then she looked at the keyhole. She put the key back and pushed, harder this time, and twisted it a little, left and right, and something inside the lock gave a dry little click, like a beetle turning over in its sleep. But the key still would not turn. "Come on," she said, and her voice sounded strange out here in the open, small against the wind. "Come on." She set both hands on the key and turned. The metal bit into her palms. Nothing moved. The wind came over the wall and lifted the ivy leaves and set them down again. Somewhere out on the moor a bird called twice and stopped. Mary let go and sat back on her heels. Her hands were red and shaking. The key was old. The lock was old. Everything here was old and had been left alone a long time, and old things did not want to be woken. She thought of going back to the house. She thought of the long corridors and the closed rooms and the maid who told her to keep out of the way. Then she thought of the robin, who had shown her the turned earth, and the key, and the door. The robin had not been afraid. She put her hand flat on the door and pushed. The door did not move, but the ivy above her rustled, and a drop of cold water fell on her wrist. "I am not going away," she said, to the door, to the lock, to the whole stubborn wall. She took the key again. This time she pushed it in and turned it the other way, and felt something give, a fraction, a hair's breadth. She held her breath and turned again, slow and steady, and the rust inside the lock ground and complained and then let go. The key turned all the way round. There was a sound like a long-held sigh. Mary stayed perfectly still. Her heart was beating so hard she could feel it in her ears. The key was warm now in her hand, warm from her own grip, and the lock was open. She knelt there a moment longer, because she was afraid, and because she was not afraid, and both were true at once. Then she stood up.

***

The door was heavier than it looked. Mary put her shoulder against it and pushed, and at first it did not move at all, and she thought the years had sealed it shut for good. She pushed again, and the bottom edge scraped against the ground, and a little earth and dead leaves crumbled away. The door moved. It moved an inch, then two, then a hand's width, and the hinges made a noise like a question, high and thin. Cold air came out through the gap, and it smelled of wet earth and old leaves and something else, something green and sharp that she could not name. She pushed harder, and the door swung open. Mary stood in the doorway and looked. The garden was not dead. That was the first thing. She had expected dead things, brown stems, bare ground, a place that had given up. But the grass was long and wet and green, and it had grown tall and gone to seed, and the seed heads nodded in the wind. There were bushes along the walls, and they had grown wild and tangled, and some of them had small tight buds on them, hard and closed, waiting. A tree stood in the middle of the garden, and its branches were thick and low, and under it the ground was dark and soft. There were paths, or the ghost of paths, half swallowed by grass. There was a bench, grey and mossy, with one leg sunk in the earth. The walls went all the way round, high and red-brown, and the ivy hung over them in curtains, and above the walls the sky was white and wide and the wind kept moving. Mary stepped through the doorway. The grass was wet and cold against her legs, and it came up past her knees in places. She stood in the middle of the garden and turned around slowly, and the door was behind her, open, a dark shape in the wall. Nobody had been here for ten years. She knew it from the silence, and from the way the grass had grown, and from the bench with its leg sunk in the ground. She was the first. The bud on the nearest bush was small and hard, and she touched it with one finger, and it was cold and alive. The wind came over the wall and moved through the long grass and through the branches of the tree, and the whole garden breathed. Mary let out a breath she had not known she was holding. She was not afraid anymore. She did not know what would happen next, or who would come, or whether the garden would grow or die or stay exactly as it was. She only knew that the door was open, and that she was inside, and that for the first time in a long time she wanted to stay.
