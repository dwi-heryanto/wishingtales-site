The path through the wood was soft with last year's leaves, and Nell had been walking it long enough to stop counting her steps. When the trees opened, she saw the table first: a long one, white cloth, crowded with cups and plates that had not been cleared. Three people sat at it under a broad oak, and none of them looked up.

There was a chair on the near side, empty, with a cushion. Nell stood at the edge of the clearing and waited to be noticed. The man in the tall hat was pouring tea from a pot that had nothing in it. The woman with the long ears was buttering a slice of bread that had already been buttered twice. The small one between them was asleep with his face in a saucer.

"Excuse me," Nell said. "Is this seat free?"

The tall-hat man turned his head slowly, as if she had asked him to solve something difficult. "Free?" he said. "No. Not free. Occupied."

"But there's no one in it."

"That's because you're standing in front of it," said the long-eared woman. "Move along, and you'll see it's taken."

Nell did not move. The chair was clearly empty, and the table had room for a dozen more. "I only want to sit down for a minute," she said. "I've been walking since morning."

"Then you should have sat down in the morning," the tall-hat man said. "Sitting is a morning activity. In the afternoon we stand, or we move, or we argue. We do not sit."

"You're sitting," Nell pointed out.

"That's different. We've been sitting so long it's become a kind of standing." He lifted his cup and drank from it without tipping it. "If you want tea, you may have tea. There's plenty of room at the table for tea."

Nell looked at the empty chair again. The small one in the saucer stirred and mumbled something about treacle. The long-eared woman pushed him gently with one finger, and he went back to sleep.

"There is room," Nell said. "There's a whole chair."

"There is no room," said the tall-hat man, "and if you keep saying there is, we shall have to move, and we have only just got comfortable." He set down his cup and folded his hands. "Now. Since you are here, you may answer a riddle."

***

"Why is a raven like a writing desk?" the tall-hat man asked.

Nell thought about it. She had seen a raven once, on a fence post, and she had seen a writing desk in her father's study. They did not seem alike at all. "I don't know," she said.

"Good," said the tall-hat man. "That's the correct answer."

"It isn't an answer," said the long-eared woman. "It's a surrender."

"It's the only answer that fits," he replied. "If she knew, she would have said. Since she doesn't, she has answered honestly. Honesty is a kind of answer."

"Honesty is not a riddle answer. A riddle answer has to be a thing. A raven is a thing. A writing desk is a thing. You can't solve a riddle with a shrug."

"I can," he said. "I just did."

Nell watched them argue. The long-eared woman's ears twitched when she was annoyed, and the tall-hat man's hat sat higher on his head when he was winning. Neither of them looked at Nell, though they were arguing about her.

"I could try again," Nell offered.

"No need," said the tall-hat man. "The riddle is finished. We have moved on. Now I shall show you my watch."

He drew it out of his waistcoat pocket on a chain. It was a large watch, silver, and when he flipped open the lid, Nell saw the face. There were no hands. There was only a small window with a number in it: a seven.

"It tells the day of the month," he said. "Nothing else. I have no use for hours."

"But how do you know when to have tea?" Nell asked.

"We have tea always," said the long-eared woman. "That solves the problem."

The tall-hat man nodded. "Time and I had a disagreement," he said. "Some years ago. I made a remark, and Time took offence, and now Time does not speak to me. It goes on for other people. For me it stopped, on a Tuesday, and it has been Tuesday ever since, except when it's the seventh."

"That doesn't make sense," Nell said.

"It makes perfect sense," he said. "You simply haven't been here long enough to see it."

"The riddle is done," said the tall-hat man, and he put his watch away. "Now the mouse will speak."

The small grey mouse sat between the teapot and a plate of crumbs. Its whiskers drooped, and it kept its eyes half closed, as if it had been asleep for a long while and preferred to stay that way. "I knew three sisters," it said, "and they lived at the bottom of a well."

"What did they live on?" Nell asked.

The mouse did not open its eyes. "Treacle," it said.

"They couldn't live on treacle alone," Nell said. "They'd be ill."

The long-eared woman set down her cup. "They were very well," she said. "You are being rude."

"I only meant—"

"They were very well," the tall-hat man said, "and they drew all manner of things from the well. Treacle, and other things. It was a treacle well. That is what wells are for."

Nell looked at the table. The table was under a tree, and the tree was in a wood, and there was no well anywhere she could see. "But a well is for water," she said.

"This one was for treacle," the mouse said, and it sat up a little. "The three sisters were learning to draw. They drew treacle, and they drew the things that come after treacle. They were very ill, of course. That was the point."

"I don't understand," Nell said.

"You are not meant to," said the tall-hat man. "You are meant to listen."

The mouse went on. "The youngest sister drew a little. The middle sister drew a great deal. The eldest sister drew everything, and then she stopped, because there was nothing left to draw."

"What happened to her?" Nell asked.

"She was very tired," the mouse said. "She sat at the bottom of the well and waited. The other two sat with her. They are sitting there still."

"That is a sad story," Nell said.

"It is not sad," said the long-eared woman. "It is a story about drawing. You keep asking questions, and you are not listening."

"I am listening," Nell said. "I am trying to understand."

"Then stop trying," the tall-hat man said. "Understanding is a different department. We do not keep it here."

The mouse yawned, a long, slow yawn that showed its small teeth. "I am tired," it said. "I shall finish the story tomorrow."

"You always say that," said the long-eared woman.

"It is always tomorrow," the mouse said, and it closed its eyes.

The tall-hat man reached across the table and picked up the teapot. He lifted the lid, and the mouse, without opening its eyes, slid off the plate and into the pot. The lid went back on. The pot rattled once, then was still.

"It will sleep now," the tall-hat man said. "It sleeps better in there."

Nell stared at the pot. "You put it in the teapot," she said.

"It likes the warmth," said the long-eared woman. "And it is out of the way."

"But it was telling a story."

"It had finished," the tall-hat man said. "You were not listening. Now, we must move."

***

"Move?" Nell said. "Move where?"

No one answered. The long-eared woman stood up, and the tall-hat man stood up, and they both picked up their chairs. The table itself gave a small shudder, like a horse before it walks, and then it shifted sideways across the grass. Cups and saucers slid with it, and the teapot, and the plate of crumbs. Nell's own cup tipped over and spilled, but no one minded.

"Sit down," said the tall-hat man. "You are in the way."

Nell sat down on the grass, because there was no chair left for her. The table came to rest a little way off, under a different part of the tree. The long-eared woman set her chair at the head. The tall-hat man set his at the side. They sat, and the mouse's teapot was placed between them, and the table was exactly as it had been, only moved.

"There," said the long-eared woman. "That is better."

"What was wrong with before?" Nell asked.

"The cups were dirty," the tall-hat man said. "We do not wash up. We move."

"You move the whole table?"

"One place round," he said. "Then the dirty cups are in front of someone else. It is a system."

Nell looked at the cups. They were dirty. She looked at the new place, and there was a clean cup waiting, and a clean plate, and a small knife. "But the cups are still dirty," she said. "They are just in a different place."

"Exactly," said the long-eared woman. "That is the beauty of it."

"You could wash them," Nell said.

"We could," the tall-hat man agreed. "But then we would have to stop. And we are not stopping. We are having tea."

The table shifted again. This time Nell was ready for it, and she watched how it moved. It went in a circle, always the same circle, always one place round. The tree did not move. The grass did not move. Only the table, and the chairs, and the people in them, and the cups going round and round like a slow wheel.

"You do this all day?" she asked.

"All day," said the tall-hat man.

"And all night?"

"There is no night here," he said. "There is only tea."

The long-eared woman poured from the teapot. The teapot was empty, but she poured anyway, and a thin brown stream came out, and she filled her cup. "You may have some," she said, "if you sit still."

Nell sat still. The table came round again, and this time it stopped beside her, and there was a cup in front of her, and it was clean. She picked it up. The tea was hot, and it tasted of nothing she could name, and she drank it.

"There," said the tall-hat man. "You are learning."

Nell did not feel like she was learning. She felt like she was going round, and the table was going round, and the tree was standing still, and somewhere far above the leaves the sky was a pale, unchanging grey. She set down her cup and watched the table move on without her, and then come back, and move on again, and she began to see the pattern. It was a circle. It was always a circle. And nobody ever got anywhere.

The cup in Nell's hands had gone cool, and the tea tasted of wet stone. She put it down on the cloth and stood up. Her legs had gone stiff from sitting, and the table had come round so many times she had lost count. "I think I'll go now," she said.

The long-eared woman looked at her over the rim of a cup. "Go where?"

"Home."

"There is no home," said the tall-hat man. "There is only the table. Sit down. You have not heard the riddle yet."

"I have heard a riddle," said Nell. "It had no answer."

"That is the best kind," said the tall-hat man. "It lasts." He leaned forward, and his hat tipped, and the brim caught the edge of the teapot and knocked it over. Nobody picked it up. "Why is a raven like a writing desk?"

Nell had not heard that one. She stood still, because leaving felt like stepping off a moving thing, and she did not know how to do it. "I don't know," she said.

"No one does," said the long-eared woman. "That is the point. Now sit."

But Nell did not sit. She took a step back from the table, and the grass was soft under her shoes, and the tree was very tall above her. "I don't want to sit," she said. "I want to go home."

The tall-hat man took out his watch. He held it to his ear and shook it, and the little hand did not move. "It is the fifth," he said. "It has been the fifth for a long while. You cannot go home on the fifth. Nobody travels on the fifth."

"That makes no sense," said Nell.

"It makes the only sense there is," said the long-eared woman. "Sit down, and we will move up, and you will have a fresh cup, and everything will be as it was."

The table gave a small lurch, the way it did when it was about to turn, and Nell felt it pull at her, the way a current pulls at your ankles when you stand in a river. She took another step back. "No," she said. "Thank you. No."

The tall-hat man put his watch away. He did not look at her. "Then you will miss the next riddle," he said. "It is a good one. It has no answer either."

"I don't care," said Nell.

"And you will miss the tea," said the long-eared woman. "The tea is the same tea. It never runs out."

"I don't care," said Nell again, and this time her voice came out louder than she meant it to, and the Dormouse lifted its head from the teacup and blinked at her.

"She is going," said the Dormouse. "They always go. I went once. I came back."

"You did not go," said the tall-hat man. "You were asleep."

"I was not asleep," said the Dormouse. "I was going. There is a difference."

Nell turned and walked away from the table. The grass gave way to a path, and the path went into the trees, and the trees were close together, and it was dim under them, and the air smelled of leaves and rain. Behind her, she heard the table move again, and the clink of cups, and the long-eared woman saying something she could not make out. Then the tall-hat man's voice came clear through the branches. "She will be back," he said. "They always come back. It is the fifth, and there is nowhere else to go."

Nell did not answer. She kept walking.

***

The wood was quiet, and the quiet was a different kind from the quiet at the table. At the table, the quiet had been full of waiting, of cups set down and picked up, of the table turning. Here the quiet was made of small sounds: a bird shifting on a branch, a leaf falling, her own feet on the path. She walked until the tea party was only a sound behind her, and then only a feeling, and then nothing at all.

She stopped once and looked back. Through the trees she saw a patch of grey sky and the dark line of the table. The three of them were still there. The table was moving, one place round, then another, and the cups went with it. Nobody was washing anything. Nobody was going anywhere. They would be there tomorrow, she thought, and the day after, and the day after that. The tea would never run out, and the riddle would never be answered.

For a moment she wanted to go back. Not because she liked them, but because the table was warm, and the wood was dark, and she did not know the way home. She stood on the path with her hands at her sides and felt the pull of it, the way the table pulled when it turned. Then she thought of the cup that tasted of wet stone. She thought of the riddle with no answer, and the watch that told only the day of the month. Going back would be like sitting down in a river and letting it carry her round and round.

So she did not go back.

The path went on, and the trees opened, and there was a field, and beyond the field a wall, and beyond the wall she could see the shape of a roof. It was not her roof. She did not know whose roof it was. But it was a roof, and a roof meant a door, and a door meant someone who might know the way. She walked toward it, and the grass was wet, and the sky was still grey, and behind her the tea party went on without her, moving round and round, and she did not look back.

She did not look back once.
