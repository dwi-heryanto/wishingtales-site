Rip Van Winkle was the kindest man in the village and the laziest, and he would have told you the two were related. He had a way of sitting down on the inn porch that made the whole afternoon feel invited. Children brought him chestnuts and broken toys and questions, and he answered every one of them with the patience of a man who had nothing else to do, which he did not.

"Tell about the day the cow got into the church," said little Dirk, climbing onto the rail beside him.

"That cow had a fine singing voice," said Rip. "She found the hymn before the minister did."

The children laughed and Rip laughed too, and across the green his wife's voice came out of their doorway like a bell that had been struck wrong.

"Rip! The fence is down and the roof leaks and you sit there like a sack of barley!"

He raised a hand to her, agreeable as ever, and did not get up. The children watched him with the loyalty of small people who understand that a man may be good and useless at once.

It had always been this way. His fields went weedy while he mended other men's gates. His own roof leaked while he patched the schoolhouse. Every dog in the village knew his whistle, and every child knew his pocket held something. Only his own house was a place where he was never quite enough, and his wife never let him forget it, and he never once shouted back, which somehow made it worse for her.

By late afternoon he had taken all he could carry of her scolding, and he stood up slowly, the way a man does when he has decided something.

"Where are you off to?" Dirk asked.

"Up the mountain," said Rip. "Just for the quiet of it. There's a ledge above the ravine where a man can hear himself think."

He whistled, and his dog came out from under the porch with an old bone in her mouth and a look of complete agreement. He took down his gun, which was mostly for the look of the thing, and set off up the slope with the dog at his heel and the village shrinking behind him.

The path climbed through pine and then through rock, and the air turned thin and cold. Behind him the roofs of the village lay small as scattered coins. He could no longer hear any voice at all, not even hers, and the silence was so pleasant that he sat down on a flat stone and let it settle over him.

***

The ledge above the ravine was a shelf of grey rock with a view of the whole valley and a narrow path threading up behind it into the trees. Rip had come here for years. He had never once met anyone.

So when he heard the creaking, he thought it was a branch. Then he heard boots, many boots, coming down the ravine path in a slow and heavy rhythm, and the dog went still against his leg.

They came out of the trees in single file, and the first thing Rip noticed was that they made no more noise than if they had been painted there. Men in old-fashioned coats, with long beards and long faces, carrying a keg between them on a pole. Their eyes were the colour of wet slate, and not one of them looked at him.

"Good evening," said Rip. "That looks a heavy load for such a climb."

No answer. The line kept coming. The keg creaked on its pole.

"Well," said Rip, because he could not bear a silence he had not made himself, "I'll not stand in your way. But if a man might help, he might."

One of them turned his head, just slightly, and pointed at the keg. Rip took the end of the pole. The weight was greater than it looked, and the men around him smelled of cold stone and old rain, and none of them spoke a word the whole way up.

They carried the keg to a hollow among the rocks where others of their kind sat in a ring, playing at ninepins with stones and making a sound like distant thunder whenever one fell. Rip set down the keg and stood there, awkward and cheerful, and one of the silent men handed him a flask.

"For the climb," said Rip. "I thank you."

He drank. It was the best thing he had ever tasted, warm as a fire and cold as a spring at once, and he drank again before he meant to. The ninepins fell somewhere far off with a rumble. The dog lay down and put her head on her paws. Rip sat down on the rock to rest his eyes for a moment, only a moment, and the grey men went on with their game and paid him no mind at all, and the valley below grew very quiet, and then it was gone.

Cold woke him first, then the stiffness, a deep ache in his back as if he had slept on the world's hardest bench. Rip sat up and rubbed his face, and his palm found hair. A great deal of hair. He pulled at it, and it pulled back at his chin, and he made a small startled noise that the ravine threw back at him.

"Easy," he said to nobody. "Easy now."

The hollow among the rocks was empty. No kegs, no ring of grey men, no stones scattered from their game. Grass had grown up through the cracks where they had sat, thick and old. Rip's gun leaned against the same rock he had leaned it on, and when he lifted it the barrel had gone to rust, brown and flaking, and the stock had split along its grain.

"Wolf," he called. "Wolf, girl. Here now."

He whistled. The ravine gave him back the whistle and nothing else. He looked for her tracks and found his own, or what might have been his own, half filled in and furred with moss.

"Twenty paces," he muttered, getting his feet under him. "I shut my eyes for twenty paces of a walk."

His knees complained the whole way down. The path he remembered had been narrow and stony; now it was wide, and there were stumps where the old pines had stood, and a fence line running where no fence had been. A woman was gathering sticks at the bottom of the slope, and Rip raised a hand to her, glad of any face at all.

"Good day to you," he said. "Is this not the road down to the village?"

The woman straightened and looked him over, beard and rusted gun and all, and her hand went to the sticks in her apron.

"It is," she said slowly. "Though I would not walk it looking like that, if I were you. Are you from the far side of the mountain?"

"From the village," said Rip. "From the village at the foot of it."

She looked at him a moment longer, then nodded as though that explained nothing and turned back to her gathering. Rip went on, and the road took him down into a place with the shape of his home, and none of its face.

***

Houses he knew had been pulled down and others built where they stood, and the others were taller and plainer, with straight chimneys. A young man passed him carrying a bundle of printed papers and did not look up. Two women at a gate stopped talking when Rip came near and started again when he had gone by, quieter.

The square was full. There was a crowd there, and a platform, and a man on the platform making a speech with his arms going, and the crowd answering him in a way Rip did not follow at all. He stood at the edge of it with his rusted gun over his shoulder and his beard down his chest and felt the whole square slide its eyes past him and then come back.

"Friend," he said to a man beside him, a stout fellow with a red face. "Friend, what is all this? Is it an election?"

The man turned. "An election? It is a celebration, sir. We are a free people now, and we do not forget it."

"Free," said Rip. "Free of what?"

The red-faced man stared at him. "Of the king, sir. Of the king across the water. Where have you been kept?"

Rip did not answer. He had found the inn, and the inn had a new sign, and the sign was the thing that stopped his breath. The old painted face was gone from it. In its place was a man in a plain coat with a long nose and a look of patient severity, and under him letters in fresh gilt that Rip could not read, though he could read.

"That is not the king," he said aloud.

"That is General Washington," said the red-faced man, with some heat. "And you will speak of him with respect in this square."

"I did not mean," Rip began. "I only meant that the other one, the painted one, he was there when I..." He stopped. The words would not come out in any order that made sense. "Where is Nicholas? Nicholas kept the inn. He had a wart by his eye and he watered the beer."

"Nicholas Vedder has been dead these sixteen years," said the man. "His house is my house now. I keep the beer and I do not water it. Who are you, that you do not know it?"

Rip looked around the square for one face he knew. There were old men in it, and he studied them one by one, and they were strangers with familiar chins, or familiar noses over strange mouths. A dog barked somewhere, and his heart turned over, but it was a small brown dog, not his, and it ran to a boy who called it by a name Rip had never heard.

"I am Rip Van Winkle," he said, and his voice sounded thin to him. "I am Rip Van Winkle, and I would like someone to tell me what has happened to my village."

The crowd had gone quiet around him. The man on the platform had stopped his speech. Somewhere a baby began to cry, and a woman hushed it, and in the hush Rip heard his own name said back to him by a voice he did not know, and he turned to find who had said it.

The voice came from the edge of the crowd, where a young woman stood with a baby on her hip. She had a plain dress and a tired, kind face, and she was looking at Rip as though she had seen him in a dream and was not sure yet whether to trust it.

"Sir," she said, "you gave a name just now. Would you say it again?"

"Rip Van Winkle," he said. "That is my name. It has been my name for forty years and more, and I would be glad to know why it makes everyone stare."

The woman's mouth opened, and then closed. The baby on her hip reached out a hand toward Rip's beard and made a small sound of wonder. She shifted the child and stepped closer, and her eyes went over his face the way a person reads a letter they have been waiting for.

"My father was called Rip Van Winkle," she said slowly. "He went up the mountain with his dog and his gun when I was a girl no higher than that fence. He never came down. My mother said he was a good man who could not keep a promise to be home for supper, and she said it without much anger by the end."

Rip felt the ground shift under him, though it was flat and solid. He looked at her face, and then at the shape of her chin, and then at the small scar above her eyebrow where she had fallen from the woodpile when she was three.

"Judith," he said. "You had a fever the week before I went up. You would not drink the tea."

The woman made a sound that was not quite a laugh and not quite a sob. "I still will not drink tea," she said. "My mother used to say it was the one thing I got from you."

People were pressing in around them now, and someone at the back asked loudly who the old man was, and someone else said to hush. Judith did not look away from Rip's face. She reached out and touched the sleeve of his coat, as if to check that he was there, and the baby on her hip grabbed a fistful of the fabric and held on.

"I do not understand it," she said. "You are older than my mother was when she died. You are older than the house. But you are my father. I would know you anywhere."

***

The crowd had thickened, and the man on the platform had come down from it, and everyone was talking at once. Rip stood in the middle of it with his hands hanging at his sides, and the noise went over him like weather. He heard the word ghost twice, and the word liar once, and a woman near the front said he was a beggar who had learned a story to get a meal.

"He is no beggar," Judith said, and her voice carried farther than Rip would have expected from someone so tired. "He is my father, and he has come down off the mountain after twenty years, and I would like to know what the village means to do about it."

The man from the platform, who had a broad red face and a ribbon on his coat, cleared his throat. "Madam," he said, "the village has a great deal on its mind today. We are choosing our leaders. We are a free people now, and we have no time for old stories."

"Then you will make time," Judith said. "Because this story is standing in front of you and it is my father, and I am not going to let him be turned out into the road."

She turned to Rip and took his arm, and the baby on her hip looked at him over her shoulder with wide, calm eyes. "Come and sit down," she said. "You look as though you have not sat in twenty years."

Rip let himself be led to the bench by the inn door. His legs were shaking, and the bench was the same bench, worn smooth in the same places, though the wood had been painted since he last saw it. He sat, and the crowd made a ring around him, and Judith stood beside him with the baby on her hip.

"There was a sign here," Rip said. "It had a picture on it. A king, with a red coat. I used to sit under it and let the children climb on my knees."

"The sign is still here," Judith said. "It has been repainted. Look up."

Rip looked up. The sign hung over the door on its old iron bracket, and it showed a man in a general's coat with a serious face and a sword. Underneath the picture, in fresh gold paint, were the words GENERAL WASHINGTON. The red coat was gone. The king was gone. The village had painted over its own history while he slept.

"It is a different name," Rip said quietly.

"It is a different country," Judith said. "And you are still in it."

She shifted the baby to her other hip and looked at the crowd, and then she looked at Rip, and something in her face changed. "You want to know what happened to your village," she said. "I will tell you what happened. It went on without you. It had a war, and it had a peace, and it had babies and funerals and bad harvests and good ones. It kept going, the way a river keeps going when a stone falls in."

Rip stared at the sign. The gold letters were bright in the afternoon light, and the general's painted eyes looked out over the square at nothing in particular.

"Then I have no place in it," he said.

"You have a place," Judith said. "You are sitting in it."

She turned and pointed up at the sign, and then she read the words aloud, slowly, so that everyone in the square could hear her. "General Washington," she said. "That is the new name over the door. And here is the old name." She looked down at Rip. "Rip Van Winkle," she said. "My father. He went up the mountain and he came down again, and he is sitting on this bench, and he is going to tell us what he saw."

The square went quiet. Rip looked at his daughter, and at the baby who was watching him with her thumb in her mouth, and at the faces of the village, which were strange and familiar all at once.

"I went up the mountain," he said slowly, "to get away from my wife's tongue, and I met some men in the ravine who gave me a drink, and I slept. When I woke up, my dog was gone and my gun had rusted and my beard was a foot long, and the world had gone on without me."

"And what did you see up there?" someone called from the back of the crowd.

"I saw men playing at ninepins," Rip said, "and I heard the thunder of it, and I think I have been asleep ever since."

There was a pause, and then a man near the front laughed, and then another, and then the whole square was laughing, not cruelly, but with the relief of people who have been frightened and are glad to be able to stop. Judith sat down on the bench beside her father, and the baby climbed into his lap, and the afternoon went on the way afternoons do.

"You will have supper with us," Judith said. "And then you will tell the children the story, and they will tell their children, and that will be that."

Rip looked up at the sign one more time, at the general and the gold letters, and then at his daughter's face, which was tired and kind and set like a stone against the world.

"That will be that," he said, and he settled back on the bench, and the village went on around him.
