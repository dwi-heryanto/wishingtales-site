Ichabod Crane came to the valley in a borrowed cart, with a bundle of books and a stomach that growled like a distant drum. He was a schoolmaster, lean as a rake and twice as hungry. The valley folk called him clever, which meant he could read Latin and add up a farmer's debts without using his fingers.

"You'll board with the Van Tassels this winter," said the man who drove the cart. "Old Baltus has a daughter, and she's worth more than the whole harvest put together."

Ichabod sat up straight. "A daughter?"

"Katrina. Pretty as a peach and twice as sweet. The farm runs all the way to the creek."

The schoolmaster's eyes went wide. He saw orchards heavy with apples, fields of yellow corn, and a warm kitchen with a pie on the sill. His mouth watered. "And does she like schoolmasters?"

"She likes dancing," the driver said, and laughed.

At the Van Tassel farm, Ichabod stepped down and dusted his coat. The house was wide and white, with a barn bigger than the church. A girl in a red skirt was feeding chickens, and she looked up and smiled. Ichabod forgot every Latin word he knew.

"You must be the new teacher," she said. "I'm Katrina. Come in before the soup gets cold."

He followed her inside, and his heart thumped like a book dropped on a floor.

***

That autumn, the Van Tassels gave a harvest feast, and half the valley came. Lanterns hung from the barn beams, and fiddles sawed away in the corner. Ichabod wore his best coat and danced until his knees creaked. Katrina laughed at his long steps and taught him a turn she liked.

"You're lighter on your feet than you look," she said.

"I have a great deal to be light about," Ichabod said, and she laughed again.

Across the room, Brom Bones sat on a cider barrel like a horse waiting to bolt. He was broad and loud, and he had wanted to marry Katrina since he was old enough to hold a pitchfork. Now he watched Ichabod spin her under the lanterns, and his grin turned to stone.

"Teacher," Brom called out, "you dance like a scarecrow in a windstorm."

"And you sit like a sack of turnips," Ichabod said, before he could stop himself.

The room went quiet, then burst out laughing. Brom's ears went red, but he laughed loudest of all.

Later, when the cider was low, the talk turned to ghosts. Old Baltus told of a Hessian soldier who lost his head to a cannonball in the war. "He rides the Hollow road at night," the old man said, "looking for it."

Ichabod pulled his chair closer. "With his head off?"

"Clean off. Some say he carries it in his lap. Some say he carries it under his arm."

Brom Bones leaned forward, and his eyes shone. "I've seen him myself. Rode him down to the bridge and back. He couldn't catch me, head or no head."

Ichabod's spoon shook in his hand. He looked at the dark window, and the dark window looked back.

***

Ichabod said his goodbyes with his hat in his hands and his heart in his boots. The night air smelled of woodsmoke and wet leaves. His old horse, Gunpowder, walked slow and steady, and Ichabod let him have his head. "Easy now," he murmured. "Nothing out here but trees."

The trouble was, the trees did not agree. Every stump wore a hood. Every branch reached out a crooked arm. Ichabod had read a great many books about witches and omens, and now the books rode along with him, whispering.

A white shape drifted across the lane. His breath stopped. Then it turned its head and made a sound like a rusty gate. It was a cow. Ichabod laughed at himself, a short, shaky laugh. "A cow," he said. "Only a cow."

Ahead, a great old tree spread its branches over the road. Fireflies blinked in the hollow of its trunk, and for one long moment Ichabod would have sworn the hollow glowed like a mouth. Gunpowder shied, and Ichabod grabbed the mane.

"Steady," he whispered. "We are nearly home. Nearly there."

He began to hum, because humming is braver than silence. The road dipped toward the bridge, and the water below it ran black and quick. Somewhere behind him, hooves sounded. He told himself it was an echo. He told himself twice.

***

At the bridge, the mist stood waist high and cold. Gunpowder stopped and would not go on. Ichabod kicked, and the horse only shivered.

"Go on," he begged. "Please."

Then the mist moved. A rider came up out of it, tall and dark on a black horse. The rider wore a cloak and carried a sword, and where his head should have been there was nothing at all. He carried his head in front of him on the saddle, and it watched Ichabod with a fixed and awful grin.

Ichabod's mouth went dry. "Good evening," he croaked, because he had been a schoolmaster too long to forget his manners.

The horseman did not answer. He only turned his horse and came on.

Ichabod whipped the reins. Gunpowder bolted, and the bridge planks drummed under them like a heart. Behind, the hooves came faster. The trees flew past. The wind pulled tears from his eyes.

He looked back once. The rider was closer, head held high, cloak snapping. Ichabod shouted at the horse, at the night, at his own foolish books. The road bent toward the church, and he prayed the ground would hold.

Then the rider drew back his arm, and the thing in his hand was round and heavy and dark. Ichabod ducked low over the horse's neck. The air whistled.

Something struck him, and the night went quiet.

***

Morning came slow and grey over the valley. The sun found the bridge and the water under it and the road that ran past the church. And it found, on the road, a shattered pumpkin. The pieces lay in a wide fan, orange and wet, as if something had burst there in the night.

Of the schoolmaster, there was no sign. His horse stood cropping grass by the fence, saddle empty, reins trailing. A farmer found him first and called the others.

"Ichabod?" they shouted into the woods. "Master Crane?"

Only the crows answered. They searched the bank and the water and the haystacks. Nothing. A hat was found, but no head to go under it. The pumpkin told its own story to anyone who looked.

By noon the whole valley had walked out to see. "It was the Hessian," said old Marta, crossing herself. "He got his head at last."

"The pumpkin proves it," said another. "A head hurled hard enough would break like that."

Nobody laughed at the idea. The bridge felt colder after that, and people took the long road instead. The schoolmaster's books were packed in a box and left by the door. A new teacher came from over the hill before the frost. She was sensible and slept well, and she never once walked home alone at night.

***

Brom Bones heard the news at the tavern and did not look surprised. "A pumpkin, you say?" He turned his cup slowly. "Fancy that."

Someone asked him what he thought had happened. "Maybe the schoolmaster found a better school," he said. "Somewhere with more books and fewer ghosts."

The men laughed. Brom laughed loudest. But when the talk turned to the shattered pumpkin by the bridge, Brom laughed again, and it was a strange laugh, a little too long. He never explained it.

Katrina married him in the spring, and the farm prospered, and there were children in the house before long. Yet on autumn evenings, when the harvest was in and the fire was low, someone would bring up the schoolmaster.

"Poor Ichabod," they would say. "Gone like a spark in the wind."

And Brom would grin into his cup and say, "Pumpkins are dangerous things."

The children would shiver and ask for the story again. So the tale was told and told, and it grew a little with each telling. The horseman grew taller. The chase grew longer. The pumpkin always broke at the end.

And somewhere, perhaps, a lean man with a good appetite was teaching letters in another valley, and keeping to the lit roads after dark. The bridge still stands. The water still runs under it. And if you pass that way at night, you may hear hooves behind you, and you may ride a little faster, and you will not be the first.
