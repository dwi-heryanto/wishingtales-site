The crow found the cheese on a windowsill, cooling in the last of the afternoon. It was a good piece, pale and firm, with a rind the colour of weak tea. She took it in her beak and lifted herself into the air, wings pulling hard against the weight of it, and settled in the high branches of an oak at the edge of a field.

From up here she could see everything: the field, the fence posts, the small brown movements of mice in the stubble. She tucked the cheese against the branch with one foot and looked at it. It was hers. She had taken it fairly, or fairly enough, and now she would eat it slowly and watch the evening come.

A fox came out of the hedge below. He was thin, and his coat had a rent in it where a bramble had caught him, and he walked with the careful step of an animal who has not eaten well for several days. He stopped at the foot of the oak and looked up.

"Good evening," he said.

The crow said nothing. Her beak was full.

"That is a fine piece of cheese," the fox said. "I have not seen better. The farmer's wife makes it with the milk of two goats and a little salt from the marsh. You can smell the marsh in it, can you not?"

The crow could, a little. She shifted her grip on the branch.

"I do not beg for it," the fox said. "I would not. A crow who has flown as far as you have flown has earned her supper. I only came to say that I have heard about you."

The crow tilted her head.

"They say in the field that the crow in the oak has a voice like nothing else in the county," the fox said. "They say that when she sings, the dogs stop barking. They say the farmer's wife leaves her churning and stands at the door with her mouth open."

The crow looked down at him. She had never heard any such thing, and she had lived here for three years.

"I thought it was flattery," the fox said. "I came to see for myself. And now I see you, and I think it might be true. You have the throat for it. You have the chest for it. Will you sing? Only a note. Only one note, so that I can say I heard it."

The crow swallowed. The cheese sat heavy in her beak, and the evening was still, and the fox waited below with his thin face turned up and his ears pricked forward as if he were already listening.

She opened her beak.

***

The cheese fell.

It was not a long fall. The oak was not a tall oak, and the fox was standing directly beneath the branch, and the cheese struck the grass at his feet with a soft, wet sound. He had it before the crow had closed her beak. He did not even have to lunge. He simply bent his head, took it up, and stepped back a pace.

The crow screamed. It was not a song. It was a raw, tearing sound that went out across the field and came back off the far hedge, and the mice in the stubble stopped moving, and a dog somewhere began to bark.

The fox chewed. He chewed slowly, with his eyes half closed, and when he had swallowed he licked the corner of his mouth and looked up at her again.

"There," he said. "That was the note I came for."

The crow came down out of the oak in a rush of wings and struck at him, but he was already moving, a low red shape slipping under the hedge, and her claws closed on nothing but the space where his head had been. She stood in the grass with her feathers up and her heart going hard, and the smell of the cheese was still in the air, faint and salt and gone.

She walked the ground beneath the tree for a while, though she knew it was useless. The grass was short and the light was going. She found a crumb, one crumb, no bigger than a seed, and ate it, and it tasted of nothing but itself.

Then she went back up into the oak and sat on the branch where she had sat before. The field darkened. The fence posts went grey, then black. The moon came up thin over the far hedge, and the mice began to move again in the stubble, and the dog stopped barking, and the night settled in around the tree the way it settled in around everything.

The crow sat with her beak closed. Her crop was empty, and her throat ached from the scream, and she could feel the shape of the hunger now, the particular shape of it, the shape of a piece of cheese that had been hers and was not. She thought about the fox's voice, and how it had sounded like the truth, and how she had known it was not the truth, and how she had opened her beak anyway.

She did not sing. There was nothing in her to sing with. She sat on the branch in the dark, and the hunger sat with her, and after a long while she tucked her head under her wing and waited for morning.

The tortoise lived at the edge of a field where the grass grew thin and the stones were warm. She had lived there a long time and she knew every inch of it. She knew where the blackberries ripened first and where the ground went soft after rain. She walked the field every morning, slow and steady, and she was content.

The hare came through the field one afternoon, running for no reason, the way hares do. He stopped when he saw her. He stood up on his hind legs and laughed.

"I have never," he said, "seen anything move as slowly as you. I think the stones move faster. I think the shadows move faster."

The tortoise did not stop walking. She put one foot in front of the other and kept her eyes on the far hedge.

"You are very fast," she said. "I have seen you run. I have seen you run straight past things you might have wanted to see."

"What is there to see?" said the hare. "I have seen it all already. I have been to the end of the field and back before you have crossed a single furrow."

The tortoise reached the edge of the field and turned. She looked at the hare properly then, for the first time. He was quick and bright and he was already bored.

"Race me," she said.

The hare laughed again, but this time it was a different laugh, a laugh that had a question in it. "Race you where?"

"Across the field," said the tortoise. "From this stone to the old oak at the far side. Tomorrow morning, when the sun is up."

"You cannot be serious."

"I am always serious," said the tortoise. "That is one of the things about being slow. You have time to be serious."

The hare looked at the field, and then at the tortoise, and then at the field again. He could already see himself at the oak, sitting in the shade, waiting. He could see the tortoise a long way back, a small dark stone on the grass.

"All right," he said. "Tomorrow. But do not say I did not warn you."

***

The morning came clear and cool. The field was wet with dew and the light was long and low. A few of the other animals had come to watch: a beetle on a blade of grass, a pair of sparrows in the hedge, a field mouse who had heard about it from a cousin. They did not make much noise. They had come because they wanted to see the hare run, and they had come because they wanted to see the tortoise, and they did not know which of those two things would be the one worth watching.

The tortoise stood by the starting stone. She had not slept well. She had spent the night thinking about the field and how long it was and how many steps it would take. She had counted the steps in her head and then she had stopped counting because the number was too big. She did not feel brave. She felt like a tortoise, which is a thing that is slow and steady and has a hard shell and no way to go faster than it goes.

The hare arrived late, as hares do. He stretched and yawned and looked at the tortoise with an expression that was almost kind.

"You are sure?" he said. "There is no shame in changing your mind. I would not think less of you."

"I am sure," said the tortoise. "Are you?"

The hare laughed and shook his ears. "I was born sure," he said. "Ready? Go."

And he was gone. He went off across the field like a thrown stone, like a thought, like something that had never had to think about moving. The grass bent behind him and then straightened again. In a few heartbeats he was a small shape near the middle of the field, and then he was smaller, and then he was a dot, and then he was at the far hedge, and then he was over it.

The tortoise watched him go. She put one foot forward. Then she put the other foot forward. The dew was cold on her feet and the sun was warm on her shell and she settled into the long, even rhythm that she had known all her life.

"He will be at the oak before I reach the first furrow," she said to the beetle, who was watching from a blade of grass. "But I am not racing him. I am walking to the oak. That is a different thing."

The beetle did not answer. The tortoise did not need it to. She walked.

***

The hare reached the old oak in a rush of speed that made the leaves shiver. He looked back across the field and saw nothing on the grass but the small dark shape of the tortoise, far away, moving so slowly that she seemed to be standing still. He laughed out loud. There was no one to hear him but the oak and a sleepy crow in the upper branches, and the crow did not care.

"I could run this race three times before she gets here," the hare said. "I could run it four times. I could take a nap and still win."

The sun was warm. The shade under the oak was deep and cool. The hare had run hard and the run had made him sleepy, and the thought of the nap settled into him like water into sand. He lay down in the shade, with his head on his paws, and he watched the tortoise for a while. She was still a small dark shape. She was still moving. He closed one eye. Then he closed the other.

"Just a short one," he said. "Just until she gets closer."

He slept.

The tortoise walked. The sun climbed and the dew dried and the beetle went about its business and the sparrows flew off and came back. The field mouse fell asleep in the hedge. The tortoise put one foot in front of the other. She passed the first furrow. She passed the second. She passed the place where the blackberries grew and the place where the ground went soft. Her shell grew warm in the sun and her legs grew tired and she did not stop.

When she reached the middle of the field she saw the hare under the oak, a pale shape in the shade, and she knew he was asleep. She kept walking. She did not hurry. She had never hurried in her life and she was not going to start now.

The hare woke to the sound of something very close. He lifted his head and blinked and the world came back to him in pieces: the oak, the field, the sun high and hot. And there, not two lengths away, was the tortoise, crossing the grass toward the trunk of the oak with the same slow, even step she had used all day.

The hare was on his feet in an instant. He ran. He ran faster than he had ever run, faster than he had run that morning, faster than he had known he could run. But the tortoise was already at the tree. She put one foot on the root and then the other, and she stopped, and she turned her head.

"You are here," she said.

The hare stood in the grass with his breath going in and out. He looked at the oak and the tortoise and the long field behind them, and he did not have anything to say.

"I did not beat you," said the tortoise. "I only got here first."

The lion had been asleep in the shade of a rock for most of the afternoon. He was old and his mane was thin in places, and when he slept he dreamed of nothing at all. A mouse lived in a hole under the rock, and on this day the mouse was in a hurry. He had found a store of seeds behind the farmer’s shed and he wanted to bring them back before dark. So he ran. He ran across the lion’s paw and over the lion’s rib and along the lion’s flank, and he was almost past when the lion woke.

One paw came down. It was not heavy enough to hurt, but it was heavy enough to hold. The mouse lay flat and felt the warmth of the pads and the rough edges of the claws, and he did not move.

"You," said the lion. His voice was low and sleepy and not unkind. "You woke me."

"I did," said the mouse. "I am sorry. I was running. I did not see you."

"You did not see me," said the lion. He lifted his paw a little and looked at the small brown shape beneath it. "I am the largest thing in this field."

"You are," said the mouse. "But I was thinking about seeds."

The lion was quiet for a moment. Then he laughed, a short breath out of his nose. "Seeds," he said. "And what will you do with seeds?"

"Eat them," said the mouse. "In the winter. If I have enough."

The lion looked at the mouse for a long time. The sun was going down behind the rock and the field was turning grey. "If I let you go," said the lion, "what will you give me?"

The mouse did not have to think. "I will remember you," he said. "And if you are ever caught, I will come."

The lion laughed again, but softer. "You are very small," he said. "What could you do?"

"I do not know," said the mouse. "But I would come."

The lion lifted his paw. The mouse stood up and shook himself and looked back once, and then he was gone into the dark under the rock. The lion put his head down again and closed his eyes. He did not think about the mouse again that night. But the mouse thought about the lion. He thought about the weight of the paw and the warmth of it and the way the lion had laughed, and he kept the thought with him like a seed of his own.

***

The hunters came in the autumn, when the grass was dry and the nights were cold. The lion had been drinking at the stream and he did not hear them until the net was already over him. It was made of thick rope, woven tight, and when he tried to stand the ropes cut into his shoulders. He roared and the sound went out across the field and into the hills, and then he lay down because standing only made it worse.

The mouse heard the roar from his hole under the rock. He had grown fat on seeds and he had a family now, small and quick, and he was not looking for trouble. But he remembered the paw. He remembered the laugh. He came out into the cold and ran toward the sound.

The lion was in the net near the stream, and two hunters were sitting by a fire not far off, waiting for morning. The mouse crept close. The ropes were thick and the knots were tight, and his teeth were small. He began to gnaw.

It took a long time. His jaw ached and his mouth filled with the taste of hemp, and twice he had to stop and rest. The lion watched him with one eye. "You came," he said.

"I said I would," said the mouse, and kept chewing.

"They will wake soon," said the lion.

"Then I will chew faster," said the mouse.

The rope parted. One strand, then another. The mouse worked at the knot over the lion’s shoulder and the lion pulled and the net loosened, and then the lion stood up and the whole thing fell away. He did not roar. He looked at the hunters by the fire and then at the mouse, and he put his nose down close to the small brown body.

"You are still very small," said the lion.

"I am," said the mouse. "But I was here."

The lion turned and walked into the dark, and the mouse went back to his hole under the rock. The hunters woke to an empty net and a cold fire, and they did not understand what had happened. But the mouse understood. He lay in the warm dark with his family around him and he thought about the weight of a paw and the sound of a laugh and the long night’s work, and he slept.
