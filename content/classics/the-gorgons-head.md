The island was small enough that Perseus knew every goat by name. He knew each rock by the sound it made when the sea slapped it. He had grown up there with his mother, Danaë, in a white house with a low wall. The two of them kept to themselves. Their neighbours were fishermen who nodded and said little. The king, on the hill above the harbour, said a great deal. None of it was kind to a boy who had no father anyone would name.

"You are growing tall," Danaë said one evening, trimming the wick so the flame stood straight. "That is the trouble."

"Tall is trouble?"

"Tall is noticed." She looked at the door as if it might knock by itself. "Keep your head down and your hands busy. Never let him see you're afraid of him."

Perseus was afraid of him. Everyone was. The king had a voice like a door dragged over stone. He had a way of smiling at a person while deciding what to do with them. When the summons came, it came with two soldiers and no explanation.

The hall smelled of smoke and wet wool. The king sat at the long table with his court around him. He did not look up from his cup until Perseus was standing right in front of him.

"So this is the boy," the king said. "I am to be married. Did you hear that? Married. And a wedding needs a gift."

"Congratulations," Perseus said, because his mother had taught him manners.

The king laughed. The court laughed with him, a beat later, like waves following a boat. "Good. You have a tongue. Then you can use it to ask for what I want. There is a creature called the Gorgon. One look from her and a man becomes a stone in his boots. I want her head in a box, brought here, for my bride. Go and fetch it."

The room went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with respect. Perseus felt his ears go hot. He wanted to say that no one could do this. He wanted to say that the king knew no one could do this, and that this was not a wedding gift but a way of getting rid of him politely. What he said was, "Where do I find her?"

The king smiled, pleased, as though the boy had finally said something useful. "That is the interesting part. Nobody knows. Ask the road. It goes everywhere."

***

Perseus left before dawn. He carried bread and a knife and the small hope that the road might be kinder than the hall. For two days it was only dust and hills and the sound of his own feet. Fear sat on his shoulder the whole way. It whispered that he should turn back. It told him his mother would be alone. It said a stone in his boots was the best he could hope for.

On the third morning a young man was sitting on a milestone, eating an apple. He looked as though he had been waiting there since the world began. He wore a traveller's cloak and a smile that had already decided to be a nuisance.

"You walk like a man going to his own funeral," the stranger said. "Is it a good funeral? Should I bring flowers?"

"I'm going to find the Gorgon," Perseus said, because there was no point in lying to a stranger on a milestone.

"Ah. The Gorgon." The stranger took another bite. "And how do you plan to look at her without looking at her?"

"I hadn't got that far."

"No. You hadn't got as far as the door, by the look of you." He stood and brushed apple juice from his hand. "I'm Quicksilver. I'm going your way, and I talk too much, and you'll thank me later. First, you'll meet some old women who can tell you where to go. Be polite. Be clever. Don't be brave yet, it's too early in the day."

They walked together, and Quicksilver teased him the whole way, gently, until the fear loosened its grip a little. At a spring under a stand of olive trees, three women were waiting. Perhaps they were not women at all, only the idea of them.

"These are the nymphs," Quicksilver said. "They've been keeping things for you. They like a boy who turns up."

The tallest of them held out a pair of sandals with wings at the heels. "Wear these and the air will hold you." The second gave him a wallet, soft leather, closed with a drawstring. "Put what you take in here. It will keep." The third set a cap on his head, light as nothing. "And this will keep you."

Perseus turned the cap in his hands. "What does it do?" he asked.

Quicksilver laughed and clapped him on the shoulder. "Put it on and find out. That's the only way anyone learns anything worth learning."

Perseus put it on. The world went quiet and strange. Quicksilver, grinning at the place where he stood, said, "There. Now even I can't see you. Off you go, and mind the old women with the eye."

***

The Grey Sisters lived on a bare hill where even the goats had given up. Perseus found them by the sound of arguing. Three old women sat on a stone, and none of them was doing anything, because between them they had one eye and one tooth. Just now each was waiting for the other to hand them over.

"It's my turn," said the first.

"It is not," said the second. "You had it at breakfast and you looked at nothing but your own hands."

"Give me the eye, sister."

"In a moment."

Perseus stood very still, invisible in his cap, and watched the eye go from hand to hand like a coin in a game. It was a grey eye, wet and tired. It blinked at the sky while the sister who held it squinted at the clouds and said the weather would turn. The tooth went round more slowly. The sister who had it chewed her lip and complained that the others never let her finish her bread.

When the eye came his way, Perseus put out his hand and took it.

For a moment the hill was quiet. Then all three began to feel about on the stone, patting, muttering. The first one said, "Where is it, then?" and the second said, "You had it." The third said nothing at all. She only turned her blind face toward the exact place where Perseus was standing.

"Who's there?" she said.

Perseus took off the cap. It seemed only fair, and Quicksilver had told him the cap was for what came later.

"A traveller," he said. "I've got your eye. I'll give it back when you've told me the way to the Gorgons."

The three sisters went quiet in a way that was worse than the arguing. "The Gorgons," said the one with the tooth. "He wants the Gorgons."

"Then he can want," said another. "Give us the eye first."

"Tell me first."

So they told him, all three at once and all three differently, until he made them take turns. Go west, said the first, past the salt flats, until the sea goes black. Look for the island shaped like a sleeping dog, said the second, and the cave above the tide line, and the stone figures that stand outside it. "You'll know them," she said, and her voice had gone flat and careful. "They were people once. They were curious people."

"And the tooth?" said the third. "Do I get my tooth?"

Perseus gave back the eye. After a moment he gave back the tooth as well, because the old woman had begun to cry, and because it cost him nothing. The sisters passed the eye around one more time and looked at him properly. The eldest said, "You'll do. You're not clever, but you'll do."

He put the cap on and left them to their stone and their arguing.

***

The island shaped like a sleeping dog had a cave above the tide line. Outside the cave stood the figures. Perseus counted them on the way in and did not count them on the way out. There were men with their hands up, and a woman with her mouth open, and a boy no older than himself who had turned his head at the wrong moment. They were grey and smooth and very patient, and the gulls sat on their shoulders as though they were rocks.

Perseus crouched behind the last of them and looked at his shield. The bronze was old and polished by someone who had cared for it. In it he could see the cave mouth, and the dark inside the cave mouth, and something moving in the dark.

"Quicksilver," he whispered, though Quicksilver was not there. "You said I'd learn by doing it."

The thing came out into the light. He did not look at it. He looked at the shield, at the small bright picture in the bronze, and in that picture she was terrible and calm and her hair moved by itself. He walked in sideways, one step at a time, watching only the reflection.

His hand went out. The wallet was open.

The bronze shook. Something cold and heavy went into the leather, and the drawstring pulled itself tight. Perseus stood in the cave mouth with his eyes shut and his heart going like a bird in a box.

When he opened them, the cave was empty and the sea was the same sea. He did not look in the wallet. He walked past the stone people with his head down. The winged sandals took him up off the rock. The wind came under him and lifted him, and below, the island grew small. The grey figures stood where they had always stood, waiting for someone who would not come.

Far off, he heard a voice he knew, cheerful and far too pleased with itself. "There you are," it said. "I told you. Mind the old women with the eye."

The wind was steady and warm, and the sea went by underneath like a rug being pulled away. Perseus flew until his arms ached from holding the wallet out from his body, as if it might bite. The sandals did the work. He only had to think up and the air lifted him, and think down and the water came closer. It was, he admitted to himself, the best part of the whole errand, and he was almost sorry when the sun began to sink and a coastline rose up out of the haze, rocky and pale, with a town at the edge of it.

Something was wrong with the town. From above, the streets had a queer grey look, and the people in them did not move. Perseus came down low over the harbour wall and saw a woman standing by a fish stall with her hand out, as if she had been about to pay. She was stone. So was the fish. So was the man behind the stall, and the dog at his feet, and the gull on the post. All of it grey and still and patient.

"Don't land," said a voice at his elbow. Quicksilver was walking beside him on the air, hands behind his back, as comfortable as a man on a garden path. "Or do. You've a cap that hides you and sandals that outfly anything with wings. What could possibly go wrong?"

"What happened here?"

"A sea beast," said Quicksilver, "with a taste for pretty things. The local king chained his daughter to the rocks to keep it happy. Very tidy. Very kingly." He nodded at the headland. "She's still there, if you're curious. The beast comes at dusk."

Perseus looked at the wallet. Then at the grey town. Then at the headland, where the rocks ran out into the water like a hand.

"I'm curious," he said.

***

The princess was called Andromeda, and she was not weeping. She stood against the rock with the chain tight at her wrists and her chin up. When Perseus appeared out of the air she looked at him the way you look at a gull that has landed on your windowsill — surprised, and not impressed.

"If you've come to gawp," she said, "there's a whole town of gawpers behind you, and they're better company."

"I came to help." He pulled the cap from his pocket. It was soft and grey and it made his hand look like nothing at all. "Put this on. It hides you. When the beast comes, I'll be behind it."

"And what will you be holding?"

"Something it won't like."

The water went still first. Then it bulged, and the bulge became a back, and the back became a neck, and the neck came up out of the sea with weed hanging off it and a mouth like a cave. Perseus put on the cap. The world went dim and close, as if he were inside a cloud, and he flew up and around, and the beast's head turned to follow a sound it could not find. Andromeda had vanished from the rock. The chain hung empty, swinging.

The beast roared at the empty chain, and Perseus came down behind it with the wallet open.

He did not look. He held the shield up and watched the reflection, and the reflection of the beast went grey and then still. The great neck froze in mid-turn, and the mouth stayed open. The sea came up around it and broke, and broke again, and did not move it. Perseus shut the wallet and pulled the drawstring and let out his breath.

Andromeda took off the cap. "You might have warned me," she said, "that you were carrying that." But she was almost smiling.

***

They flew home together, the princess holding onto the back of his belt and complaining about the cold. The island came up out of the evening sea exactly as he had left it — the white house, the smoke from the chimney, his mother's garden gone dark. Perseus set Andromeda down on the beach and stood for a moment with his hand on the wallet, because the king would be at the house. The king was always at the house now.

He was. He had brought his court, and they filled the courtyard with torches and laughter, and the king sat in the chair that had been Perseus's grandfather's chair. His mother stood at the edge of it with her hands folded. When she saw Perseus her face did a thing he had never seen it do — it went glad and frightened at the same moment.

"The boy returns," said the king. "And empty-handed, I see. Well. A wedding gift after all — a head that failed to bring a head."

The court laughed. Perseus walked up the path with the wallet in his hand and the cap in his pocket, and he did not hurry.

"I brought it," he said. "You asked for the Gorgon's head. I have it. I'd advise you to look away."

"Look away?" The king leaned forward. "I want to see what you've brought me."

Perseus opened the wallet.

The torches went out one by one, or appeared to, and the courtyard filled with a grey light like the bottom of a well. The king did not scream. He simply stopped, with his mouth open and his hand raised, and the stone of him was the colour of the chair he sat in. The courtiers stopped too, and the guards, and the dogs, and the little page boy who had been laughing loudest. The courtyard was very quiet.

His mother had covered her eyes. Andromeda had not. She stood by the gate and looked at the grey figures and said nothing at all.

"Wrap it up," said Quicksilver, from somewhere near the olive tree. "You've made your point. There's a goddess waiting, and she doesn't like to be kept."

Perseus closed the wallet. The grey light went out of the courtyard, and the ordinary evening came back, and the sea sounded again, and the stone people stood where they had stood.

He gave the head to Athena at the top of the hill. She took it without a word and set it on her shield, where the face of it looked out at the world and turned nothing to stone at all. Perseus did not ask how.

He walked back down the hill in the dark. His mother was waiting at the door of the white house with a lamp in her hand. Andromeda was sitting on the step with her chain-marks still red on her wrists. Quicksilver was already gone, the way he always went, without saying goodbye.

"Well," said his mother, and held the lamp higher. "You've grown."

"I flew," said Perseus. "I'll tell you about it."

And he did, and the lamp burned steady, and the sea went on the way it always had.
