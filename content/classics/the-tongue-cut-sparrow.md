The old man woke before the light did. He always had. He would sit on the edge of the porch with his rice bowl warm in both hands and watch the sky turn from ink to grey to a soft blue like a bird's egg.

His sparrow was always there first. It came hopping along the railing, tilting its head, and the old man would hold out a pinch of rice from his own bowl.

"Eat up, little friend," he said. "There's plenty for both of us."

The sparrow took the rice and chattered back at him, a bright scolding sound that meant nothing and everything. Then it would sit on his knee while he ate his breakfast, and the two of them would share the quiet.

This went on for many mornings. The old man's wife did not care for it. She had a sharp tongue and a short temper, and she did not like the bird on her clean porch. She said so often, and loudly.

"It leaves feathers everywhere," she said one morning, sweeping the step with quick, angry strokes. "It eats the food that should be in your stomach. Send it off to the hills where it belongs."

The old man only smiled and said, "It's a small bird. It takes only a small bit. Let it stay."

But his wife's patience was a thin thing, and it was wearing through.

One morning the old man went out to the well to draw water. He was gone only a little while. When he came back, the porch door was shut hard, and the step was swept clean, and there was no sparrow on the railing.

"Where is my bird?" he asked.

His wife did not answer. She stood at the stove with her back to him, and her shoulders were stiff.

"Where is it?" he asked again, softer.

"Gone," she said. "I told it to go. It went."

The old man set down his bucket and went out to the hills without another word.

***

He walked all morning. The path climbed through bamboo and then through pine, and he called the sparrow's name into the trees, though a sparrow has no name, or only the one he had given it.

By noon he was tired and his voice was thin. He sat on a stone and put his head in his hands.

Then he heard a small sound. Not a chirp. A rustle, low in the brush.

He parted the leaves, and there it was. The little bird sat hunched in the grass, one wing held crooked against its side. Its beak opened, but the noise it made was not its usual bright chatter. It was thin and tangled, as if the tongue had been tied in a knot, and the bird could not find the right shape for any sound.

"There you are," the old man whispered. "There you are."

He cupped the sparrow in both hands and carried it home. On the porch he made a nest of a folded cloth and set the bird in it. He brought warm water in a saucer and held it close.

"Rest now," he said. "The wing will mend, and the tongue will sort itself out. You are safe here."

The sparrow slept, and the old man sat beside it all the afternoon, and his wife said nothing at all from the other room. That night he lay awake and thought: a hurt bird cannot stay on a porch where it is not wanted. So in the morning he would take it to the hills himself, and find the place where sparrows go.

At first light the old man wrapped the sparrow in the folded cloth and set off up the hill behind his house. The path was narrow and stitched with tree roots. He walked slowly, because of the bird, and because of his knees.

"Where do you keep your village?" he asked the bundle against his chest. The sparrow only blinked at him. So he followed the sound of small wings overhead, and the wings led him.

The path climbed through cedar and stone, then dropped into a hollow he had somehow never seen before. There, tucked among the ferns, stood the smallest houses he had ever laid eyes on. Each one had a roof of woven grass and a door the height of a teapot. Sparrows hopped along the lanes, and every one of them stopped to look at him.

"A guest," said a small voice. "A guest has come up the hill."

The sparrows gathered around his boots, polite and curious. They bade him sit on a flat stone, and they brought him water in an acorn cup. A brown elder with grey at his throat bowed low. "You are the one who shared his bowl," he said. "We know your name in our songs. Come in, come in."

The old man knelt, so as not to loom. "I only came to bring back a hurt bird," he said. "Is it well enough to stay?"

"It is well enough to be home," said the elder.

***

Inside the largest of the little houses, the sparrows had set a feast. It was laid out on a table no bigger than a book, and every dish was a wonder: seeds glossed with honey, a thimble of warm tea, crumbs of sweet cake stacked into a tower. The old man ate his portion one careful pinch at a time and declared it the finest meal of his life. The sparrows fluttered with delight.

Then a door opened at the back, and out hopped his own sparrow. The wing was bound in a leaf bandage, and it still held the wing a little high. But it came straight across the table, and it made a sound at last, small and cracked but bright.

"There," said the old man. "There you are."

The elder nodded. "The tongue will loosen. Birds heal best among their own."

When the tea was finished, two baskets were carried out and set before him. One was tiny, light as a husk, and fit in the crook of his arm. The other was broad and heavy, and the handles creaked when the sparrows lifted it.

"Take whichever you like," said the elder. "Both are gifts. Choose with your own heart."

The old man looked at the great basket, then at the small one. "The little one is all my arms can carry down the hill," he said, and he smiled. "And it is the one I can thank you for without needing help."

"A good answer," said the elder. "Go slowly. The path is kind to those who go slowly."

The path down the hill was kind. It levelled itself under the old man's sandals and kept the small basket steady in the crook of his arm. A few sparrows flew along beside him, then peeled away into the hedges. By the time the rooftops of his own village showed through the trees, he was humming.

At his gate he set the basket down and wiped his feet, out of habit. His wife stood at the door with her arms folded.

"A whole day gone," she said. "And you come back with a lunch box."

"It was the heavier one," said the old man, and left it at that.

Inside, on the low table, he lifted the lid. His wife leaned over his shoulder. There was rice, and a folded cloth, and beneath the cloth a little packet of coins. Not a fortune. Enough. Enough for new sandals, and the good tea, and a warm coat before winter.

"That's all?" said his wife.

"That's plenty," he said. "Tell me it isn't."

She said nothing. But she took the coins and counted them twice, and when she put them in the tin she did not slam the lid.

That evening she asked, without looking up from the stove, "Was there more?"

"There was a great basket," said the old man. "I couldn't have carried it."

The rice bubbled. She said no more that night.

***

Morning came, and with it the sound of the gate swinging hard. The old man came out to find his wife already halfway up the hill path, a scarf tied tight under her chin.

"You don't know the way," he called.

"I'll find it," she called back. "Sparrows are not hard to follow."

He was right. It was not hard. The small birds came out to meet her at the edge of the trees, and though they did not bow, they did not stop her either. The village of small houses lay open in the hollow. The elder stood at the door of the largest one, which was no larger than a cupboard.

"You are the guest's wife," said the elder.

"I am," she said. "You gave my husband a basket. Now give me mine."

The elder looked at her a moment. Then the sparrows brought out the great basket, broad and heavy, the handles creaking.

"It is yours if you can carry it," said the elder. "But I would leave it here."

"I can carry it," she said, and she did, all the way down, stopping three times to breathe.

At home she set it on the kitchen floor and unlatched the lid. Something leapt out. Then something else. The room filled with a clatter and a wind that smelled of wet feathers. She shrieked and ran outside, and the door banged behind her.

When the old man opened it, the house was empty of everything but a single grey feather. He picked it up and set it on the shelf.

His wife sat on the step a long while. Then she said, quietly, "Put the kettle on."

"I will," he said, and did.

They drank their tea in the small warm kitchen, under the one bright feather, and the tin of coins stayed exactly as it was.
