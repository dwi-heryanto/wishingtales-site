The river ran past the old couple's house the way it had run for sixty years. Every morning the old woman went down to it with a bucket. She came back with her back aching and her arms full of nothing but water. Her husband mended nets on the porch and watched the road for travelers. Between the two of them they had a house with two chairs and no one small enough to sit in either.

They did not say it aloud. They had been married too long for that. But when the neighbors' grandchildren ran shouting through the lane, the old woman would stop sweeping and look at the dust they left behind. The old man would find some reason to go and check the roof.

One morning the river brought something down that was not a log and not a fish. It was a peach, and it was the size of a washtub, rocking in the current like a boat that had lost its way. The old woman set down her bucket. She walked into the water up to her knees, which she had not done in years. Then she put both arms around it and hauled.

"Husband," she called, because she could not manage more than that.

He came down the bank fast for a man his age. Together they rolled the peach up the slope and through the door. It took up most of the floor between the chairs, and it smelled like the whole of summer.

"We'll share it with the village," the old man said, wiping his forehead. "It's too much for two."

The old woman fetched the knife. She had barely touched the skin when the peach split itself, clean down the middle, like a seam coming undone.

Inside sat a boy.

He was small and round and damp. He looked up at them with an expression of mild surprise, as though he had expected a different house. The old woman dropped the knife. The old man sat down heavily in his chair, which creaked.

"Well," said the boy, in a voice like a bell that had been rung under water. "Is there anything to eat?"

So they named him Momotaro, peach boy, and they fed him. They found that the two chairs in the house were now three, and that the lane outside had someone in it small enough to shout.

***

The boy grew the way river weeds grow, which is to say without asking permission. By the time he could carry the water bucket he could carry two. By the time he could reach the high shelf he could lift his father out of his chair and set him down again, laughing. The old man pretended to resent it and did not.

But Momotaro was gentle with it. He mended the nets beside his father and learned which knots held and which slipped. He walked to the market with his mother and carried the rice home on his shoulder. When the neighbor's grandchild fell and skinned a knee, Momotaro knelt in the dust. He told her a story about a fish until she stopped crying.

"You'll want a trade," his father said one evening, watching him split kindling with one clean stroke. "A strong boy could go to the capital."

"Maybe," said Momotaro. He stacked the wood. "Maybe I'll stay and split yours."

Then the ogres came.

They came down out of the northern hills in the dark. They were loud the way bullies are loud, which is to say they made more noise than harm and did not know it. They took the winter rice and a cart and two goats and the bell from the shrine. They left the gate hanging off its hinge because one of them had thought it funny. Nobody was killed. That was the strange thing about the ogres. They were enormous and they had horns and they laughed like pots breaking. But what they wanted was to take things and be seen taking them.

Still, the village went quiet. The children were kept indoors. The bell was gone, and the mornings felt wrong without it.

Momotaro stood at the gate one night and looked north. His mother came out with a lamp and stood beside him and did not ask.

"I want to go," he said at last. "To their island. I want to make them stop."

"You're one boy."

"I know." He turned to her. "But I'm a boy who came out of a peach, and they're ogres who steal bells. I think one of us is owed something."

She laughed, and then she cried a little. Then she went inside and came back with a cloth bag tied at the neck.

"Millet dumplings," she said. "The best I make. If you're going to be a fool, be a fed fool."

His father gave him a straw hat and a look that lasted longer than words.

At dawn Momotaro tied the bag to his belt and walked north. The river ran beside him for a while before it turned away.

***

The road north was a cart track first, then a path, then a suggestion. Momotaro walked it with the dumpling bag knocking against his hip. By noon the river was gone and the hills had begun to fold themselves around him. He had never been this far. He kept his eyes on the ruts and his hand near the bag, and he did not sing, because the village had not sung since the bell went missing.

Something was following him. He had known it for a mile. There was a soft, patient padding, keeping just off the path. When he stopped, it stopped. When he walked, it walked. Momotaro did not turn around. Instead he sat down on a stone at the edge of the track, untied the bag, and set one dumpling on his knee.

"If you're going to rob me," he said to the empty road, "you may as well have the small one first and be honest about it."

A dog came out of the hedge. It was the colour of wet straw, all ribs and long ears. It looked at the dumpling the way a man looks at a warm fire after a week of rain. It sat. It did not beg, exactly. It simply sat with such deep attention that the attention itself felt like a question.

"Where are you bound?" the dog asked.

"North. To the ogres' island."

"To fight them?"

"To make them stop."

The dog's ears came up. "They took the bell from my village too. I have hated them quietly for a year, which is a poor way to hate anybody." It looked at the dumpling again. "I can smell millet. I have not eaten since the second day of my journey, and I have forgotten what I am for."

Momotaro broke the dumpling in half without thinking about it. The dog ate its half in one motion and then sat very still with its eyes shut, as if something had been put back in its proper place.

"There are more," Momotaro said. "One for each day, if you stay."

"Staying is the only sensible thing I have thought of in a year," said the dog, and got up, and walked beside him.

***

By the third day the hills had become cliffs, and the path ran along a ledge with the sea a long way below, grey and turning over. The dog walked in front now, nose down. It stopped at a bend where the rock had been scraped clean.

"Something up there," it said. "Something with clever hands."

A monkey dropped onto the path in front of them. It was small and grey, and its hands were working even while it stood still, turning over a pebble, then a twig, then nothing. It looked at Momotaro, at the bag, at the dog, and did the arithmetic quickly.

"Ogres," the monkey said, before anyone had asked. "Going north to the island. Everyone who goes north on this path is going to the ogres. There's nothing else north."

"You've been watching the road," Momotaro said.

"I watch everything. It's the only thing I'm good at, and I'm very good at it." The monkey turned the pebble over. "I know where their gate is. I know the gate has a bar across it that lifts, not slides. I know they're lazy at night and loud at everything. I know more about that island than the ogres do, and I've never been on it."

"And what do you want?"

The monkey looked at the bag and then, briefly, at its own thin wrists. "The same as the dog. One of those, and a reason to go somewhere. I've been on this cliff so long I've started naming the stones."

Momotaro untied the bag and gave him a dumpling. The monkey ate it sitting on the ledge with its feet hanging over the sea. When it was finished it wiped its hands on its ribs and stood up briskly.

"Right," it said. "Follow me, and don't look down, and when we get there let me do the talking. Nobody expects a monkey to talk, so they never listen properly, and that's half a battle already."

The dog snorted. "And the other half?"

"The other half," said the monkey, "is him." It jerked a thumb at Momotaro and set off along the ledge at a trot.

***

That evening, on the last of the hills before the water, they were cooking nothing at all around a small fire. A shape came down out of the dark and landed on a rock above them with a clatter of feathers.

It was a pheasant, long-tailed and glossy, and it looked at the three of them with the flat disdain of a creature that has never once been impressed.

"You are loud," the pheasant said. "I heard you from the next valley. If you're going to the island, you'll want someone who can go over the top of things instead of around them."

"Can you?" Momotaro asked.

"I can fly onto their gate and make a noise they cannot ignore. I have a great deal of practice at being impossible to ignore." The pheasant ruffled its feathers. "I offered the same to a fisherman once and he laughed at me. He is still afraid of the dark, and I am still here."

The monkey, who recognised a fellow schemer, handed up the last dumpling without being asked. The pheasant took it gravely, as if accepting a contract.

They set off at dawn with the sea widening before them, and Momotaro counted his companions. There was a dog that smelled trouble, a monkey that knew the gate, a bird that could not be ignored. What was left in the bag was small. What walked beside him was not.

"When we get there," said the dog, "I go first."

"You always go first," said the monkey.

"And I go highest," said the pheasant.

Momotaro said nothing. He looked at the island rising out of the water. He tightened the knot on the bag, and he kept walking.

***

The island rose out of the sea like a clenched fist. Momotaro stood at the water's edge with his three companions and looked up at the gate. It was a heavy thing of black wood and iron studs, set in a wall of rough stone. Beyond it, they could hear the ogres: laughter, the clatter of bowls, a drum being beaten badly.

"They are having a party," said the monkey. "I hate parties I am not invited to."

"You are invited," said the pheasant. "I am about to send the invitation."

The dog growled low in its throat. "I still go first."

"You always go first," said the monkey, and this time it was not a complaint.

The pheasant launched itself into the air, a flash of copper and green, and landed on the top of the gate. It began to peck. Not gently, but with the steady, maddening rhythm of a bird that has found a worm and intends to get it. The sound rang out across the courtyard.

From inside came a roar. "What is that?"

"It is a bird," said another voice, smaller. "On the gate."

"Well, get it off!"

The pheasant pecked harder. It pecked at the hinges, at the studs, at the wood itself, and it made a noise like a hammer on a cracked bell.

The monkey was already up the wall, fingers and toes finding holds in the stone. "The latch," it muttered. "There is always a latch." It hung by one arm and worked at the iron bar with its other hand. "A child could open this. A stupid child. An ogre child."

The bar slid back with a clang. The gate swung open.

The dog went first. It went in low and fast, and the first ogre it met, a fat one with a bib, went over backwards into a pot of soup. The monkey dropped onto the second ogre's head and pulled its ears until it howled. The pheasant flew in circles above them, pecking at any hand that reached for a club.

Momotaro walked through the gate and stood in the middle of the courtyard. The ogres stopped. They looked at him. He was not large, and he was not armed, and he was holding a bag.

"Who are you?" demanded the chief, a grey ogre with tusks like sickles.

"I am Momotaro," he said. "You have been raiding my village. You took the rice and the salt and the winter blankets. You frightened the children. You made my mother cry."

The chief laughed. "And now you have come to ask for it back?"

"No," said Momotaro. "I have come to tell you that your gate is open, your soup is on the ground, and your dog is being bitten by mine. I have also come to offer you a dumpling."

He held out the bag. The chief stared at it.

"A dumpling," said the chief.

"The best in the district," said Momotaro. "My mother made them. If you eat one, you will sit down. You will stop raiding. You will give me the treasure you have stolen, and you will promise never to come back. If you do not eat one, my friends will continue, and they are very good at continuing."

The pheasant landed on the chief's shoulder and pecked his ear. The chief yelped.

"All right," he said. "All right. Give me the dumpling."

The treasure filled a cart: bolts of silk, jars of coins, a chest of gold, and a small iron kettle that the chief insisted was lucky. The ogres stood in a line by the gate and bowed as Momotaro and his companions left. The chief had a lump on his ear and a dumpling in his hand, and he looked, on the whole, relieved.

"You will not come back," Momotaro said.

"We will not come back," said the chief. "The dumpling was very good. Tell your mother she makes a fine dumpling."

"I will tell her," said Momotaro.

The journey home took three days. The dog rode in the cart and slept on the silk. The monkey counted the coins twice and found them satisfactory. The pheasant sat on the highest jar and told anyone who would listen that it had been the one to start it all.

When they came over the last hill, the village was waiting. The old man and the old woman stood at the front, and the old woman had her hands pressed to her mouth. Momotaro jumped down from the cart and walked to her.

"You came back," she said.

"I came back," he said. "And I brought the blankets and the salt and the rice. And a cart."

The old man looked at the cart and then at his son and then at the dog and the monkey and the pheasant. "You brought friends," he said.

"They brought me," said Momotaro.

The dog wagged its tail. The monkey bowed. The pheasant said, "I was the one on the gate," and the village children laughed and cheered.

That night there was a feast, and the old woman made more dumplings. The old man told the story to anyone who would listen, and then told it again. He told it wrong every time. He said the dog had flown and the pheasant had bitten the chief, and Momotaro let him, because it was his story now, too.

The treasure was shared out among the village. The kettle was kept on the hearth, and it never did anything lucky, but the old woman said it made good tea. The ogres never came back. For years afterwards, when anyone spoke of the proudest parents, they spoke of the old couple by the river. Those two had found a peach and raised a son and sent him out into the world. They had waited, and they had been right to wait.
