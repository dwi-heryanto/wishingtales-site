The miller had three sons. When he died, the eldest took the mill, the middle took the donkey, and the youngest got the cat. It was not much of a cat. It was a gray thing with a torn ear. It had a habit of sitting on the windowsill and staring at the youngest son as if it were doing sums in its head.

"Well," said the youngest son, whose name was Emile. "I suppose I can eat you, and that will be the end of it."

The cat did not run. It turned its head slowly and spoke in a voice like a door creaking open. "Give me a pair of boots, and I will make your fortune."

Emile sat down on the floor. He was not a stupid boy. He was the kind who thought first and spoke later, and just then he could not think of anything to say. He had heard of cats that talked in stories. He had never expected to own one. His brothers had gone off with their mill and their donkey, and neither of them had looked back.

"Boots," he said finally. "You want boots."

"Leather ones," said the cat. "High on the leg. I cannot do business barefoot."

Emile had a little money saved from selling eggs. He took it to the cobbler, who looked at the cat, and looked at the money, and made the boots without asking any questions. That was the kind of town it was. When the cat put them on, it stood taller somehow. It walked out the door like a man going to market.

The house was empty after that. Emile sat by the cold hearth and waited. He did not know what else to do. The cat came back at dusk with a rabbit in its teeth and a look of quiet satisfaction.

"Tomorrow," it said, dropping the rabbit at Emile's feet, "we begin."

***

The king of that country liked two things: fresh game and being told he was clever. The cat learned this in a single afternoon of listening at the kitchen door of the palace. So the next morning it went out to the fields, caught two partridges, and carried them to the castle gate in its mouth, boots and all.

"A gift," said the cat, when the guards let it through, "from my master the Marquis of Carabas."

The king was pleased. He had never heard of the Marquis of Carabas. The name sounded expensive, and the partridges were plump. "Tell your master I am grateful," he said.

"I will," said the cat, and bowed so low its ear touched the floor.

For a week it went on like this. A hare on Monday, a brace of pheasants on Wednesday, a basket of trout on Friday. The king began to look for the cat at the gate.

Meanwhile Emile knew nothing of any of it. He was still sitting by the hearth, eating rabbit, wondering if he had dreamed the whole thing. Then one evening the cat came in and said, "Tomorrow you will swim in the river. When the king's carriage passes, you will not answer to any name but the Marquis of Carabas."

"I don't know how to be a marquis," said Emile.

"You don't have to know," said the cat. "You only have to be wet and surprised. Leave the rest to me."

The next day Emile went down to the river. The water was cold and the morning was gray. He stood in it up to his waist, shivering, feeling like a fool. Then he heard wheels on the road. The king's carriage came around the bend, and the cat ran out in front of it, waving its boots and shouting, "Help! My master is drowning!"

The carriage stopped. The king leaned out. "Who is drowning?"

"The Marquis of Carabas!" cried the cat. "Thieves took his clothes while he bathed!"

The king, who had been waiting all week to thank the Marquis for the partridges, ordered his men to fetch a suit of clothes from the carriage. Emile was pulled out of the river, wrapped in a blanket, and dressed in a coat that fit him better than anything he had ever worn. He stood there dripping, trying to look like a man who owned land.

"You are welcome to ride with us," said the king.

Emile glanced at the cat, who was sitting on the driver's seat, licking one paw as if nothing at all were happening. "Thank you," he said. "I would like that."

And the carriage rolled on, with Emile inside it and the cat up front, and the whole road ahead of them.

***

The cat did not stay long on the driver's seat. Some miles on, when the road ran through fields of wheat and the carriage slowed at a hill, he slipped down and was gone into the green before anyone could ask where. Emile watched him go from the window. The king said something kind about young men and their restless animals, and the carriage rolled on.

The cat ran along the hedgerow until he came to a wide field where a dozen men were cutting wheat with sickles. They worked slowly, the way men work when the sun is high and the day is long. The cat climbed onto a stone at the edge of the field and put his boots together. He spoke in a voice that carried.

"Listen to me," he said. "The king is coming down this road. When he asks whose field this is, you will tell him it belongs to the Marquis of Carabas. If you say anything else, you will be sorry."

The oldest harvester straightened his back and squinted at the cat. "And who are you," he asked, "to tell us what to say?"

"I am the one who knows," said the cat. "The Marquis is a great man. His men are not far behind me. Say the words and you will eat well tonight. Say anything else and you will not." He did not explain the last part. He did not need to.

The harvester looked at the others. One of them shrugged. The cat's boots were very fine, and fine boots had a way of meaning something, even on a cat.

"Marquis of Carabas," the old man said. "All right. We will say it."

When the king's carriage reached the field, he leaned out and asked whose land it was. Twelve men in a row answered, "The Marquis of Carabas, your majesty." The king nodded and looked at Emile with new interest. Emile looked at his hands and said nothing at all. He had never owned a field. He was not sure he wanted one now, but he kept his face still, the way the cat had told him.

A little further on, the cat was already standing in a meadow where shepherds watched a flock. He said the same words to them. They laughed at first, until the cat mentioned the king, and then they stopped laughing. When the carriage came, they said the sheep belonged to the Marquis of Carabas. The king said, "Your lands are rich, Marquis." Emile said, "Thank you, your majesty," because what else could he say?

The cat kept running. At every field, at every orchard, at every row of vines, he stopped just long enough to make the same bargain. By the time the sun began to lean toward the hills, the whole valley had learned to say the name of the Marquis of Carabas. The king had grown quiet with respect. He had not known there was such a man in his kingdom.

Emile sat in the carriage and watched the country go by. He thought about the cat, and how strange it was that a creature with four feet and a pair of boots should be the only one of them who knew what he was doing. The princess, beside him, asked if he liked music. He said he did, and she smiled. He did not tell her that he had never heard any music except the mill wheel and the cat's singing, which was not really singing at all.

***

Far ahead, where the fields ended and the woods began, the cat came to a castle. It was a good castle, with towers and a wall. It sat on a hill as if it had been dropped there by someone very sure of himself. The cat sat down in the road and looked at it for a long time. Then he walked up to the gate and knocked with the toe of one boot.

The gate opened. A servant looked down at him and said nothing.

"I have come to see the lord of this castle," said the cat. "Tell him it is a cat in boots, and that I have news of the road."

The servant went away and came back. Then the cat was led through a hall and up a stair into a room where a great ogre sat at a table, eating. He was very large. His hands were the size of plates, and his teeth were the size of the cat's whole head. He looked at the cat and laughed.

"A cat," he said. "In boots. What do you want?"

"To see what you can do," said the cat. "They say you are the greatest of all the ogres. They say you can turn into anything. I did not believe them."

The ogre put down his bone. "You did not believe them," he said.

"No," said the cat. "I think it is a story. I think you are just a big man who eats too much."

The ogre stood up. The room got smaller. "I can be a lion," he said. "I can be an elephant. I can be anything I choose."

"A lion," said the cat. "That is easy. Anyone can be a lion. But a mouse? I do not think you can be a mouse. A mouse is small, and you are not."

The ogre's face went red. "I can be a mouse," he said. "I can be a mouse right now."

"Show me," said the cat. He sat down on the floor and folded his paws and waited, with his boots pointing at the ogre like two small promises.

The ogre closed his eyes. There was a sound like a breath going out, and then there was no ogre at all. There was a mouse on the floor, a small gray mouse with a tail. It looked up at the cat and twitched its nose.

The cat did not wait. He had been waiting all his life, and he did not need to wait any longer. He pounced, and the mouse was gone. The cat sat in the middle of the room licking his whiskers as if he had done nothing more than catch a fly. The castle was quiet. The servants had run when the ogre changed, and they did not come back.

The cat walked through the rooms, opening doors. He found a kitchen with a fire still burning, and a table set for a great man, and a bed with curtains, and a room full of gold. He found a bell by the gate and he rang it. The servants came back one by one, and he told them that the castle now belonged to the Marquis of Carabas, and that if they served well they would keep their places. They looked at him and at each other, and then they nodded, because the mouse was gone and the cat was there, and cats have a way of being obeyed.

He went out to the gate and sat on the wall and looked down the road. Far off, he could see the dust of the king's carriage. He smiled a cat's smile, which is not a smile at all, but something better.

***

The carriage came up the long drive with its wheels loud on the gravel. The cat went down to meet it. He stood on his hind legs by the steps and bowed, and his boots made him look taller than he was. The king put his head out of the window.

"Marquis!" he called. "Is this yours?"

"All of it," said the cat, before the son could speak. "The woods, the fields, the castle. My master bids you welcome."

The son climbed down from the carriage and looked at the towers and the gate and the bell. His mouth stayed open until the cat walked over and pressed a paw against his ankle. "Bow," the cat said, very low. "You are a marquis. Marquises bow."

The son bowed. He was not stupid. He had simply never been told to be a marquis before, and now he was one, and there was nothing to do but hold out his arm to the princess. She took it and laughed, because she had watched him swim in the river and liked him better for it.

Inside, the servants had lit the candles and laid the table. There was soup and there was wine, and there was a fire in every room. The king ate and drank and grew red in the face. "I have never," he said, "seen a house so well kept. Marquis, you must be very rich."

"He is," said the cat from his chair at the foot of the table. He was eating from a dish of his own, and he had a napkin tied around his neck. "Richer than he looks. He does not like to boast."

The son looked at the cat. The cat looked back, and his yellow eyes said, Later. There will be time for questions later.

After the meal the king walked with the son in the garden. The princess walked behind with the cat, who told her about the orchard and the dovecote and the view from the west tower. She listened, and she asked questions, and the cat answered every one of them, because he had spent the afternoon learning the castle the way a mouse learns a wall.

"Will you stay?" she asked the cat.

"I go where he goes," said the cat. "But he will stay. He likes it here. And you."

The princess blushed, and the cat pretended not to notice, which is a courtesy cats extend to people they intend to keep.

The wedding was in the spring. The king paid for it, and the cat arranged it, and the son stood at the altar in a coat that fit him. When he said his vows his voice shook only a little. The cat sat on a cushion by the door and watched. Somewhere in the crowd a mouse ran along the wall. The cat turned his head, and then he turned it back. There was no need. He was not hungry, and he was not a mouser anymore. He was something else, and he was still deciding what.

***

The son became a prince, which meant that people called him "your highness" and stood when he entered a room. He never quite got used to it. He was kind, because he had been poor, and he was careful with money, because he had been poorer still. The kingdom did well under him. The harvests came in, and the roads were mended, and the people at the edge of the woods stopped being afraid of the dark, because there was nothing in the dark anymore but foxes and owls and the ordinary night.

The cat became a lord. There was no ceremony for it, but the king gave him a title anyway, and a little house of his own by the gate, and a chair at every table in the land. He wore his boots still, and he walked on two legs when he chose. When he was tired of being a person, he went up on the roof and lay in the sun and was a cat.

"You could have been a king," the prince said to him one evening, on the wall above the moat. "You did all of it. I only swam."

"You swam well," said the cat. "And I have no wish to be a king. Kings have to sit still and listen to people. I would rather be a lord and do as I please."

"Do you please?"

"Often," said the cat. "You married a woman who laughs at your jokes. I have a house with a fire and a dish that is always full. What more is there?"

The prince laughed. "And mice?"

"Ah," said the cat. "Mice." He stretched one paw and looked out over the fields, which were his fields, and the woods, which were his woods. "I chase them still, sometimes. But only for sport. I never eat them. It seems unkind, now that I know what it is to be one."

"You were never a mouse."

"No," said the cat. "But I ate one, and that is almost the same."

They sat on the wall until the stars came out, and the servants lit the lamps below, and somewhere in the castle the princess was singing. The sound came up through the stones and the dark. The cat closed his eyes. He had begun with nothing but a pair of boots and a promise, and he had kept it, which is the only thing a cat really cares about. In the morning he would go down to the kitchen and see what was for breakfast. For now there was the wall, and the night, and his friend beside him, and that was enough.
