Mulan sat at her loom, and the shuttle went back and forth, back and forth. The room smelled of warm straw and the oil in the lamp. Outside, a drum sounded at the gate, and a man's voice read names from a long scroll.

"The northern army calls," the officer said. "Every household sends a man."

Mulan's hands stopped. She leaned toward the window and listened. The officer read on, and then he came to a name she knew.

"Wei of the eastern field."

Her father's name. Mulan looked across the room. Her father sat by the fire, mending a strap. His hair was grey at the temples, and his hands shook a little when he pulled the thread. He was old. He was not strong enough to march.

"Father," she said.

"I heard," he said. He did not look up. "I will go. I went before."

Mulan stood. The loom was still, and the shuttle lay in her hand. She thought of the road north, of the cold and the years. Then she thought of her father's grey hair and his shaking hands, and she made up her mind.

"You will not go," she said. "I will."

Her father looked at her then. "Mulan," he said. "You are a girl."

"I am a girl who can ride," she said. "And I am young. The army will not know."

Her mother came in from the yard with flour on her sleeves. "What is this?" she asked.

"I am going in Father's place," Mulan said. "Before dawn. I will need a horse."

Her mother was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "Then we will find you one."

***

Before dawn, Mulan walked to the eastern market with her father's old armour wrapped in cloth. The market was just waking. A man with a lantern sold her a brown horse, steady-eyed and patient. She counted out coins into his hand.

"She's a good one," the man said. "Where are you riding?"

"North," Mulan said.

He looked at the bundle under her arm. "North is a long way," he said. "Ride careful."

She led the horse home. Her mother was waiting by the gate with a bag of food. Her father stood beside her in his night robe, and his eyes were wet.

"You do not have to do this," he said.

"I do," she said. "But I will come back."

Her mother put the food in her hands. "Eat it slow," she said. "And keep warm."

Mulan put on the armour. It was too big at the shoulders, but the straps held. She braided her hair tight and tucked it under the helmet. In the grey light, she looked like any young soldier.

She mounted the horse. Her father raised his hand, and her mother did too. Mulan did not look back until the gate was far behind her. Then she turned north, and the road went on and on.

The first river was cold and wide, and the horse did not want to cross it. Mulan coaxed her through, her boots full of water by the far bank. The first mountain took three days to climb, and the wind at the top pulled at her helmet strap. She tied it tighter and kept going.

Winter came, then went, then came again. There was a night watch on a ridge when the frost got into her gloves. There was a summer camp where the heat made the armour too hot to touch. She learned to sleep on hard ground and to wake at a whisper.

"You snore like a badger," said Tien, the soldier who slept on her left. He was a farmer's son with a crooked smile and a cracked tooth.

"I do not," Mulan said.

"You do. Like a whole family of badgers." He threw a blanket at her. "Here. It's cold."

She took it. "Thank you."

Rivers, and mountains, and night watches. A torn cloak mended by firelight. A broken wheel fixed with a hairpin. Snow that came up to the horse's knees. Grass that grew taller than a man. Comrades who laughed and argued and shared their bread, and never once guessed. Twelve years, and the count of them was just a list of places she had slept.

***

The Khan's tent was warm and gold, and the Khan himself was old and tired, the way her father had been tired. He looked at the line of soldiers and nodded at Mulan.

"You have served twelve years," he said. "You have not once asked for anything. What would you have?"

The men around her murmured. They knew the answer. A high office, a title, a place at the court with a fine house and servants. Mulan thought of her loom.

"A camel," she said. "A swift one. To carry me home."

The Khan laughed, then stopped, then looked at her closely. "A camel," he said. "That is all?"

"That is all."

"Then take two," he said. "And ride fast."

Tien found her by the horses that evening. "A camel," he said, shaking his head. "Twelve years, and you ask for a camel."

"I have a long way to go," Mulan said.

"Then we ride with you part of the way," he said. "All of us. We've earned the trip."

So they rode south together, the whole line of them, and the mountains fell away behind. Mulan kept her helmet on. Her heart was already at the gate.

The road south ran past fields turning gold. Mulan rode the swift camel and kept her helmet low, and the men sang as they went. On the third morning they came to a river she knew, and past it a road she knew better.

"This is where I turn," Tien said. "My village is east of here."

"Mine is past the orchard," Mulan said. "The one with the crooked wall."

He looked at the orchard, then at her. "You never said where you lived."

"You never asked," she said, and the men laughed, and Tien laughed too, and they rode on.

At the gate of her house, someone had already seen them coming. Smoke rose from the cookhouse. Her mother stood in the doorway with flour to her elbows. Her father came down the steps slowly, his grey hair bright in the sun, and Mulan slid off the camel and bowed to him.

"You came back," he said.

"I came back," she said.

Her sister ran out in her best blue dress with her hair pinned up, and hugged her so hard the helmet knocked against her cheek. "You're taller," her sister said. "That isn't fair."

Her little brother was behind the house, sharpening the long knife on the whetstone. He had grown a whole head taller. "I'm doing the pig," he said, very serious. "I've been practising on turnips."

"Then the turnips are lucky," Mulan said, and he grinned.

Her mother set out grain to grind and called over her shoulder, "Wash first. You smell of camel."

***

Mulan went to her old room when the feast was nearly ready. The loom stood where it had always stood, dust on the beams above it. She set her helmet on the sill and took off the armour piece by piece. It felt heavier off than on.

Her old clothes were folded in the chest. The dress was faded and soft and smelled of cedar. She put it on and pinned up her hair, and her hands remembered the pins without being told.

"Mulan," her mother called from the yard. "Your friends are asking for you."

She went to the window. Tien was below with the others, holding his cup, waiting. He saw a woman in the window and did not know her, and turned to ask someone where the soldier had gone.

Then he looked again.

"Mulan?" he said.

The cup stopped halfway to his mouth. The men went quiet, all of them. Then one of them said, "Twelve years," very softly. Another said, "Twelve years." Then they were all talking at once. Tien was laughing. Mulan was laughing. The little brother dropped the knife and had to be told to pick it up.

"You let me call you 'brother' for twelve years," Tien said.

"You never once looked at my boots," Mulan said.

Her father came and stood beside her at the window, and she leaned her shoulder against his. Down past the orchard wall, two hares ran out of the tall grass and stopped, side by side, ears up, watching the house. Nobody could say which was the doe and which was the buck. It did not matter. They ran off together into the gold field, and the feast was called, and Mulan went down to it.
