My first memory is grass. It was cool and sweet, and it came up to my knees. My mother stood beside me in the meadow, and her coat was the same deep black as mine. She taught me where the stream ran clear and where the shade was thickest at noon. At night I slept with my nose near her flank and listened to the wind comb the long grass.

One morning men came. I was not afraid, because my mother was not. A man with slow hands put a bit in my mouth. It was cold and strange. Then a saddle on my back, and shoes on my hooves. He spoke softly the whole time. "Easy now, young one," he said. "This is only the beginning." My mother watched from the gate. She did not call me back.

I learned to walk, then to trot, then to stand while the farrier worked. The men never shouted. They gave me time. When I pulled against the reins, they waited until I understood. The bit was not a punishment; it was a language. The saddle was not a weight; it was a job. By the end of that first summer I could carry a rider down the lane and back without stumbling once.

***

My next home was a farm with low stone walls and a yard that smelled of hay and rain. A groom named John met me at the gate. He was not young, and he was not tall, but he moved like a man who had never hurried in his life.

"Well," he said, running a hand down my neck. "You're a steady one. I can tell."

He showed me the stable, the water trough, the field where I would graze. Every morning he came with a brush and a quiet word. He never yanked the halter. He never raised his voice. When I was unsure, he stood still and let me think.

One day he put a young boy on my back. The boy was nervous and gripped too tight. I shifted my feet. John put a hand on my shoulder. "Wait for him," he said to the boy. "He'll wait for you. That's the bargain."

And I did. We walked the length of the field, slow as a cloud. The boy laughed. John nodded. "There," he said. "That's how it's done." I learned that work could be kind. I learned that a soft voice carries farther than a whip.

The grand estate was called Oakwood, and the stable there shone like a church. Brass fittings, smooth cobbles, a clock that chimed the hour. The head groom was a man named Ellis, who wore gloves even in summer. "This one's for the carriage," he told the stable boy. "Keep him polished."

At first I liked the work. The carriage was light, the roads were smooth, and the other horses were quiet and kind. But Ellis had a temper. One morning the bit was cold and I tossed my head. He raised his whip, just a flick, but it stung my flank like a wasp. "Steady," he said. "You'll learn."

I learned. I learned to stand still while the harness was buckled too tight. I learned to pull when the load was heavy, even when my legs ached. One day we took a cart of stone up the hill to the big house. The wheels sank in the mud. I leaned into the collar until my breath came in short gasps. Ellis shouted, "Pull, you lazy brute!" But the boy beside him, a thin lad named Tom, said quietly, "He's doing his best, sir." Ellis glared, but he didn't hit me again.

That night Tom came to my stall and slipped me a piece of apple. "You're all right," he whispered. "You just got to keep going." I learned that not all men with whips are cruel. Some are just in a hurry. And some, like Tom, know that a horse has a heart.

***

London was noise and wet. The cab stand on Bridge Street was my home for three years. My driver was a man called Jerry, who wore a patched coat and spoke to me like a friend. "Morning, old boy," he'd say, buckling the harness. "Rain again. We'll take it slow."

Some drivers were not like Jerry. One man, sharp-faced and always late, yanked the reins and shouted at every corner. His hands were hard and quick. I learned to read hands. A man who touches your neck before he touches the reins is gentle. A man who grabs the bit first is not.

Jerry's hands were rough but kind. He never asked more than I could give. When the hill was steep, he climbed down and walked beside me. "Easy now," he said. "We're not racing." The other cabbies laughed at him. "You'll never make a living, Jerry," they said. But Jerry just smiled. "I make a living," he said. "I just don't make a killing."

One night a passenger shouted at Jerry to go faster. Jerry shook his head. "This horse has done a full day," he said. "He'll get you there, but he'll get you there safe." The passenger grumbled, but he sat back. I felt Jerry's hand on the reins, light as a leaf. That was the night I understood: a good driver is not the one who hurries. It's the one who listens.

Years passed. I pulled cabs through rain and fog and once through a night of snow that would not stop. My knees grew stiff, and my wind grew short. Jerry noticed before I did. "You've earned a rest, old friend," he said one evening, rubbing my shoulder. But Jerry grew ill that winter, and the cab had to go. I was sold to a man who worked me six days a week and did not know my name. He called me "the horse." His hands were always in a hurry. One wet morning my feet slipped on the cobbles, and I went down. I was not badly hurt, but the man said I was used up. So he took me to a fair, where horses stand in rows and people look in their mouths and walk away. I stood with my head low. The noise was a river. A man in a brown coat stopped and looked at me a long time. "This one's tired," he said. "He's been a good horse," said the fair man. "He's just old." The brown-coated man ran his hand down my leg and then up to my neck. His hand was slow. It was kind. "I had a horse like this once," he said, half to himself. "When I was a boy." He paid, and he led me away. I did not know where we were going. I only knew his hand on the rope was gentle, and that was enough for now.

***

The road led to a farm with a low stone wall and a field that ran down to a stream. A boy came running when he heard the gate. He stopped short when he saw me. "Is that him?" he asked. "That's him," said the man in the brown coat. The boy walked around me slowly. He put out his hand, and I smelled hay and apples and boy. Then he said my name. My old name. The one from the meadow. "Black Beauty," he said. "You're Black Beauty." I remembered him then. He had been small, and he used to bring me windfall apples. Now he was taller, but his hands were the same. They went to my neck first, before the halter. "He can stay in the long field," said the man. "No work. Just grass and sun." So that is where I am. The grass is deep and green, and the stream talks to itself all day. In the mornings the boy comes with a bucket and sits on the wall and tells me things. I do not always understand the words, but I understand the voice. My legs still ache on cold days, and I am slower than I was. But there is no bit in my mouth here, and no cab to pull, and no one shouts. Some evenings I stand at the top of the field and look at the hills, and I think about my mother, and the meadow where I was foaled. The boy brings me an apple and leans on the gate. "You're home now," he says. And I am.
