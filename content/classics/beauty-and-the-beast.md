The merchant had three daughters, and the youngest was called Beauty. When his ships went down in a storm, the family lost everything. They moved to a small farmhouse at the edge of the woods.

"We have bread and a roof," said Beauty. "That is enough."

Her older sisters disagreed. They complained about the cold and the chores. Beauty learned to bake and mend and milk the goat without complaining.

One morning a letter arrived. One of the merchant's ships had not sunk after all. It had been towed to port with a full cargo.

"I must go and settle the goods," he said. "What shall I bring you when I return?"

The older sisters asked for dresses and jewels. Beauty thought for a moment.

"A rose," she said. "Just one rose. I miss the garden we used to have."

Her father laughed. "A rose? That is all?"

"That is all," she said.

But when he reached the port, the cargo had been seized for debts. He had no money left to bring home. He turned back on foot, tired and ashamed.

***

Snow began to fall as the merchant walked through the forest. He had no food and no coat. Then, through the trees, he saw a light.

It was a castle, warm and golden, with the door standing open. He called out, but no one answered. Inside, a table was set with soup and bread. A fire crackled in the hearth. He ate and slept in a soft bed, and in the morning he found new clothes laid out for him.

"Thank you," he said to the empty room. No one replied.

As he left, he passed a garden where one red rose bloomed in the snow. He thought of Beauty and picked it.

A roar shook the ground. The Beast stepped from the shadows, tall and dark and furry.

"You took my rose," the Beast said. "I gave you shelter, and you stole from me."

"I meant no harm," the merchant said. "It was for my daughter."

"Then she may come in your place," said the Beast. "One of your daughters must return here. Go home and ask them."

The merchant wept and promised. He carried the rose home, though it felt heavy in his hand.

Beauty read the note her father left and folded it small. Her sisters said the castle would eat her alive, but she packed a bag anyway. The road ran uphill through bare trees, and the snow squeaked under her boots. At dusk she reached the open door and stepped inside.

The fire was already burning. Soup steamed on the same long table. "Hello?" she called. Nothing answered but the crackle of the logs. That night she slept in a room with blue curtains, and she did not lock the door.

In the morning she found the Beast in the garden, brushing snow off the rosebushes with one enormous paw. He was very tall, and very furry, and he kept his claws turned away from the flowers. "You came," he said. "I did," said Beauty. "My father is old. I am not."

The Beast nodded slowly. "Then this house is yours," he said. "The library is down the hall. The orchard is through that gate. You may walk anywhere you like." Beauty looked at his sad, careful eyes. "Thank you," she said.

Every evening they ate together at the long table. The plates were warm, the bread was fresh, and the Beast ate neatly, which surprised her. When the last dish was cleared, he asked the same question in the same quiet voice.

"Beauty, will you marry me?"

"No," she said, politely. "Not tonight."

"Then good night," said the Beast, and he bowed his big head and went away. He never argued. He never sulked. He simply asked again the next evening, the way the sun asks the field to grow.

Weeks passed. Beauty learned which halls were warm and which windows caught the morning. She read half the library and taught herself to whistle. "You are happier," said the Beast one night. "I am," she said. "This place is kind to me." The Beast looked at the fire and said nothing more.

***

One evening the Beast carried something to the table and set it down gently. It was a hand mirror, small and silver-backed. "For you," he said. "Look into it and think of home."

Beauty lifted the glass. At first she saw only her own face. Then the picture shifted, and there was her father in his chair by the window, and her sisters sewing, and the kettle singing on the stove. "Oh," she whispered. "They are all right."

"They are," said the Beast. "You can look whenever you miss them." He waited. "Will you marry me, Beauty?"

"No," she said, still watching the little room. "Not tonight. But thank you for this." The Beast bowed and left her with the mirror.

After that she looked every night before bed. Her father waved at the glass, though he could not see her. Her sisters turned their faces away. Winter thinned into spring, and the orchard filled with small white blossoms.

Then one night the mirror showed her father in bed. His face was grey, and her sisters stood around him, and no one was smiling. Beauty set the mirror down and ran to find the Beast.

"He is ill," she said. "I have to go to him."

The Beast stood very still in the dark hall. "Go," he said. "But promise me you will come back within the week."

"I promise," said Beauty. She held out her hand, and he set his great paw in it, light as a leaf.

"Then go quickly," he said. "The door is open for you. It will stay open." Beauty took the mirror and her cloak and ran out into the night, and the road home shone pale under the moon.

Beauty reached home before dawn and found her father no better. His hand in hers was light as paper. "You came," he whispered. "I came," she said, and stayed by his bed all that day. By evening he sat up and asked for soup. By the next morning he was scolding the dog for sleeping on his feet. Her sisters watched from the doorway. "You look well," said the eldest, and her mouth was thin. "Too well for someone who lives with a monster." "He is kind," said Beauty. "Kind," said the second sister. "Kind and rich, and you have rooms and books and a mirror that shows us our own faces. How lucky you are." Beauty said nothing. She helped her father to his chair and opened the shutters. The week passed quickly. On the sixth night the sisters came to her room with honey cakes and warm milk. "Stay one more day," said the eldest. "Father is still weak." "One more day," said the second. "What harm could it do?" Beauty looked at her father, asleep and breathing easy. "One more day," she agreed. The sisters smiled and drew the curtains. That night Beauty dreamed she stood in the castle garden. The roses were bare and black. The Beast lay on the stones by the fountain, and his breath came slow and rough. "You promised," he said, but not as a scold. "I did," said Beauty, and woke with her heart pounding. The moon was high. She pulled on her cloak and ran. Through the gate, down the pale road, past the orchard and the sleeping fields. The castle door stood open. She found him in the garden, and he was cold to the touch.

***

"Beast," she said, and knelt beside him. "Beast, I am here. I am sorry. I am here." His eyes opened, and they were the same kind eyes. "You came back," he said. "I said I would," said Beauty. "I should not have stayed." "You are here now," he said. "That is enough." She took his paw in both her hands and held it. The garden was very quiet. "Beast," she said, "I have been thinking all the way down the road. I do not want to leave you again. Not for a week, not for a day." He blinked slowly. "You know what I am," he said. "I know what you are," she said. "You are the one who gave me books and a mirror and a door that stays open. You are the one who asks me every night and never once makes me say yes. I will marry you, Beast. If you will have me." For a moment nothing happened. Then the roses opened all at once, white and red, and the fountain ran again. Light spilled from every window of the castle. The Beast was not a beast. A young man knelt in the wet grass, holding her hand, and he was blinking at the sky. "You," said Beauty. "Yes," he said. "The castle was asleep, and so was I. You woke us." From the door came the sound of feet and voices, and the whole household came out into the garden, laughing and weeping. Beauty stood up and pulled him up with her. Her father came down the road at sunrise, leaning on a stick, and her sisters followed with their eyes on the ground. Beauty did not scold them. She took her father's arm and led him in to breakfast. The table was long and warm, and there was room for everyone. She looked around the room once, and then she sat down beside the prince, and stayed.
