Efriam Duke had a drum, and because of it his kingdom ate well. The drum was tall as a child and made of pale wood. A skin stretched tight across its head, and a crack down one side had been mended with brass. When the king struck it, a feast arrived. Not the smell of a feast, not the promise of one. Tables came up out of the ground like mushrooms after rain. They were long and crowded with yams and peppers and rice and roasted fish, and the whole country was invited.

That was why the border quarrel ended the way it did. Two families had argued for three generations about a field where the river bent. The field grew good millet and better beans, and both families claimed it, and both families had cousins with spears. They met at dawn on the disputed ground, and the king met them there, and the drum came on a cart behind him.

"You have brought your cousins," said Efriam Duke, looking at the spears. "I have brought my drum. I think mine will win."

He beat it once. The ground shook, the river shivered, and the tables rose between the two families, row after row. They rose until the beans were covered and the spears were leaning against benches and the cousins were sitting down. There was palm wine in clay jugs and a whole roasted goat with its sides split and steaming.

"Eat first," the king said. "Then we will talk about the field."

They ate first. By the time the yams were gone, the eldest of the two families was telling a story about the river bending when his grandfather was a boy. The eldest of the other family was laughing at it. The field was forgotten until someone remembered to ask who had won.

"The field is yours," said Efriam Duke to both of them. "Grow your beans together. If the harvest is bad, I will bring the drum again."

So the border quarrel ended in a dinner, and the drum went back on its cart, and the country slept with full bellies. It had one rule, the drum. The king had learned it from his father, who had learned it from his. If the owner of the drum ever stepped over a stick lying in the road, the feast would spoil. Every dish would turn cold and grey. The drum would fall silent and stay silent until it came back to the king.

Efriam Duke was careful about sticks. He walked with his eyes down, and his sandals were soft, and he had never yet crossed one. He told no one the rule, because it was a small, shameful thing to be afraid of, and because the drum had served him well. But a king's secrets are not always kept. His servants knew, and the servants' children knew, and the children told the birds.

***

Tortoise lived at the edge of the village, in a compound of mud and thatch. He shared it with his wife, his many children, and one lazy brother-in-law who ate more than he worked. Tortoise was not poor. He was simply never full, which is a different thing, and he thought about food the way other people think about money.

One afternoon he was sitting under the palm tree when a grey parrot landed on the fence and began, in the way of parrots, to talk.

"The king's drum," said the parrot, "is the best drum in the world. One beat and there is food for a thousand. One beat and the wars stop."

"I have heard this," said Tortoise. "Everyone has heard this. It is a story for children."

"It is a story for children," said the parrot, "because children are the only ones with sense enough to listen. The drum has a rule. If the owner steps over a stick in the road, the feast spoils. Every dish goes cold and the drum goes quiet. The king walks with his eyes on the ground, and that is why the food keeps coming."

The parrot flew off to bother someone else, and Tortoise sat very still. His mind was not a fast mind, but it was a patient one, and it turned the rule over like a stone in a river. A drum that spoiled if you stepped over a stick. A king who was careful. A tortoise who was not the king, but who might be, if the drum could be made to change hands.

"Husband," said his wife, coming out with a basket, "you have the look you get before you do something foolish."

"I have the look I get before I do something clever," said Tortoise. "Fetch me my best cloth. I am going to see the king about a palm nut."

"We have no palm nut."

"We will have," said Tortoise, "a very famous one."

He began that evening. He did not go to the king at once. He went to the market and told the story of a stolen palm nut to anyone who would listen, and by morning the story had grown. By evening it had a thief in it, and a witness, and a small boy who had seen the whole thing. Tortoise polished it the way a man polishes a story he means to sell. Then he put on his cloth and walked slowly up the hill to the king's compound, rehearsing his case.

The king's compound smelled of rain on hot stone. Efriam Duke was sitting under his thatch with three elders and a bowl of kola nuts, settling the ordinary business of the country. Tortoise came in slowly, the way an important man comes in, and bowed twice. The second bow was lower than the first. "Great king," he said, "I would not trouble you. I am a small man with a small trouble. But my trouble has been told all over the market now, and a small man with a loud trouble must bring it to the proper place."

"Say it plainly," said the king.

"Plainly, then. I had one palm nut. One. It was the last of my father's tree, and I kept it on my shelf for the day my youngest son should marry. A man came into my house while I slept and took it." Tortoise pressed a hand to his chest. "I do not want the man punished. I want the nut. And I want the tree it came from, and the field the tree stands in, and the road to the field, because a thing stolen is never only a thing."

The elders looked at one another. One of them, old and dry as a husk, said, "Who is the thief?"

"Ah," said Tortoise, and let the word sit. "I have thought long about naming him. But if I name him, he is ruined, and his children are ruined, and I am a man who would rather be paid than be right."

Efriam Duke turned the kola nut over in his fingers. "Then name a price."

Tortoise told them. He told them at length. He spoke of the nut, and what the nut would have grown into, and what the tree would have been, and how a man's whole life may hang on one seed, and how he had lain awake. He spoke until the shadows moved off the veranda and the king's own food came and went and came again. He was oily and patient and he never once raised his voice. When at last he stopped, the compound was quiet enough to hear the goats.

"You want the drum," said the king.

"I want what is fair," said Tortoise. "But yes. The drum."

"The drum is not wealth," said Efriam Duke. "It is a way of ending quarrels. If I give it to you, what will you do with it?"

"End quarrels," said Tortoise. "Feed the hungry. Am I not a man of peace?"

The king looked at him a long while. Then he sighed, the way a man sighs when he has weighed a thing and found the scale dishonest but the law heavier. "Take it," he said. "And know that I give it freely, and that you did not take it from me."

Tortoise carried the drum down the hill on his back, walking carefully, and anyone who passed him that evening would have said he was the happiest creature in the country.

***

For a week, Tortoise told no one. He kept the drum in the corner of his house and looked at it, and his wife looked at him looking at it, and said nothing, which was her way of saying a great deal.

Then he beat it. He beat it under the mango tree at the edge of his compound, and the tables came. They came the way rain comes, all at once and out of nothing, and they were loaded, and his children shouted, and his wife put her hands over her mouth. "Eat," said Tortoise, enormously. "Eat, all of you. There is more where this came from. There is always more."

After that he could not stop. He beat the drum on market day, and the whole market ate. He beat it at a wedding, and the wedding ate. He beat it at the river crossing, and travellers who had never heard his name sat down and ate and asked who had done this. The answer ran ahead of them down every road: Tortoise, Tortoise, the tortoise has the king's drum.

"Is it true?" they asked him. "Did the king give it to you?"

"The king saw justice," said Tortoise, "and justice was persuaded." He grew round in the belly and slow in the leg, and he began to wear his cloth like a title. "I did not steal it," he told the crowd at the river. "I reasoned for it. Any man who can reason as I reason could have the drum. But no other man did."

His wife found him one evening asleep beside it with his mouth open. "Husband," she said, "you have not walked to the well in three days. You have not counted the goats. You are fat and you are careless, and careless is a small word for what you are."

"I am a great man," said Tortoise, without opening his eyes.

"Great men still step in the road," she said.

He laughed at her. The drum sat beside him in the lamplight, and he patted it like a dog, and he did not think about the road at all.

The palm wine came out at the naming feast of Tortoise's third cousin. Tortoise did not count the cups, because counting was for small men. He drank until the room tilted gently, like a boat on a slow river, and he told the story of the stolen palm nut four times, each time with more gestures. His wife watched from the doorway with her mouth set in a line.

"Husband," she said, "the road home is dark. Leave the drum and sleep here."

"A great man carries his own drum," said Tortoise, and he heaved it onto his shoulder and went out into the blue evening.

The path to his house ran between two rows of plantain, and halfway along it someone had laid a stick across the dirt. A plain stick, grey and thin, the kind a child drops. Tortoise saw it. He was sure he saw it. But his feet were wine and his head was singing, and stepping over a stick seemed like a thing that happened to other people. He lifted one leg, then the other, and came down on the far side with a small stumble. The drum knocked against his hip. He laughed at the night and went home and slept with his mouth open.

Three days later his wife said, "The yams are finished. Beat the drum."

Tortoise set the drum in the yard and called his family around him. He raised the stick and brought it down, and the sound went out over the roofs the way it always had, deep and round and promising. They waited. The children licked their lips. A pot of water boiled for nothing. The drum sounded again, and the sky stayed empty. No tables came walking. No smoke, no stew, no bread.

"Beat it harder," said his wife.

He beat it until his arm ached. The drum spoke, and the world did not answer. Tortoise put down the stick and looked at the drum as if it had betrayed him. Then he remembered the stick in the road, and his face went the colour of old ash.

"What is it?" said his wife.

"Nothing," he said. "The drum is tired. Drums get tired."

But the yams were finished, and so were the beans, and the children began to ask for food in the small voices that are worse than shouting. Tortoise sat with the drum and thought about the rule. The more he thought, the more he understood that he had done it himself, with his own two feet, on a plain grey stick.

***

For a week Tortoise tried to give the drum away. He carried it to his brother and said, "A gift, from a great man to a good one." His brother asked why the great man's children were thin, and Tortoise had no answer, and the drum came home.

He took it to the palm-wine tapper, who said, "I have heard about the stick, Tortoise. Everyone has heard about the stick." He took it to the headman of the next village, who said, "Keep your gifts. I have a road of my own to walk." He even left it on the step of a rich trader in the night, and in the morning it was back on his own step, with a leaf tucked under the rope to show it had been carried, not lost.

"Nobody will take it," said his wife. "Because nobody is as foolish as you, and everybody knows it."

Tortoise sat down in the dust and put his hands over his face. The drum sat beside him, silent and round, and it was the heaviest thing he had ever lifted.

In the end he carried it back to the king himself, and he did not make a case. He set it on the ground before the throne and stepped back. "It is yours," he said. "I stepped over a stick. I was drunk and I was proud, and the food went cold, and I cannot give it to anyone else."

King Efriam Duke looked at the tortoise for a long moment. "You could have kept it and told the country it was a lie," he said. "You could have blamed the drum."

"I could have," said Tortoise. "But the children are hungry, and a lie does not fill a pot."

The king had the drum taken into his own house, and he said nothing more about the matter, and Tortoise went home smaller than he had come.

At harvest, when the fields were cut and the barns were full, the king came out and beat the drum once. Tables came walking up the road, long and laden, and the whole country sat down to eat, the rich and the poor and the thin and the round. Tortoise sat at the far end with his family, and he ate quietly, and he did not tell anyone the story of the stolen palm nut. His wife passed him a bowl, and he took it with both hands.

"Eat," she said. "And when you have eaten, walk home slowly, and mind the road."

And he did.
