The snow had been falling for three days without stopping, and the cottage at the edge of the wood was buried to the sills. Inside, the family kept the fire low to make the wood last. The youngest daughter sat closest to the hearth, mending a coat that had been mended too many times already.

Her mother was stirring a pot of thin soup. "We'll need to sell the goat come spring," she said, not looking up. "Or the cow."

"Not the cow," said the father. "We'll starve without the cow."

The youngest daughter said nothing. She was used to saying nothing. There were three older sisters, and all of them talked more and better, and all of them had married or gone to service in the village, leaving her behind to mind the fire and the pots.

A knock came at the door.

Nobody knocked out here in weather like this. The father rose, took the poker, and opened the door a crack. Snow blew in. Outside stood a white bear, tall as the doorframe, with fur that steamed in the cold air.

"Good evening," said the bear. Its voice was low and polite. "I've come a long way."

The father's hand tightened on the poker. "What do you want?"

"The youngest daughter," said the bear. "I've watched this house for a while. She's the one who keeps it going. I want her to come with me."

The mother stepped forward. "Come with you where?"

"To my home. She'll be warm there. She'll have food, a bed, and no more mending of coats."

The youngest daughter looked at the bear. Its eyes were small and dark, and they did not look cruel. She thought of the thin soup and the low fire and the endless snow.

"What if I don't want to go?" she asked.

The bear turned its head toward her. "Then I'll leave, and you'll never see me again. But think on it. You could stay here all your life and never see anything else."

Her mother pulled her aside. "Go," she whispered. "We can't feed you. And a bear that talks might be a prince in disguise. Stranger things happen in these woods."

The daughter packed a small bundle. She put on her boots and her heaviest shawl. The bear crouched low, and she climbed onto its back, and the fur was warm and thick, and it smelled of pine and clean snow.

They travelled through the night. The bear did not speak, and she did not ask where they were going. Trees passed. Hills passed. The snow stopped, and the stars came out, and then the sky began to lighten in the east.

They came to a castle she had never seen before, though she had lived in these woods her whole life. It stood on a green hill with no snow on it at all. The bear stopped at the door.

"Go in," it said. "Everything is ready for you."

***

Inside, the castle was warm. Fires burned in every room, though she saw no servants. A table was laid with bread and cheese and cold meat and a jug of milk. She ate until she was full, and then she found a bedroom with a bed so soft she sank into it like water.

When the lights were out, someone came into the room. She heard footsteps, and then the weight of a body on the other side of the bed. She lay still. The person said nothing. In the morning, whoever it was had gone.

It happened every night. The silent person came, and lay down, and left before dawn. She never saw a face.

She could have lit a candle. There were candles on the table. But something held her back. The bear had asked her to trust it, and she had come this far.

Weeks passed. She learned the castle. She learned the garden, where roses bloomed out of season. She learned to cook for one, and then for two, though the second never ate with her.

A bird came to the window one morning. It had a letter tied to its leg. The letter was from her mother.

*Come home for a visit. We miss you. Your father is ill.*

She went to the bear and asked. The bear was in the garden, sitting in the sun.

"You may go," it said. "But your mother will try to keep you. She will tell you things. Do not listen to her."

"She's my mother," said the girl.

"I know," said the bear. "That's why I'm warning you."

She rode home on the bear's back. The cottage looked smaller than she remembered. Her mother hugged her too long. Her father was in bed but not dying. Her sisters had come, and they asked a hundred questions.

That night, her mother came to her room.

"Who is it you sleep beside?" she asked.

"I don't know," said the girl. "He never speaks."

"Then find out," said her mother. "Take a candle. Look at his face. A man who won't show himself has something to hide."

"The bear told me not to."

"The bear," said her mother, "is not your family."

So the girl took a candle. She went back to the castle, and that night, when the silent man slept beside her, she lit the candle and held it over him.

He was beautiful. She had never seen anyone so beautiful. His hair was dark, and his face was calm, and she leaned closer to see him better.

Her hand shook. A drop of hot tallow fell onto his shirt.

He woke. He looked at her, and his eyes were full of sorrow.

"You've ruined us," he said. "I was promised to the troll princess. One year of freedom I had, and now it's over. I must go to her, east of the sun and west of the moon."

The castle began to fade around them. The walls grew thin as mist.

"Can I come with you?" she asked.

"You can try," he said. "But the way is long and cold, and no one has ever found it."

And then he was gone, and the castle was gone, and she stood alone in the snow with only her thin shoes and her small bundle.

She walked all that night because standing still was colder than walking. By dawn her shoes had worn through at the heels, and the snow had found her feet. The castle was gone behind her, and the road ahead was only a road, rutted and grey, going down toward a valley she did not know.

The first old woman lived in a hut at the edge of a pine wood, and she was sitting on the step with a bowl of hot porridge when the girl came down the path. She had one eye, and she used it well enough.

"You look like someone walking the wrong way," the old woman said.

"I'm looking for a place east of the sun and west of the moon," the girl said.

"Never heard of it. Eat something." She pushed the bowl over. When the girl had emptied it, the old woman reached under the step and brought out a small apple, red and hard as a stone. "Take this. You'll know when to bite it. And go and see the winds. They get everywhere. If anyone knows the way, they do."

"Which wind first?"

"East, and mind your manners. He's proud of his temper."

The girl thanked her and went on, and the apple sat in her bundle like a coal. That night she slept under a hedge, and the frost put its hands on her face, and she woke with her hair stiff and white.

The second old woman kept a goat and a spinning wheel in a shed that smelled of wool and smoke. She looked at the girl's feet and made a sound through her teeth.

"Sit," she said. "You can't walk to the ends of the world on those."

She cut the soles from a pair of old boots and tied them under the girl's thin shoes with strips of leather. Then she gave her a small iron key, no longer than a finger.

"The winds don't open doors," she said. "I do." And she laughed at her own joke, and it was not a kind laugh, but it was warm.

"Where do I find them?" the girl asked.

"Walk against the weather. That's the whole trick of it."

The third old woman sat on a stone by a river, washing nothing, just moving her hands in the water. She was so old that her skin had gone the colour of the stone, and for a moment the girl thought she was part of it.

"You've come a long way for a shirt," the old woman said, without looking up.

"How do you know about the shirt?"

"I know about stains. They follow people." She dried her hands on her skirt and took something from a fold in it: a small wooden cup. "Drink from this when you're offered nothing. It makes water taste like something worth drinking."

She pointed downstream, where the land flattened and the sky got big. "South wind first, then west, then north. The north one is the one you want, but he'll only carry you if you ask properly. He doesn't like to be told what to do."

"How do I ask properly?"

"You don't ask like you're owed it," the old woman said, and turned back to the river.

***

The east wind met her on a bare hill where nothing grew but thorn. He came as a great grey push of air that knocked her sideways, and he would not stop moving long enough to be looked at.

"East of the sun and west of the moon?" he said, when she shouted her question into him. "That's not my country. I blow over it, that's all. Cold place. No trees." He spun her around once, as if to see whether she was worth the trouble. "Go and ask my brothers. South first. He's slower, but he listens."

She found the south wind in a field where the grass was still green under a crust of frost. He was warm and smelled of rain and faraway flowers, and he lay in the grass like a man resting after work.

"I can't take you," he said, before she finished asking. "My road goes down to warm water. Yours goes up." He looked at her feet, at the boot soles the old woman had tied on, and something in his face softened. "But I'll tell you this much. The castle you want sits on a rock in a sea that never thaws. The north wind goes there. He hates it there."

"Why does he hate it?"

"Because it doesn't move," the south wind said. "Nothing to push against."

The west wind was a woman, or she was when she wanted to be, standing at the edge of a cliff with her hair streaming out to sea. She turned when the girl came up, and her face was sharp and tired.

"You again," she said, though they had never met. "The bear's girl. I heard you broke the year."

"I did."

"Then you'll have a hard road and deserve it." She looked out at the water a long time. "I can't carry you. I'm going the other way, over the islands, where it's warm. But I'll do one thing for you." She put two fingers in her mouth and whistled, and the sound went up and up, thin as wire, until it was lost. "That calls the north. He'll come when he's ready. He always does."

"How long will he take?"

"However long he takes," the west wind said, and was gone.

The girl waited on the cliff for three days. Snow came and went. Gulls cried over the water and left. On the third night the air went still, and then the north wind arrived, and he was not a man or a woman or a sound. He was a cold that got into her bones and stayed there.

"You want passage," he said. It was not a question.

"I want to go east of the sun and west of the moon," she said. "I know it's far. I know no one has found it. I'm asking anyway."

A long pause. The cold pressed against her, testing.

"You'll have to hold on," the north wind said at last. "I don't stop for anything, and if you let go, I won't come back for you."

She wrapped her arms around his back, and he lifted her off the cliff, and the world went white and roaring, and she held on.

The north wind set her down on a shore of black stones, and the cold went out of him all at once, leaving only a man-shaped hush. "There," he said, and pointed east, and was gone before she could thank him.

She walked until her thin shoes were nothing but strips of leather tied to her feet. The land here had no name she knew. It was all grey rock and grey water, and above it a sky the colour of old tin. Then, at the edge of a cliff, she saw it.

The castle stood on an island that floated in the air, tethered to the sea by a bridge of light. The sun hung west of it. The moon hung east of it. The castle itself sat in the place between, where neither light reached, and it was dark and quiet and very large.

She crossed the bridge of light. Nothing stopped her. That frightened her more than a guard would have.

Inside, the halls were warm and smelled of beeswax and something sour underneath. Trolls sat at long tables, eating. They were huge and slow and their eyes were like wet stones. None of them looked at her twice. She was too small to be a threat and too thin to be a meal.

At the far end of the hall sat a troll princess on a throne of whalebone. She was beautiful the way a cliff is beautiful, and just as kind.

"You're the one," the princess said. Her voice was like gravel in a jar. "The girl who couldn't leave well enough alone."

"I've come for the man," the girl said.

The princess laughed, and the hall laughed with her. "He's mine," she said. "Promised and sealed. You can't unseal a promise with wanting."

"Then let me see him."

"Why would I?"

"Because you have him," the girl said. "And I have nothing. What harm can nothing do?"

The princess looked at her for a long time. Then she smiled, and it was worse than her laugh. "One night," she said. "You may sit beside him until dawn. If you can wake him, he's yours. If not, you stay here and work in my kitchens until you die. That's the trade."

"I'll take it."

They brought her to a room with a single window and a bed where the man lay sleeping. He was thin and pale, and his shirt was stained with tallow that had hardened into yellow wax. He did not move when she came in. He did not move when she said his name. She sat on the edge of the bed and took his hand, and it was cold.

All night she talked to him. She told him about the cottage in the snow and her mother's advice and the bear who had been kind. She told him about the three old women and the four winds and the north wind's cold that was still in her bones. She told him she was sorry. She told him she was not sorry. She told him she would do it all again, every step, every wrong turn, if it brought her here.

He did not wake. But just before dawn, his fingers curled around hers.

***

At first light the troll princess came in with two servants and a bowl of water.

"Dawn," she said. "He's still sleeping. You lost."

"He held my hand," the girl said.

"That's nothing. That's a twitch." The princess set the bowl on the table. "Wash up. The kitchens are this way."

"Wait," the girl said. She was looking at the shirt. The tallow had spread in the night, warmed by his body, and now it covered the whole front of the shirt like a map of every wrong thing that had happened. "Let me wash his shirt."

The princess frowned. "Why?"

"Because it's dirty," the girl said. "And because I made it dirty. And because if I'm going to work in your kitchens, I'd like to leave him something clean."

The princess shrugged. "Fine. It'll take you all day, and you'll still be mine by supper."

She left. The girl took the shirt and knelt by the bowl and began to wash. The tallow was stubborn. It had soaked into the cloth and set there, and the water was cold, and her hands were raw from the journey. She scrubbed and rinsed and scrubbed again. She did not stop. She did not look at the man, because looking at him made her want to stop and cry, and crying would not get the tallow out.

She washed until the water was grey and her arms ached. She washed until the sun moved across the floor and the room grew warm. She washed until the stain began to lift, thread by thread, until the shirt was white and clean and smelled of nothing but soap and sea air.

When the last of the tallow came out, the man opened his eyes.

He sat up. He looked at her. He looked at the shirt in her hands. Then he looked past her, at the room, at the window, at the floating island outside, and something in his face changed.

"You found me," he said. His voice was rough from long silence.

"I did."

"You washed the shirt."

"I did."

He stood. The floor trembled. Outside, the troll princess was screaming, and the castle was screaming with her. The walls cracked. The whalebone throne split down the middle. Trolls ran in every direction, and the light bridge snapped, and the island began to fall.

"Come on," the man said, and took her hand.

They ran through the breaking halls and out into the open air. The island dropped beneath them, but the man did not fall. He stepped onto the air as if it were stone, and she stepped with him, and they walked east, away from the castle, away from the trolls, away from the sun and the moon and the place between them.

The morning came up grey and gold. They walked until the sea was behind them and the ground was solid and the castle was only a speck in the sky, and then nothing at all. The man stopped and looked at her.

"I don't know your name," he said.

"You never asked."

"I'm asking now."

She told him. He said it back to her, slow, like he was learning it. Then they walked on, into the first day of the rest of their lives, and the cold was gone from her bones for good.
