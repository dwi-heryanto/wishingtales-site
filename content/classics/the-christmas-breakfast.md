Meg woke first because the house was too quiet, and quiet in their house usually meant someone was sick or someone had gone. She lay still and listened: no clatter of the stove, no kettle, no Mother's quick steps in the hall. Just the cold pressing against the window and her three sisters breathing in their beds.

She sat up. "Jo. Jo, it's morning."

Jo rolled over, hair in her eyes, and groaned. "What time?"

"Early. Too early for the fire."

Amy's voice came from the far bed, small and clear. "Did she go out already?"

"She must have," said Meg. "She was up before any of us."

Beth said nothing. She was already dressed, sitting on the edge of her bed with her hands folded, looking at the empty shelf by the window. On that shelf, every Christmas morning of their lives, there had been four small parcels wrapped in paper and tied with string.

Today there was nothing.

Jo swung her legs out and stood. "Well," she said. "We all know why."

"I don't want to talk about it," said Amy.

"You spent your dollar on Mother," said Jo. "So did I. So did Meg. So did Beth. That's why there's nothing. We gave it away, and now we get to be noble about it."

"I wasn't being noble," Amy said. "I wanted the pencils."

Meg laughed, and it came out softer than she meant. "You wanted the pencils and you bought her gloves instead. That's about as noble as it gets."

"It's not funny," said Amy. "I wanted them so much."

"I know," said Meg. "I wanted the blue fabric. I've wanted it for a month."

"What did you get her?" Jo asked, turning to Beth.

Beth looked up. "Nothing much. A thimble. Hers wore through."

Jo stared at her. "A thimble. That's all?"

"She needed one."

"She needed a hundred things," said Jo. "You could have bought yourself something and she'd never have known."

"She'd have known," said Beth, and went back to looking at the empty shelf.

They heard the front door open. The cold came in with it, and then Mother, unwinding a scarf from her face, her hands red, a smear of something on her sleeve that she wiped at with her thumb before she saw them watching.

"You're up," she said. "Good. I was afraid I'd have to wake you."

"Where were you?" said Jo.

"Down the lane. The Hummels."

Meg stood. "Is it the baby?"

"The baby is very sick," said Mother. "And there's no food in that house. None. I carried what we had left from last night, but it wasn't enough."

She sat down on the edge of the bed and looked at her four daughters, and none of them said anything, because they all knew what was coming.

***

"I want to ask you something," said Mother.

"Ask," said Jo.

"Our breakfast is in the kitchen. Bread and milk and the cream we were saving. It's not much, but it's more than they have."

Amy's face changed. "You want us to give it to them."

"I want to know if you will."

"That's not the same thing," said Amy.

"No," said Mother. "It isn't."

Jo was already on her feet. "Of course we will. What are we standing here for?"

"Jo," said Meg, "wait. Let her finish."

"She finished. The baby's sick and they're hungry and we have food. What's to discuss?"

Amy said, "I'm hungry too."

"So am I," said Mother. "I've been up since before light and I haven't eaten. I'm not asking because it's easy."

Beth stood up and went to the kitchen without a word. They heard her lifting the pot off the shelf, and the soft clink of the cream jug against the rim of the basket.

Meg looked at Amy. "We can eat later. We always eat later."

"That's not the point," said Amy, but she got up and followed Beth.

The kitchen was cold. The bread sat on the board, a whole loaf, and the milk was in the stone jug, and the cream was in its little jar with a cloth over the top. Beth had already laid the cloth in the basket. She put the bread in first, then the jug, then the cream, and then she stood looking at the basket as if she might add something else to it and couldn't find anything.

"It looks like nothing," said Jo. "It looked like a whole breakfast in there."

"It's enough for a while," said Mother from the doorway. "Put your coats on. All of you. It's bitter out."

They wrapped up. Amy's coat had a torn lining and she kept her arms crossed over it. Jo's boots were too thin at the toes. Meg wound her scarf twice around her neck and then unwound it and wrapped it around Beth's instead, and Beth let her, and nobody said anything about it.

The lane was narrow and frozen hard, the ruts like iron under their feet. Their breath came out white and hung there. The Hummels' house was small and low, and the window was clouded with steam and something else, something that made Amy hang back and take Meg's sleeve.

"I'll carry the basket," said Jo, and took it from Beth without asking.

Mother knocked. Nobody answered, so she opened the door, and the cold went in with them.

The room was crowded with children. Four or five of them, it was hard to count, sitting on the floor and on the one bed where the mother lay with the baby in her arms. The baby was small and grey-faced and did not move except to breathe, and the breathing was wrong, too fast and too shallow, like a bird's.

"We brought breakfast," said Mother.

The woman on the bed turned her head. "You shouldn't have."

"We had it to spare," said Mother, which was not true, and every one of the girls knew it was not true, and none of them said so.

Jo set the basket on the table. Beth uncovered the bread and began to cut it. Meg found the bowls, and they were not clean, and she wiped them on her own handkerchief and did not mention it. Amy stood by the door with her arms crossed and watched the children's faces turn toward the bread, and after a moment she uncrossed her arms and went to help.

There was one piece left on the board when the children had been fed. Beth tore it in half and gave half to the smallest boy, who had already eaten, and put the other half back in the basket, and nobody saw her do it, which was the way she wanted it.

The sick baby lay in a drawer pulled out from the dresser, because there was no crib. Its face was the color of candle wax, and its breath came in small catches, as if each one cost more than the last. Beth went to it first. She had been the one to carry the milk, and she set the jug down on the floor beside the drawer and knelt. The baby's eyes were open but not looking at anything. Beth dipped her finger in the milk and touched it to the baby's lips. The lips moved. She did it again. "There," she said, very low. "There now."

Jo had the bread out and was cutting it into pieces small enough for the children's hands. They gathered around her without pushing, which was worse than pushing, because it meant they had learned not to push. The oldest girl, who might have been nine, held the smallest boy on her hip and watched Jo's knife. "Is that all?" she asked.

"That's all," said Jo. "But it's good bread. Our mother made it."

The girl nodded as if this mattered. Jo gave her the first piece, and the girl broke it in two and gave the larger half to the boy on her hip before she ate her own.

Meg had found a basin and was washing the bowls with water from the pitcher. The water was cold and the bowls were not very dirty, which told its own story. Amy stood in the middle of the room with her hands at her sides, not knowing where to put herself. One of the children, a girl of about six with a scab on her chin, came and stood in front of her and looked up.

"You have pretty hair," the child said.

Amy touched her braid. "Thank you," she said, and then, because she could not think of anything else, she sat down on the floor and let the child touch the end of the braid. The child's fingers were sticky. Amy did not pull away.

Mother was at the bed, speaking to the woman in a voice the girls could not hear. The woman was crying now, without noise, the tears running sideways into her hair. Mother held her hand. The room smelled of damp and sickness and bread, and under all of it the cold smell of snow coming through the walls.

Beth fed the baby three more times from her finger, and then the baby turned its head away and slept. Beth stayed kneeling by the drawer. She had not eaten anything. The half piece of bread she had put back in the basket was still there. Jo saw it and opened her mouth and then closed it again, because there are things you do not say out loud even when you are Jo.

When the basket was empty and the bowls were stacked and the milk jug held nothing but a white ring at the bottom, Mother stood up. "We'll come again tomorrow," she said to the woman. The woman did not answer. She had fallen asleep with her mouth open, and the baby slept too, and for a moment the room was as quiet as any room anywhere.

The girls put on their coats. Amy's fingers were sticky and she wiped them on her skirt, which she would regret later. Beth was the last to leave. She looked back once at the drawer, and then she went out into the cold, and Jo pulled the door shut behind them.

***

The snow had stopped while they were inside, and the world had gone white and still. Their boots made the first marks on the lane. The air hurt to breathe, but it was a clean hurt, and after the close room it felt like something being washed.

For a while nobody spoke. Meg walked with her hands pushed into her pockets and her chin down. Jo walked faster than the others and then had to wait, and then walked fast again. Amy lagged behind, looking at her skirt where the stickiness had made a darker patch.

"I'm starving," Jo said at last. She said it flatly, the way you say a fact. "I know it's not a nice thing to say. But I am. I could eat a whole loaf and then start on the table."

"Jo," said Meg.

"I'm not complaining. I'm reporting."

"You're complaining a little."

"Fine. A little." Jo kicked at a drift and sent a spray of snow across the lane. "But we gave them our breakfast, and now we have no breakfast, and I'm allowed to notice."

Meg was quiet for a few steps. "You're allowed to notice," she said. "Just don't say it in front of Mother."

"I wouldn't."

"I know you wouldn't."

They walked on. The houses along the lane had smoke coming straight up from their chimneys, which meant no wind, which meant the cold would settle in for the night. Amy caught up with them, breathing hard.

"My dollar," she said. "I could have had a whole box of chocolates. The kind with the pink cream inside. I saw them in the window."

"You gave your dollar to Mother," said Meg.

"I know what I did. I'm just saying what I could have had."

Jo laughed, one short breath of it. "At least you're honest."

"I'm not honest. I'm cold." Amy shoved her hands deeper into her coat. "And my skirt is ruined. That child had jam on her fingers, or something worse, and now it's on me."

"It'll wash," said Beth. It was the first thing she had said since they left the house, and they all turned to look at her. She was walking with her arms wrapped around herself, and her face was pale, and she smiled at them as if nothing at all were the matter.

"Beth," said Jo, "did you eat anything?"

"I had some."

"How much is some?"

Beth considered this. "Enough," she said, and would not say more, and Jo knew better than to push, because Beth could be as solid as a wall when she chose.

They came to their own gate. The house looked warm from the outside, which was a lie the windows told. Meg stopped with her hand on the latch.

"We did a good thing," she said. "That's what we'll remember. Not the chocolates. Not the breakfast."

"I'll remember the breakfast," said Jo. "I'll remember it very clearly for at least an hour."

Meg opened the gate. "Then remember it quietly."

The fire had burned low, but Meg knelt and coaxed it back with kindling and a breath. Flames caught the dry wood and threw light across the room. Jo pulled the chairs close. Amy brought the small rug from her own bed and spread it over Beth's knees without being asked. Beth let her, which was how they knew she was cold through.

Mother came in with the last of the milk in a jug and set it on the table. Four cups. One loaf, already cut. She looked at the bread and then at her daughters, and something in her face shifted, the way a window shifts when the sun comes out from behind a cloud.

"There's a letter," she said. "It came while we were out. I saved it until we were all together."

She took it from the mantel and sat down in the low chair by the fire. The paper was thin and creased, and she held it the way you hold something that has traveled a long way to reach you.

"Read it," said Jo.

Mother unfolded it and read in her quiet voice. Father wrote about the cold and the long marches and the mud that got into everything, but he did not dwell on it. He wrote about the men he had met, and a dog that had adopted the whole company and slept in whoever's tent was warmest. He wrote that he thought of them every night before he slept, and that he hoped they were keeping their spirits up. He wrote that he was proud of them. At the end he said he hoped they would not forget to be good to one another, because that was the whole of the law, as far as he could tell.

When she finished, nobody spoke. The fire cracked and settled.

"He's cold," said Beth.

"He's cold," Mother agreed. "And he's thinking of us."

Jo got up and went to the window. Outside, the lane was empty. The Hummels' chimney was smoking again, thin and steady, which meant the fire was lit and the children were warm. That was something. She came back and sat down.

Mother poured the milk. It was thin and blue-white, and there was not much of it. She gave each girl a cup and a piece of bread, and took the smallest piece for herself. They ate slowly. The bread was yesterday's and a little dry, and the milk tasted of the cold.

"This is a poor breakfast," said Amy.

"It's ours," said Meg.

"That's not the same thing."

"No," said Mother. "It isn't. But it's what we have, and we have it together."

They ate. Jo dunked her bread and watched the milk soak in, and thought about the Hummel baby, who had stopped crying by the time they left. She thought about Beth, who had given away her own breakfast and would not say so. She thought about the dollar she had spent on Mother, and how it had bought a pair of gloves that Mother would wear to church and never know the cost of. That seemed like a good trade. She caught Beth's eye across the table and smiled, and Beth smiled back, small and private, as if they were sharing something no one else could see.

The morning felt empty and full at once. Empty because there was no feast and no presents, and full because of the fire and the letter and the four of them breathing in the same room. Jo decided that was enough. She decided it on purpose, the way you decide to carry something heavy instead of putting it down.

Then something rattled in the lane. A cart, by the sound of it, and a horse blowing in the cold.

***

Meg was the first to the window. "There's a cart," she said. "At our gate. It's got a red bow on it."

They all crowded behind her. The cart was small and neat, and the driver was old Mr Laurence's man, bundled up to his eyebrows. He climbed down and came up the path with a crate in his arms, and behind him, hanging back by the gate, was a boy in a good coat with his hands in his pockets.

Mother opened the door. "Good morning," she said. "This is unexpected."

"Mr Laurence sends his compliments," said the man. "He heard the young ladies were having a quiet Christmas, and he thought you might not mind a little something from his kitchen. It's no trouble. It was mostly made already."

He set the crate on the table. Inside was a cake with white icing, and a bowl of oranges, and a dish of something cold and pale that steamed faintly when the lid came off — ices, real ices, in December. There were little cakes and a jar of jam and a bag of nuts.

Amy made a sound that was not quite a word.

"Who's that?" asked Jo, nodding at the boy by the gate.

"That's the young master," said the man. "He wanted to come. He's shy."

Mother went to the door and called out, "You'll freeze out there. Come in."

The boy came up the path slowly, as if he expected to be sent back. He was about Jo's age, with a serious face and hair that needed cutting. He stood in the doorway and looked at the four of them and then at the floor.

"I'm Laurie," he said. "My grandfather said I could come. I hope it's all right."

"It's more than all right," said Meg. "Sit down."

He sat. Mother cut the cake and passed the plates, and the ices went round, and the room filled up with the sound of spoons and the smell of oranges. Jo told Laurie about the Hummels, and he listened as if it mattered. Beth gave him the biggest piece of cake without saying anything. Amy ate two ices and did not apologize.

They ate until they could not eat more, and then they sat by the fire with the crumbs and the orange peel and the letter on the mantel. The morning had been empty and then full, and now it was neither. It was just itself, a Christmas breakfast, and it was enough.
