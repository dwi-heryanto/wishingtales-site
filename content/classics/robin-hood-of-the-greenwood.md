The Nottingham fair smelled of roasted chestnuts and wet wool. Robin of Locksley, seventeen and too tall for his coat, stood at the archery line with his uncle's bow in his hand. The target was a peeled willow wand stuck in the mud, thirty paces off.

A fat merchant in a green hood called out, "Five silver pennies says no lad here can split that wand." Robin grinned. "I'll take that wager, and I'll do it with my eyes half shut." The crowd laughed, but the merchant's coins clinked on the barrel.

Robin drew, breathed out, and loosed. His arrow struck the willow wand dead centre and split it clean down the middle. The crowd roared. The merchant paid up, grumbling.

But a tall man in the King's green stepped forward. He wore a forester's badge and a sneer. "That was luck, boy. And I'll wager a gold piece you can't do it again." Robin's eyes narrowed. "I don't need your gold, sir. I've won fair." The forester spat. "Fair? You're nothing but a poacher's nephew. I'll be watching you."

Robin's uncle pulled him away, but the taunt stuck like a burr. That night, Robin lay in the hayloft and stared at the moon. He could still hear the forester's laugh.

***

The deer came at dusk, a young buck with velvet antlers, stepping daintily through the ferns. Robin's arrow took it clean behind the shoulder. It was a royal deer, marked by law, but Robin had seen the family in the hollow: a widow with three children, their stew pot empty for a week.

He was dragging the deer toward their cottage when the forester stepped out from behind an oak. "I knew it," the man said, drawing his bow. "Poaching the King's deer. You'll hang for this."

Robin held up his hands. "The children are starving. Let me leave half for them and I'll go quiet." But the forester loosed. The arrow hissed past Robin's ear and buried itself in the tree behind him.

Robin didn't think. He grabbed the shaft, snapped it free, and hurled it back. It struck the forester's shoulder and he fell hard, dropping his bow. For a moment the wood was silent.

Robin stood over him, breathing fast. "I didn't want that," he whispered. But the man was out cold, and the King's law would call it murder.

By midnight, Robin had left the deer at the widow's door and walked into Sherwood Forest. The trees closed over him like a green roof, and the road behind grew small and dark. He did not look back.

The first night in the greenwood was cold, and the second was colder. By the third morning, Robin had learned to sleep with his back against a holly tree and his hood pulled over his ears. He was crossing a stream on a fallen log when a shadow fell across the water.

A man stood at the far end of the log. He was a head taller than Robin and twice as wide, with a staff as long as a door in his hand.

"Turn around," the big man said. "This bridge is mine."

"I was here first," Robin said, though he wasn't sure that was true.

The man planted his staff on the log. "Then we settle it."

Robin cut himself a staff from a hazel branch and stepped onto the log. Their staves cracked together, and the sound rolled through the trees. The big man was strong, but slow. Robin was quick, and he landed three taps on the man's knuckles and one on his shoulder.

"You fight well for a small fellow," the man admitted, rubbing his hand.

"And you fight long for a big one," Robin said. He grinned. "But you keep your feet planted like a fence post."

The man's eyes narrowed. He swung hard and low. Robin hopped back, but the log was slick with moss, and his heel went out from under him. He hit the water with a splash that soaked him to the chin.

The big man laughed so hard he had to lean on his staff. Robin came up sputtering, hair in his eyes, and then he started laughing too.

"You win," Robin said. "Fair and square. What's your name?"

"John Little."

Robin climbed out and shook himself like a dog. "John Little. That's the worst name I've ever heard. You'll be Little John from now on." He held out a wet hand. "I'm Robin. I've no roof and no lord. Will you walk with me?"

John looked at the hand, then at the green wood around them. He took it. "I will."

***

News traveled through Sherwood faster than any deer. The Sheriff of Nottingham had posted a notice on every oak from here to the river: an archery contest, open to all, with a golden arrow for the winner.

"It's a trap," John said, reading the notice by the light of their small fire. "He wants to hang you in front of a crowd."

Robin turned the arrow they'd taken from a careless soldier. "Of course it's a trap. But a golden arrow, John. He's offering it because he thinks I can't resist."

"Can you?"

Robin was quiet for a moment. "No," he said. "But I can go as someone else."

He found an old scarlet cloak in a peddler's cart, and a patch for one eye. He blackened his teeth with soot and stooped his shoulders. When he walked into the contest field, nobody looked twice at the beggar with the crooked back.

The target stood far off, a tiny white circle against the green. The Sheriff sat on a raised chair, smiling at the crowd. "Any man who strikes the center wins the arrow," he called. "And I'll know his name before he leaves this field."

Robin shuffled to the line. He drew the bow slowly, as if his arms were weak. The arrow flew. It struck the center with a thud that made the Sheriff stand up.

The crowd roared. Robin shuffled forward to take the golden arrow, and as he passed the Sheriff's table, he set down a folded paper.

The Sheriff unfolded it and read: The arrow is mine. The name is not. Good evening to your dinner.

The beggar reached the table where the golden arrow lay on a red cushion. He picked it up with two crooked fingers and held it to the light. The crowd pressed in close to see.

"A fine shot, old fellow," said a guard, leaning on his spear. "Who taught you to shoot like that?"

"Many years of missing," said Robin, in a cracked old voice. "You learn a great deal from missing."

At the raised chair, the Sheriff had gone the color of a plum. He read the paper twice, then crushed it in his fist. "Stop that beggar!" he shouted. "Bring him back here!"

But the crowd was thick and merry, and the beggar was already moving. Robin stooped past the guards, one hand on his back, muttering about his poor old bones. A farmer's boy offered to carry the arrow for him. A baker's wife pressed a bun into his pocket. Nobody in that crowd wanted the Sheriff to win anything that day.

Behind him the Sheriff was standing on his chair, waving both arms. "The man in scarlet! Stop him!"

Robin turned at the edge of the field. He straightened his back, just for a moment, and lifted the golden arrow high. A cheer went up like a wave. Then he ducked into the trees, and the green swallowed him whole.

He ran a long way, and he laughed the whole way. The scarlet cloak snagged on brambles, and he left a few bright threads on the thorns like a trail of small fires. The one-eyed patch slipped off. He did not go back for it.

By the time the guards reached the edge of Sherwood, evening was coming down, and the forest was all shadow and quiet birdsong. They stood at the tree line and did not go in.

***

In the deep greenwood, a fire was burning in a ring of stones. Little John was turning a fat hare on a stick, and the others were sprawled on the moss, arguing about nothing at all.

Leaves rustled. Robin stepped into the firelight with the golden arrow in his hand.

"Well," said Little John, and he stood up slowly, all seven feet of him. "Did the Sheriff catch you, then?"

"He caught a folded paper," said Robin. "He is welcome to it."

He held out the arrow. The men gathered round, and the firelight ran up and down the gold. Nobody touched it at first, as if it were too fine for their hands.

"We should hang it somewhere," said a thin fellow named Wat. "Where everyone can see it."

"On a tree," said Robin. "Where the Sheriff's men can see it too, if they ever dare come this far." He drove the arrow into the trunk of a great oak, and it stood there shining in the dark.

Then he sat down by the fire and took the baker's bun from his pocket, and broke it into pieces for everyone. The hare was ready. They ate, and the fire cracked, and the leaves talked above them all night.

Robin leaned back against the oak and looked up at the arrow and the branches and the small bright pieces of sky. He was not the forester's nephew anymore. He was somebody else now, and he found he did not mind at all.

"Tomorrow," said Little John, "we should find you a proper bow."

"Tomorrow," said Robin, "we should find breakfast."

And the greenwood kept them both.
