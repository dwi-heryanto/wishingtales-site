Ivy and her family moved into the old house on a Tuesday. The floors creaked, the doors stuck, and the upstairs windows rattled in the wind. Her little brother, Sam, ran through the empty rooms, shouting, "This place is a castle!"

Their dad laughed. "A castle with no furniture and a lot of spiders."

By bedtime, the boxes were still stacked in the kitchen. Ivy's room smelled of dust and old wood. She lay on her mattress on the floor, listening to the house settle. Then she heard it: a soft patter, like rain on a roof, but it came from the hallway.

She sat up. The sky outside her window was clear and full of stars. "Mom?" she called. "Do you hear that?"

Her mom's voice came from the next room. "Hear what, honey?"

The sound stopped. "Nothing," Ivy said. "Just the house."

But she didn't believe that. She pulled on her socks and crept to her door. The hallway was dark, and the patter had stopped. Still, something felt wrong. She put her hand on the wall. It was cold and damp.

***

The next morning, Ivy went back to the hallway. A small puddle sat on the floorboards, right in the middle of the hall. She touched it. Cold water. She looked up at the ceiling. No leak. No crack. The puddle was just there, like it had always been.

Her dad came out of the kitchen with a mop. "Found that too, did you?" he said. "I mopped it up last night. It came back."

"That's weird," Ivy said.

"Old houses are weird," he said. "Don't worry about it."

But Ivy did worry. She crouched down and studied the puddle. It was perfectly round, and the water was so clear she could see the wood grain beneath it. She pressed on the floorboards around it. One of them shifted under her hand.

She pried it up. The board lifted easily, as if it had been waiting. In the space beneath, something glinted. Ivy reached in and pulled out a small brass key. It was cold and heavy, and it fit in the palm of her hand.

She looked at the puddle. She looked at the key. "What are you for?" she whispered.

Ivy carried the key around the house all morning, trying it in every lock she could find. It didn't fit the front door, or the back, or the pantry, or the little cabinet where her mother kept the good teacups. By afternoon she had tried every keyhole she could see, and her fingers were sore from twisting.

"What are you doing?" her brother Sam asked from the stairs.

"Looking for a lock," Ivy said.

"For what?"

"If I knew that, I wouldn't be looking."

Sam shrugged and went back to his game. Ivy sat on the hallway floor, thinking. Then she noticed something she had missed. Down low, near the baseboard, behind the coat rack, was a tiny door. It was painted the same color as the wall, and it was no bigger than a shoebox.

She knelt and fitted the key into the little lock. It turned with a soft click.

The door swung open. Inside was a narrow room, barely wide enough for Ivy to squeeze into. The air smelled of wet wool and old paper. Hooks lined the walls, and on the hooks hung raincoats. Dozens of them, in every size and color, all slightly damp to the touch.

On a small shelf sat a leather journal. Ivy opened it. The handwriting was spidery and old.

"My name is Eliza Vane," the first page read. "I watch the weather. I keep the storms from hurting the village. This house is my shelter, and I am its keeper."

Ivy turned the pages carefully. Some were stuck together with damp. She read on.

***

The journal told of a great storm that came off the sea many years ago. It was bigger than any Eliza had ever seen, and it was heading straight for the village.

"I could not stop it," she wrote. "So I called it inside. I opened every window and let it pour into this house. I trapped it in the walls, in the pipes, in the wood itself. The village was saved. But the storm has never left."

Ivy's heart beat fast. She read the next page.

"The storm wants out. It pushes against the walls. It makes puddles on the floor. It whispers in the rain. I have kept it sealed for years, but I am old now, and I am tired. One day someone else will have to decide what to do with it."

The last entry was dated fifty years ago.

Ivy closed the journal. The raincoats rustled around her, though there was no wind. She backed out of the little room and shut the door.

That night, the rain came back. It started as a drip in the hallway, then a patter, then a steady beat. Ivy stood in the dark and listened. The storm was in the walls. It wanted out. And she was the only one who knew.

By morning the drip had become a drumming. Ivy stood in the hallway with her school bag on her shoulder and watched water bead along the ceiling, gather, and fall. A puddle spread across the floorboards like a slow grey hand.

Her mother came down the stairs. "Ivy, why is your bag on the wet floor?"

"It's raining," Ivy said.

"It's sunny outside. I hung the washing out." Her mother stepped around the puddle, frowning. "The plumber comes Thursday. Until then, put a bucket under it."

At school Ivy could not sit still. She counted the hours until she could go home. When she finally opened the front door, the hallway was louder than before. The water reached her ankles. It had climbed the wallpaper in dark fingers. Somewhere behind the wall, the storm thumped like a heartbeat.

Her father stood in the kitchen doorway. "I've called the plumber twice," he said. "There's no leak. The ceiling is dry. We may have to pull the walls down."

"No," Ivy said, too quickly.

That night she sat on the stairs with the brass key in her fist. The rain fell steadily in the dark. She thought of the weather-watcher's words: the storm wants out. She thought of the village that had been saved. She thought of her parents, tired and worried, talking quietly in the kitchen about damp and money and moving.

"You can't stay in there forever," she whispered to the wall. The rain seemed to answer, tapping faster. Ivy stood up. Her hands were shaking. She went to the little door and put the key in the lock.

***

She turned the key. The little door swung open, and the storm came out.

It was not water. It was wind and rain and something else, a bright grey rushing thing that filled the hallway and pressed against the walls. Curtains lifted. Pictures swung on their hooks. Ivy held onto the stair rail and shouted, "Go on, then! Out!"

Every window in the house flew open at once. The storm poured toward them. It swept over Ivy's face, cold and wild, and she laughed without meaning to. Rain hit the glass and ran down the outside in rivers. Somewhere above, her parents called out in surprise.

The hallway emptied. The water on the floor shrank, then vanished. The wallpaper dried pale and clean. The house creaked once, like a person setting down a heavy bag, and went quiet.

Her mother came down the stairs slowly. "The windows were all open," she said. "And it's raining, but only just outside. Only on the garden."

"I think the house was holding something," Ivy said. "And now it isn't."

Her father looked at the dry floor. He looked at the little door, standing open on its empty cupboard. "Well," he said at last, "I suppose we can stop calling the plumber."

That night Ivy slept in her new room. Rain pattered softly on the roof, the ordinary kind, the kind that falls on gardens and gutters and stays outside where it belongs. The house settled around her, warm and dry, and for the first time Ivy felt that it was home, and that it was glad she was in it.
