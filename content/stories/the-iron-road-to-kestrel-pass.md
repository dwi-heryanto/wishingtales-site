The train had been standing still for twenty minutes before anyone said the word out loud.

Anya knew the sound of the Kestrel Pass line better than her own breathing. The old blue carriage creaked when it climbed, hummed a low note through the tunnels, and always, always lurched forward again after the brief stop at Harrow Siding. This time the lurch never came. The windows had gone white, then grey, then white again as the blizzard pressed against the glass. Snow climbed the panes in slow, patient fingers.

At the front of the carriage, a man in a navy coat stood up. His name was Mr. Bell, and he had checked Anya's ticket every Friday for six years without once remembering her face. He cleared his throat, and the sound carried strangely in the cold.

"Ladies and gentlemen," he said, "I need you to stay calm. The engine has stalled. The driver is working on it."

A woman near the door pulled her scarf tighter. "Working on it how? It's a blizzard out there."

Mr. Bell's jaw worked. "The driver is working on it," he repeated, and Anya heard the crack in his voice, the small splinter that meant he did not believe himself.

The carriage was not full. Twelve passengers, maybe thirteen, scattered across the seats like dropped coins. An old man with a walking stick. A mother holding a sleeping child. Two boys who had spent the first hour kicking the back of Anya's seat and had now gone quiet. Above them, the lights flickered and settled into a dim yellow that made everyone look ill.

Anya pressed her palm against the window. The glass was so cold it burned. Beyond it, the pass had vanished. There was no mountain, no track, no sky, only a swirling wall of white that seemed to move in every direction at once. The train had become a small, bright box in the middle of nothing.

Mr. Bell walked down the aisle, checking each window, and stopped beside her.

"You're the girl from the market," he said. "You ride this line often."

"Every week," Anya said. "Since I was small."

He nodded slowly, as if that meant something. Then he lowered his voice so the others would not hear. "The engine is dead. Not stalled. Dead. The driver says the fuel line has frozen solid, and the pass is snowed in behind us. We cannot go forward, and we cannot go back."

The words landed in her stomach like stones.

"How long until someone comes?" she asked.

"The line is checked every morning at seven," Mr. Bell said. "If the storm eases, a rescue crew might reach us by noon. If it does not ease..." He did not finish. He did not have to.

Behind them, the old man with the walking stick began to cough, a deep, ragged sound that went on too long. The mother wrapped her arms around her sleeping child and stared at the white window. The two boys had stopped kicking entirely.

Anya looked at the ceiling of the carriage, at the thin metal panel above the luggage rack, and thought about a map she had seen once in the stationmaster's office. It had been old, faded at the edges, and it had shown a thin dotted line running under the pass. A maintenance tunnel. Built before the railway, kept for the men who checked the tracks.

The storm was not easing. She could feel it in the way the carriage shivered.

***

Anya stood up so fast her knees hit the seat in front.

"There's another way," she said.

Nobody turned. The wind was too loud, and she was a girl of fifteen in a wet coat, and the adults were busy being frightened. So she said it again, louder, into the middle of the carriage.

"There's a maintenance tunnel under the pass. It comes out above the village. We can walk it."

Mr. Bell turned from the window. "Where did you hear that?"

"The stationmaster's office. There's an old map on the wall, behind the door. Dotted line, all the way under Kestrel Rock." Anya pulled her glove off with her teeth and held up her hand, tracing the route in the air. "It starts about a hundred paces back along the track. There's a hatch, low down, where the cutting meets the rock."

The woman with the child shook her head. "You want us to leave the train? In this?"

"I want us to be somewhere warm when the rescue comes," Anya said. "This carriage is getting colder. The old man can't sit here till noon."

The old man raised his walking stick a little. "I'd rather walk than freeze," he said. "I've walked worse."

One of the boys spoke up. "What if the tunnel's blocked? What if it's a dead end?"

"It's not a dead end. It goes through." Anya said it firmly, though her heart was hammering. She had seen the map. She had not seen the tunnel. "And if it's blocked, we come back. The train isn't going anywhere."

Mr. Bell rubbed his face with both hands. He looked at the white windows, at the coughing old man, at the mother and her child. Then he looked at Anya.

"You're certain about the hatch?"

"I'm certain about the map," she said honestly. "I can find it."

The conductor straightened his coat. "Right. Everyone take what you can carry. Blankets, water, the emergency lamp from the front. We're going to walk."

A murmur ran through the carriage, not quite argument. The mother began to wrap her child in a second scarf. The two boys pulled on their gloves without being told twice.

Anya went to the door and pushed it open. Cold hit her like a wall. Snow swirled into the carriage, bright and silent, and beyond the small platform the world had been erased entirely.

She stepped down onto the track and turned toward the cutting, counting her paces, and the others followed her into the white.

The tunnel mouth was a black rectangle in the white world, and the snow had drifted halfway up its iron doors. Anya dug with her gloves until she could reach the latch. It lifted with a groan, and Mr. Bell put his shoulder to the door and shoved. The gap widened, and cold air rushed out, smelling of stone and old smoke. "Lamp," he said. The older boy, Tam, clicked the emergency lamp on and held it high. Its beam pushed a weak yellow circle into the dark. Rails ran straight ahead, twin lines of steel glistening under a thin skin of ice. Snow blew in behind them and settled on the sleepers. "Stay close," Mr. Bell said. "Watch your feet. The rails are slick." They went in. The tunnel swallowed the wind first, then the light. Anya counted her paces under her breath, the way her father had taught her, and the lamp swung ahead of her like a small moon. The mother carried her child, and the old man leaned on Tam's arm. No one spoke. Their boots rang on the sleepers, and the tunnel breathed damp air against their faces. Then the ground shook. It began as a low growl, felt through the rails and the soles of their boots, and Anya turned just as the roof behind them cracked. "Back!" Mr. Bell shouted, but there was nowhere to go back to. A slab of stone and packed earth came down in a roar, and the lamp swung wild, and the world filled with dust. When it cleared, the way behind them was gone. A wall of rubble blocked the tunnel, and loose stones still clicked and settled in the dark. Mr. Bell lay half under a fallen beam, his face grey. "My leg," he said, very calmly. "I think it's broken." Tam and Anya pulled the beam aside. The conductor's trouser leg was dark and wrong-angled, and when they tried to move him he made a sound that was not a word. The mother pressed her scarf to his forehead. "We can't go back," she said. "And we can't stay here." Anya looked at the rubble, then at the lamp in Tam's hand. The beam was weaker now; the battery was running down. "We go on," she said. "The tunnel has to come out somewhere." Mr. Bell caught her wrist. "The map," he said. "You still have the map." She pulled the folded paper from inside her coat. It was damp at the edges but whole. "I have it," she said.

***

They made a sling from two belts and a blanket, and Tam and the old man carried Mr. Bell between them. The conductor kept his jaw tight and did not cry out again, but his breathing was loud in the dark. Anya walked ahead with the lamp, counting her paces, watching for the marks her father had drawn. The tunnel ran straight for a long time. Water dripped somewhere to the left, and the rails gleamed faintly. Then the wall opened. A side passage branched off to the right, unmarked on the map, its mouth a rough arch of stone. The air that came out of it was colder and smelled of wet iron. Anya stopped. She unfolded the map and held it close to the lamp. The main route ran on ahead, a neat line with three small crosses where the maintenance shafts should be. The side passage was not there at all. "What is it?" Tam asked. "A junction," she said. "It's not on the map." The old man lowered his end of the sling. "Not on the map means not safe," he said. "We stay on the marked route." Anya looked down the side passage. The floor there was wet, and something glinted at the far edge of the lamplight. Water. She looked back at the main route, and then at the lamp, whose beam had begun to flicker. "The marked route has three shafts," she said. "The first one is half a mile on. If the lamp dies before we reach it, we're blind." "And the side passage?" Tam asked. "It goes down," she said. "I don't know where." Mr. Bell lifted his head from the sling. "You're the one with the map," he said. "You're the one who got us this far." His voice was thin, but his eyes were steady. "Choose." Anya looked at the dark mouth of the side passage, then at the flickering lamp, then at the faces turned toward her in the gloom. The child had stopped crying. The mother held her tight. "We take the short way," Anya said. "It's wet, and it's steep, but it's shorter. I can feel the air moving. That means it comes out." The old man opened his mouth to argue, but Tam was already turning toward the side passage. "Then let's move," he said. "Before the light goes."

The side passage narrowed until Anya had to turn her shoulders to pass. Water dripped from the ceiling in a slow, cold rhythm, and the floor sloped steeply down. Behind her, the lamp threw their shadows ahead, huge and wavering. No one spoke. The child's breathing had evened out, but the mother kept one hand on the wall as she walked.

After a hundred yards, the passage opened into a low chamber, and Anya stopped so suddenly that Tam bumped into her.

Water filled the chamber from wall to wall, black and still, reflecting the lamp in a broken line. It lapped against a ledge of stone just below their feet.

"How deep?" Tam asked.

Anya knelt and lowered the lamp until its heat touched the surface. The water swallowed the light. "Deep enough," she said.

The old man made a sound in his throat. "We go back. We take the long way."

"There's no back," Tam said. "The tunnel fell in, remember?"

Anya stood and lifted the lamp high. Across the sump, maybe thirty feet away, a metal hatch was set into the wall, rimmed with frost. "There," she said. "That's the way out."

"It's frozen," the mother said. "Look at it."

"Then I'll break it." Anya handed the lamp to Tam. "Hold this steady. I need both hands."

She stepped into the water. Cold knifed up her legs, stole her breath, and kept rising until it reached her waist. The bottom was slick with silt, and she moved slowly, testing each step. Halfway across, her foot slipped, and she went under to her chin. The lamp swung wildly behind her.

"Anya!" Tam called.

"I'm fine." Her teeth chattered. "Keep the light on the hatch."

She reached the far wall and gripped the hatch's iron handle. It didn't move. She pulled harder, braced her feet against the wall, and heaved. The ice cracked, but the hatch held. Again. The handle bit into her palms. On the third pull, something gave, and the hatch swung outward with a groan of breaking ice.

Light poured in. Not lamp light. Dawn light, pale and clean, and wind that smelled of snow and pine.

Anya hauled herself through and turned back. "Come on!" she shouted. "It's shallow here. Follow the wall!"

Tam came first, holding the lamp above the water, then the mother with the child on her back, then the old man, muttering, and last Mr. Bell, his arm still in the sling, his face grey with pain but his steps steady. Anya pulled each of them through the hatch and onto the snow.

***

They stood on a shelf of rock above the pass, and below them the world had turned white in the night. The blizzard had passed. In the east, the sky was the colour of a peach, and the last stars were going out.

Anya pointed down the slope. "There," she said. "Kestrel Pass village."

Lights moved in the valley, small and orange, strung out along the road. Lanterns. A lot of them, and they were coming up.

"Rescue party," Tam said. "They must have seen the train wasn't moving."

Mr. Bell let out a long breath. "You were right," he said to Anya. "About the tunnel. About the short way." He shook his head slowly. "I've ridden this line for thirty years, and I never knew that hatch was there."

"My father showed me the map," Anya said. "I just remembered."

The child slid down from her mother's back and stood in the snow, staring at the village lights. "Are we going home now?" she asked.

"Yes," her mother said. "We're going home."

Anya led them down the slope, picking a path between the drifts. The snow was knee-deep in places, and the wind cut at their faces, but the lights grew closer with every step. Halfway down, she heard voices calling, and then the first lantern appeared around a bend in the road, carried by a man in a red coat with a rope over his shoulder.

"There they are!" he shouted. "Over here!"

More lanterns came, and more voices, and then hands were reaching for them, blankets and flasks and steady arms. Anya stood at the edge of it, her boots soaked, her hands raw, and watched the passengers from the last train to Kestrel Pass be led down toward the village, one by one, until only Tam was left beside her.

"You coming?" he asked.

Anya looked back at the mountain, at the hatch in the rock, small now and dark against the snow. Then she turned and followed him down.
