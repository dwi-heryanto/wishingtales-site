Tomas had fished the old bridge a hundred times and never caught anything worth the worm. That was the point of it. The bridge was close, the water was slow, and nobody minded a boy sitting on the rail with his feet hanging over the dark.

The sun had gone down behind the mill when the line went taut. Not the nervous tap of a nibbling fish. A hard, steady pull, as if something on the other end had wrapped the hook around its fist and meant to keep it.

"Easy," Tomas said, to the rod or to himself, and reeled.

The thing came up through the brown water without a splash. It was flat and black and roughly the size of a serving plate, and it lay on the surface like spilled ink refusing to spread. When he lifted it clear of the river it hung from the hook and dripped, and the drips were not water. They were a darker nothing, and they fell without sound.

Tomas set the rod down and crouched. The shape twitched.

Then it crawled up the line.

He watched his own hook travel backward through the air. The shadow folded itself along the nylon in little heaves, like a hand pulling itself along a rope. It reached the eye of the rod. It reached his fingers, which had gone cold and stupid and would not let go.

"Get off," he said, and shook his hand.

The shadow slid from the line onto his wrist and lay there, flat against his skin, warm as a palm. It had no weight. That was the worst part. Something that looked so heavy had no weight at all, and it shaped itself to the bones of his arm like it had been poured there.

Tomas stood very still on the bridge. Below him the river went on doing what rivers do, which is nothing, endlessly. His wrist itched. He scratched it with his other hand and the shadow moved under his fingers, quick and shy, and settled again.

Then, slowly, it drained away down his sleeve and vanished into the ordinary dark of his own arm. His shadow on the bridge planks was just his shadow again.

He packed his rod. He did not run. He walked, which felt braver, and he did not look back at the water until he was at the far end of the bridge. When he did look back the river was flat and innocent and empty. A long way under it something turned over once, like a sleeper changing sides.

***

His grandmother was on the porch with the lamp lit, which meant she had been waiting, which meant she had known he was at the bridge.

"Sit," she said.

Tomas sat on the step. The lamp made a small yellow room out of the porch and left the rest of the night outside it. His sleeve was still damp where the thing had lain, though he had rolled it up twice to check.

"I caught something," he said. "It wasn't a fish."

"No." She rocked once, forward and back. "The bridge has been taking people since before this town had a name. That's why the crossing is stone and not wood. Wood rots, and the river gets impatient."

"People fall off bridges all the time."

"Some fall." Her hands were folded in her lap, and she did not look at him. "Some are walked off. The river calls, and a person hears a voice they know, and they go down to listen better, and the water is patient and the bank is steep."

Tomas thought of the pull on the line, steady as a hand. "And the shadows?"

"The river gives back what it takes." She said it like a lesson she had learned badly. "Not the people. Never the people. But every one it keeps leaves a shadow in the water, and the shadows stay down there, one for each soul that never came home. They gather under the bridge. My mother counted them. Her mother counted them."

"How many?"

"Don't." The word came out sharp, then softer. "Don't count them, Tomas. Counting is how it starts. You count to see how many, and then you want to know which, and then you're standing on the bank at midnight asking questions of the water."

The lamp guttered. Outside its yellow room, the night leaned in to hear.

"What if one touches you?" Tomas asked. "What if one comes up the line and lies on your arm?"

His grandmother was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached over and took his wrist in both her hands, turning it to the light. Her thumbs found the place where the shadow had lain, and she pressed there until it hurt.

"It didn't take anything," she said at last. "It came to look at you. That's worse, boy. That means it knows your face." She let go. "Go inside. Bolt the door. And tomorrow you stay off that bridge."

Tomas went inside. He bolted the door. In the dark of his room he held up his hand against the window, and the moon threw his shadow on the wall, and the shadow was the right shape, and it was the right size.

It was facing the wrong way.

***

Tomas did not stay off the bridge. He told himself he was only walking past it, on the far bank where the path ran along the water, but his feet carried him down the gravel to the place where the old stone arch met the mud. Dusk came early under the trees. The river was low and slow, and it made a sound like someone turning over in their sleep.

He stopped at the edge of the bank and looked under the bridge. The dark there was thicker than the rest of the evening, and it moved. Not water. Shapes. Flat black shapes, no thicker than paper, sliding over one another in the shallows like a pile of leaves stirred by wind. He counted seven before he lost track.

One of them lifted its head. It had no face, only a smooth black curve where a face should be, and it made a small sound, almost a question.

"Who's there?" Tomas said, and his voice came out smaller than he meant.

The shapes went still. Then the whispers started, and they were worse than silence. They were voices he almost knew, like a song heard through a wall, like the way his mother said his name when she was tired. One whisper said something about the weather. Another said a number, over and over, as if counting.

A shape near the arch rose up out of the water. It was taller than the others, and it spoke clearly, in a voice that was almost his father's.

"Tomas," it said. "You came back."

He stepped back, and his heel caught a root, and he sat down hard in the mud. The tall shadow did not come closer. It only waited, and the waiting was the worst part, because it meant the thing had all the time in the world.

"What do you want?" Tomas asked.

"You already gave it," the shadow said. "You just don't remember giving it."

The other shapes whispered their agreement, a sound like paper tearing very slowly. Tomas got up and ran, and the whispers followed him all the way to the road, where the streetlights began.

***

His grandmother was sitting at the kitchen table with the lamp turned up high and her hands flat on the wood, as if she had been holding the house down. She did not ask where he had been. She looked at his feet, at the mud on his shoes, and her mouth went thin.

"You went under it," she said.

"I had to know." Tomas sat down across from her. "One of them talked to me. It knew my name. It said I already gave it something."

His grandmother closed her eyes. For a long moment the only sound was the clock, which had never been a loud clock, and which now seemed very loud indeed.

"The river takes people," she said at last. "It has always taken people. Before the town had a name, before the bridge was stone, it took them. And it gives back what it takes as shadows, one for each soul that never came home. That's what you saw. A ledger written in black water."

"But it said I gave it something."

"Because you did." She opened her eyes and looked at him, and there was no softness in her now. "Your shadow. It was claimed the moment you pulled that thing up on your hook. A shadow is the part of you that the river can hold. It holds it now. And on the third night, it will come for the rest."

Tomas looked down at the floor. The lamp threw his shadow across the boards, and it lay there quietly, the right shape, the right size. He had checked it a hundred times since the first night. It had not changed.

"It's still there," he said.

"Is it?" His grandmother reached over and turned the lamp down, and the room went dim, and his shadow stretched long and thin across the floor. At the far end of it, near the door, it lifted one hand.

Tomas did not lift his.

"Three nights," his grandmother said. "Tonight is the second. Tomorrow is the third. Unless you bring back the shadow of the first one it took, the river will come for the rest of you, and there will be nothing left to bury."

"The first one," Tomas said. "Who was it?"

His grandmother was quiet for a long time. Then she said, "A girl. Before the bridge. She went in at the deep bend, and the river kept her. Her shadow is the oldest one down there. It waits at the bottom, where the water is coldest. It has been waiting a long time."

"How do I bring it back?"

"You go down to it," she said, "with a light and a hook, and you ask. And you had better hope it still remembers how to answer."

***

The lantern was his grandfather's, brass and glass, and it threw a small warm circle that made the dark around it seem deeper. Tomas climbed down the bank at midnight, one hand on the rope his grandmother had tied to the bridge rail, the other holding the lantern high. The reeds clicked together in the wind. The mud pulled at his boots. At the water's edge he stopped and looked back. The bridge was a black line against a sky full of stars. Under it the river ran quiet and flat as a sheet of slate.

"You don't have to do this," his grandmother had said at the door, and he had said, "Yes I do," and she had not argued. She had given him the lantern and the hook and a length of rope, and she had kissed his forehead, and her mouth had been cold.

He waded in. The cold went through his boots and up his legs and settled in his bones. The lantern light slid over the water, and beneath it the river was not empty. Shapes moved there, flat and black, drifting like leaves. Some were small. Some were the size of men. They turned toward the light, and Tomas heard whispering, a hundred voices all talking at once, low and urgent, and he could not make out the words.

"I'm here for the first one," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he felt. "The oldest. I'm here to take her back."

The whispering stopped. The shadows hung still in the water, and then they parted, drawing back to either side, and a path opened ahead of him, down and down into the cold dark. Tomas took a breath and followed it.

The water rose to his chest. The lantern hissed and spat but stayed lit. At the bottom, where the riverbed was soft and cold as a grave, a shadow waited. It was smaller than the others, a girl's shape, thin and still, and it had its hand out.

Tomas lowered the hook. "I'm here," he said again. "I've come to bring you back."

The shadow's hand closed around the hook. It was not cold. It was nothing at all, a pressure without temperature, and it pulled, gently, and Tomas understood that it wanted to come with him.

***

Getting out was harder than getting in. The current pushed against him, and the lantern swung wild, and the shadows on either side pressed close as if they would follow. Tomas kept his eyes on the bank and his hand on the rope and he pulled, one step at a time, and the shadow came with him, light as a breath, its hand still closed around the hook.

At the water's edge he turned. His own shadow lay on the mud behind him, long and thin in the lantern light, and it was not moving. It had lifted one hand, the way it had in his grandmother's room, and it was reaching toward the shadow on the hook.

"No," Tomas said. "You don't get to take her back."

His shadow did not answer. It only reached, and the shadow on the hook reached too, and where their hands met there was a sound like a knot pulling tight.

Tomas felt it in his chest. Something gave way, a small clean snap, and then his shadow was gone from the mud. The girl's shadow stood on the bank beside him, whole and separate, and his own was nowhere.

He climbed the bank. The rope was still tied to the bridge rail, and he pulled himself up it, and the lantern swung, and the river below him was quiet. The shadows under the bridge had drawn back into the dark. The whispering had stopped.

He stood on the bridge and looked down at the water. It was flat and black and empty, and the stars moved on it, and there was no second shape beside him on the planks.

The girl's shadow stood at the far end of the bridge, where the road began, and it lifted one hand, and then it turned and walked away into the dark, and Tomas did not follow it.

He walked home. The street was empty and the streetlight at the corner threw a circle of yellow on the pavement, and Tomas stood in it and looked down. There was no shadow. Only the light, and the pavement, and the cold.

His grandmother was waiting at the door. She looked at him for a long time, at his feet and the empty place beside them, and then she said, "You brought her back."

"Yes," Tomas said.

"And the river let you go."

"Yes."

She nodded and stepped aside and let him in, and the door closed behind him, and the bridge was quiet, and the river ran on under it, and the shadows under the bridge did not move.
