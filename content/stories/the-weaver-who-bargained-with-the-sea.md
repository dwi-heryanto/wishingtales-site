Sela had woven mourning cloths before. For her grandmother, a grey shawl with a border of gulls. For the miller's stillborn son, a blanket so small it fit in two hands. But this one was for Jori, and her fingers kept stopping.

The loom stood by the window, where the light came in flat and pewter-coloured off the water. She had warped it with undyed wool, the colour of fog, and she had no pattern in mind. A mourning cloth for a brother should say something. Jori had been sixteen, loud, always wet from the boats, always singing something off-key. What did you weave for that?

"You've not eaten," said her mother from the doorway. She held a bowl of broth and did not come in.

"I'm not hungry."

"Sela."

"I'll eat when the cloth's done." She didn't look up. The shuttle went across, and back, and across. Outside, the tide was going out, and the rocks below the house showed their wet black backs one by one.

Her mother set the bowl on the sill and left. Sela heard her feet on the stairs, slow, the way people walked in a house where someone was missing.

By evening the cloth was a hand's width deep, and it was wrong. She could feel it. The threads lay flat and dead, and when she pressed her palm to them they were cold, colder than wool had any right to be.

She went out to the tide pools to clear her head. The sea was flat and quiet, the way it went quiet after a drowning, as if it had done its work and was resting. In the biggest pool, the one ringed with barnacles, the water lay still as a mirror.

And then it wasn't still.

"Sela," said the pool. Not a voice from a throat: a voice from everywhere, from the stone and the weed and the cold air over the water. "You weave him a cloth. But you want more than cloth."

She should have run. Any sensible girl would have run up the shingle and barred the door. Instead she knelt by the pool and looked into it, and saw her own face looking back, and behind her face, very faint, the shape of a boy with wet hair.

"You have his voice," she said. Her own voice cracked. "You took it. He went under calling, and you took it."

"I keep what is given," said the sea. "But I am not unkind. Come down at low tide, to the cave under the point. We will talk of a price."

The water shivered, and was only water again. Sela stayed kneeling until her knees ached and the light went orange and then grey, and the pool showed nothing but sky.

***

At the lowest tide of the month, Sela climbed down the cliff path with a lamp in her teeth and a coil of rope over her shoulder. The path was slick with weed, and twice her foot went out from under her, and twice she caught herself on the rock.

The cave mouth opened like a shout. Inside, the air was cold and tasted of salt and old stone, and the lamp made the walls move. At the back, where the water pooled black and still, something waited.

"You came," said the sea, from the pool.

"I came for his voice." Sela set the lamp down. Her hands were shaking, so she gripped the rope to steady them. "His last words. He was calling for me, wasn't he? I heard him call. Then the water."

"He called," the sea agreed. "I have it still. Caught in a shell, whole and clean. I will give it to you."

"For what price?"

"For a cloak," said the sea. "A cloak woven of everything you remember of him. Every thread a memory. When it is done, I will wear it, and you will have his voice."

Sela stared into the black pool. "That's a cruel price."

"It is a fair one," said the sea. "You give me what you have of him. I give you what I have of him. Nothing is lost. Only moved."

The lamp flame leaned, as if a door had opened somewhere. Sela thought of Jori laughing on the harbour wall, Jori with his hair full of salt, Jori singing flat and unashamed. She thought of the cloth on her loom, dead and cold under her hands.

"And if I weave it," she said slowly, "I keep nothing?"

"You keep the shell," said the sea. "And the shell keeps the voice. Is that not enough?"

It was not enough. She knew it was not enough. But the alternative was the flat grey cloth and a house where nobody sang.

"All right," she said. The word came out small. "I'll weave it."

The pool rippled once, and was still. Somewhere deep in the cave, water dripped, and dripped, and the sea said nothing more, as if the bargain were already old.

The loom stood in the corner of the kitchen where the stove used to warm it, and every night after the harbour lights went out Sela sat down and began to weave. The first thread was grey. She pulled it from the bundle the sea had left at the cave mouth, cold and slick as kelp, and when it crossed the warp she heard Jori laugh.

It was a real laugh, the one he did at his own bad jokes, half a snort and half a cough, and it came from the threads themselves. Sela stopped breathing. Then she wove the next thread, and the laugh went quiet, and she could not find it again. She sat very still in the dark kitchen, her hands shaking on the shuttle.

"That's one," she said to the empty room. Her voice sounded strange, flat as a struck pot. "That's one gone."

By the third night she had woven in his laugh, the way he said her name when he wanted something, and the smell of him coming in from the boats, tar and cold and fish. She knew she had given them, the way you know you have spent coins. But when she reached for the laugh to hear it once more, there was only a smooth blank place, like a shelf where a cup used to stand.

Her mother came down at midnight with a candle. "Still at it?" she asked. "You'll wear your eyes out."

"I'm nearly done," said Sela.

"You've been saying that four nights." Her mother set the candle on the sill and looked at the cloth. It was beautiful now, a deep moving grey with silver in it, and it shifted when you looked away. "What is it for?"

"A cloak," said Sela. "For the sea."

Her mother was quiet a long while. "And what's the sea giving you?"

Sela did not answer, because she could not remember. She knew there was a reason she sat here each night until her fingers cramped. The reason had something to do with the boy who used to live in this house. But it was like a word on the tip of her tongue that would not come. She kept weaving. It seemed the only thing to do.

The fifth night she wove his face. She did not mean to; her hands knew the pattern better than she did. The shuttle went over and under and there he was in the weave, one eye and then the other, the crooked nose he broke at nine, the mouth that was always about to grin. Sela looked at it and felt nothing at all, and that frightened her more than grief had.

"Jori," she said, trying the name. It was only a sound. A gull's cry, a door banging. "Jori."

She wept then, hard, with her forehead on the loom, and could not have told anyone why she was weeping. The cloth drank her tears and went on moving, silver-grey, patient, hungry.

***

On the seventh night there was nothing left to weave in, and the cloak was finished. Sela carried it down the cliff path before dawn, and it was so light she kept checking it was still in her arms. The tide was out and the cave mouth stood open like a mouth, and the black pool inside it lay flat and waiting.

"I've brought it," she said.

The water did not move, but a voice came out of it, patient and slow. "Lay it on the pool."

She laid the cloak on the black water and it did not sink. It spread out, grey on black, and then it began to dissolve, thread by thread, into the pool, and the pool took it the way sand takes a wave. When the last of it was gone, something knocked against the stone at Sela's feet.

A shell. A big one, curled and pale, small enough to cup in two hands. She picked it up. It was warm.

"His voice," said the sea. "As promised. What he said last, before the water took him. Hold it to your ear."

Sela's heart was going like a bird in a box. She had waited seven nights for this; she had given everything for this. She lifted the shell to her ear.

And heard the sea.

Not Jori. Not her brother's flat, unashamed singing, not the laugh she had spent first. Something vast and cold and old poured out of the shell and into her head, a voice like a tide coming in over stones, like a hundred drowned bells, like the noise the world made before there were people in it. It was beautiful, and it was terrible, and it did not know her name.

She tore the shell from her ear. Her breath came in gasps.

"That's not him," she said. "That isn't Jori."

The pool lay still. Then it said, in that same patient voice, "It is what I have of him. I told you. Nothing is lost. Only moved."

"That's your voice." Sela's hands were shaking so badly the shell rattled. "That's your voice in a shell, and you've taken everything I had, and you've given me…" She could not finish. She looked at the shell, warm and pale in her hands, and she could not remember what her brother's voice had sounded like. She could not remember his face. There was only the sea-noise, and the flat grey morning, and the cold.

"You cheated me," she whispered.

"I bargained," said the sea. "You did not ask what I would give. You only asked that I give."

The pool was black and flat and gave back nothing, and Sela stood at its edge with a shell full of strangers in her hands.

Sela did not go home. Home was the cottage with Jori's boots by the door and his mug on the shelf, and she could not remember his face to put with them. She sat on the wet stone until the tide turned and the pool began to fill, and the sea spoke no more. It had no need to. It had what it wanted.

The shell lay in her lap, and when she lifted it the voice inside murmured and shifted like water in a drain. It knew things she did not. It knew the shape of the cove at midnight and the weight of a drowned boat and the long slow count of years. It did not know Jori. It had never known Jori.

"You cheated me," she said again, to the pool, to the climbing water. "You said nothing is lost."

A wave broke over the rim and slid back, and in the sound she heard the sea's patient answer: *Nothing is lost. Only moved. You moved it into me.*

The cloak. Her memories were in the cloak, woven thread by thread into the current, and the sea wore them now somewhere out past the headland. If the cloak still existed, the memories still existed. She stared at the dark water and understood what she would have to do.

"You'll drown me," she said.

*Perhaps,* said the sea, in the voice of the wave. *You are not the first to ask.*

Sela stood. Her legs were stiff with cold, and the shell was heavy in her fist. She could keep it. She could carry the false voice home and learn to call it Jori's, and after a year or two she would forget it was a lie, and the sea would have her memories and her brother both. That was the bargain it had offered.

Or she could go into the water after what was hers.

She set the shell down on the stone, gently, as if it might hear her. "I don't want your voice," she told it. "I want mine back."

Then she walked into the surf, and the cold took her breath, and she dived.

The water was green and then black, and the cloak was there, drifting in the current like a great slow weed. It was beautiful, she saw. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever woven. Every thread of it was a memory of Jori, and the sea had knotted them into a shape that moved with the tide.

She caught the hem and pulled. The first thread came loose, and something came back to her: Jori at seven, laughing so hard at his own joke that he had to hold the table. She pulled another, and there was his face, sunburnt and squinting. Another, and his voice saying her name. The sea tugged at the cloak, wanting it back, and Sela held on and pulled, and pulled, and the memories came loose one by one and rushed into her like air into a diver.

Her lungs burned. The cloak was coming apart in her hands, and the sea was roaring, and she did not care. She pulled the last thread free, and the whole thing went to nothing, and she kicked for the light.

***

Sela came up into the grey morning with her brother's name in her mouth. "Jori," she gasped. "Jori, Jori." She had his laugh. She had his face. She had the way he said her name, the exact way, and it was hers again and no one else's.

On the rock, the shell was silent.

She climbed out and picked it up, and it was only a shell now, empty and cold and ordinary. The sea had given her nothing of Jori's voice, and it never would. That was gone into the water, and the water did not give things back. She turned the shell over and over in her hands and found that she could bear it. She had the rest of him. She had enough.

"You cheated me," she said to the sea, but there was no anger left in it. "And I cheated you back."

The tide was going out. Sela walked up the cliff path with the empty shell in her pocket and her memories warm in her chest, and she did not go home to mourn. She went to the loom.

All day she wove, and it was not a mourning cloth. She wove the sea's own song into it: the green of the shallows, the white of the breaking wave, the long grey swell beyond the headland, the small bright flash of a fish turning. She wove the sound the tide made against the cave, and the taste of salt, and the way the light came up over the water in the morning. It was not Jori. It was the sea that had taken him and given him back to her, and she could hold both things at once now.

When it was finished she carried the cloth down to the cliff and hung it on the drying line, and the wind took it and filled it, and it moved like water. The sea below went on with its patient work, and Sela stood and watched it, and the shell in her pocket said nothing at all.

"That's all right," she told it. "I know his voice. I don't need yours."
