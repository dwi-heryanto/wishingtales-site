Sedge kept her holt under the roots of the old alder, where the weir's white noise drowned out the world. The river here ran slow and brown, thick with the smell of silt and the small silver fish that came to spawn in the shallows. She had lived in this bank for eleven seasons, longer than any otter in the valley, and she knew every stone of it the way she knew the ache in her left shoulder.

Her daughter Moss came downriver every few days, swimming strong against the current, her fur sleek and dark. Today she hauled herself onto the bank with a fat trout in her jaws and dropped it at Sedge's feet.

"You're thinner," Moss said, shaking water from her whiskers. "The fish aren't running as high here. Come upstream with me. The holt by the bend is empty, and my young ones would learn from you."

Sedge nosed the trout but did not eat. "I know this stretch," she said. "I know where the bank caves in and where the current pulls. Upstream is someone else's water."

"It's ours. It's always been ours." Moss's voice tightened. "You're not a rock, Mother. You can move."

A pair of swallows dipped low over the water, and Sedge watched them instead of answering. When she finally spoke, her voice was quiet. "Your father and I raised you here. I can still smell him on the wind some evenings." She turned back to the trout. "Go home, Moss. I'll be fine."

Moss stayed until dusk, then slipped back into the river with a flick of her tail. Sedge ate the trout alone and curled in her holt, listening to the weir.

***

The storm came three nights later, without warning. Sedge woke to rain hammering the alder leaves and the river already high, licking at the mouth of her holt. She scrambled out onto the bank and felt the ground tremble under her paws.

Above the weir, the water had turned the colour of wet slate. It roared over the stones in a way she had not heard in years, carrying branches and torn grass. The upper holt, where Moss's young ones stayed, sat on a shelf of rock just above the bend.

A sound cut through the rain: thin, high cries. Sedge's fur stood on end. She knew that sound. Pups in the water.

She plunged in without thinking, the cold hitting her like a wall. The current grabbed her and swung her downstream, and she fought it with every stroke, angling toward the bend. Through the spray she saw them: three small dark shapes tumbling in the flood, their heads popping up and under.

"Hold on!" she shouted, though they could not hear her. "Swim toward the bank!"

The nearest pup, a tiny female, was caught against a half-submerged log. Sedge reached her and clamped her jaws gently around the scruff, hauling her up onto the log. The pup clung there, shivering.

The second was further out, spinning in an eddy. Sedge dived, came up beneath it, and shoved it toward the log with her shoulder. It scrambled up beside its sister, eyes wide.

Then she heard the third cry, weaker now, from somewhere under the churning water near the root. And beyond it, the roar of the weir grew louder, a deep grinding thunder that shook the stones.

Sedge turned in the current, her breath coming hard, and looked back at the two pups on the log. They were safe for now, but the water was still rising.

The log shuddered. Sedge swung her head around. The two pups were pressed flat against the bark, claws dug in, and the log was turning slowly in the current, drifting toward the weir.

"Climb higher!" she shouted. "Get to the top!"

The little female scrambled up the sloping trunk, and her brother followed, both of them slick and trembling. Sedge turned back to the flood. The third pup cried again, a thin, bubbling wail that came from beneath the root tangle on the far side. She could see nothing but churning brown water and the white bones of broken branches.

She pushed off from the log and swam. The current hit her like a shoulder, shoving her sideways, and she had to fight for every length. Her hind legs burned. The root loomed closer, a dark mat of twisted wood and debris, and the water screamed through it. Somewhere under there, a small otter was trapped.

"Hold on!" Sedge called, though she did not know if the pup could hear her. "I'm coming!"

A branch caught her flank and spun her. She righted herself, gasped air, and drove forward again. The root was close now, close enough to see the gap where the water poured through, and the small dark shape pinned against the wood, struggling. The pup's head broke the surface for a heartbeat, mouth open, then went under again.

Sedge lunged. Her forepaws caught the root, and she hauled herself up, but the current tore at her and she could not get a grip. The pup was just beyond her reach, wedged in a notch where two branches met. If she let go, the flood would take her.

Behind her, the log gave a groan. She twisted to look. The two pups were still clinging, but the log had swung broadside to the current and was moving faster now, toward the roar of the weir.

She could not reach the third pup and get back to the log in time. She could not do both.

***

Sedge made her choice.

She let go of the root and let the current take her, but she did not swim for the third pup. Instead she kicked hard, angling across the stream, and threw herself against the root tangle where it met the bank. The wood was jammed there, a natural barrier, but the gaps were wide enough for a small otter to slip through and be lost. She wedged herself into the widest gap, back against the bank, forepaws braced on the branches, and made herself a wall.

"Swim!" she roared at the two pups on the log. "Now! Swim to me!"

The little female lifted her head. Sedge saw her hesitate, saw her look at the churning water between them.

"You can do it!" Sedge called. "Kick hard and aim for my voice!"

The pup slid off the log and into the flood. For a moment she was just a dark smudge in the brown water, then her head broke the surface and she was swimming, awkward and desperate, claws scrabbling. The current pushed her sideways, but Sedge held her place and kept calling, and the pup came on, nearer, nearer, until she reached the root and scrambled up over Sedge's back and onto the bank. Her brother followed, slower, nearly swept past, but Sedge lunged and caught his scruff in her teeth and hauled him over.

Both pups huddled on the grass, gasping. Sedge turned back to the water. The third pup's cry came again, weaker, from under the root. She could see it now, a small shape trapped in a pocket of branches below the surface, its head barely above water.

"Stay there!" she told the two on the bank. "Don't move!"

She took a breath and dived.

The water was colder than she remembered, and darker. Sedge opened her eyes and the flood pressed against them like a hand. The root was a tangle of black fingers, and somewhere in that tangle a small otter was crying. She followed the sound, letting the current carry her down and sideways until her shoulder bumped wood. There—a pale smudge of fur, a head barely clear of the water, one forepaw hooked over a branch. The pup's eyes were wide and fixed on her.

"I've got you," Sedge said, though her mouth filled with water and the words came out as bubbles. She gripped the branch with her teeth and pulled. It shifted, but the pup's hindquarters were wedged tight between two roots. Sedge braced her back feet against the trunk and heaved. Nothing. The current shoved her sideways, and she lost her grip, tumbling until she found the branch again. Her lungs were beginning to burn.

Above her, muffled, she heard the two pups on the bank calling. Their voices were thin and frightened. Sedge surfaced for one quick breath and saw them standing at the water's edge, their small bodies pressed together. "Stay back!" she shouted. Then she dived again.

This time she went at the roots from the side, digging her claws into the soft wood and tearing. Splinters clouded the water. The pup whimpered, a high, thin sound that went through her like a hook. She tore at the branch until it cracked and gave way, and the pup slid free—but the current caught it instantly and swept it down.

Sedge lunged. Her teeth closed on the pup's tail, and she hauled it back against her chest, then kicked hard for the surface. They broke through together, both gasping. The bank was close, but the water between them was a churning brown slope. Sedge turned on her back and let the pup scramble onto her belly, then she paddled with her forepaws, fighting the current inch by inch.

A voice came from the bank. "Sedge! Here!" It was Moss, standing knee-deep in the shallows with a long branch held out. Sedge grabbed it with her teeth, and Moss pulled them in, hand over hand, until Sedge's back scraped mud and the pup tumbled onto the grass.

***

All three pups were on the bank now, shivering in a heap. Sedge dragged herself out last and lay flat, her sides heaving. The rain had softened to a drizzle. Above them, the sky was the color of wet slate, but a pale line showed in the east.

Moss knelt beside her. "You're bleeding."

Sedge looked down. A gash along her foreleg welled red, and one ear was torn. "It's nothing," she said. "The pups—"

"They're fine. All three." Moss's voice was tight. "You could have drowned. You're too old for that swim, Mother."

Sedge didn't answer. She watched the river. It was still high and fast, but the roar had dropped, and the water was beginning to show patches of clear brown instead of black. The flood was passing. Already the root where she had wedged herself was a dark shape above the surface, and the bank where they lay was widening as the water drew back.

One of the pups crept close and pressed its nose against her flank. "Is it over?" it whispered.

"It's over," Sedge said. She licked the pup's wet head. "You did well. All of you."

Moss sat back on her heels. "Come upstream with us," she said. "The holt above the bend is dry. There's room. The pups need someone who knows the water."

Sedge looked at her stretch of river—the weir, the old log, the bank where she had lived alone for four years. The water was already falling away from it, leaving a line of silt and debris. It looked smaller than she remembered.

"I've been here a long time," she said.

"I know." Moss's voice was gentle. "But you don't have to be."

The third pup, the one she had pulled from under the root, climbed onto her back and settled there, a warm weight. Sedge felt its small heart beating against her spine.

She got to her feet slowly. The gash on her leg throbbed, but it held her. "All right," she said. "Help me get them up the bank."

They went together, the three pups stumbling and chattering, Moss leading the way, and Sedge bringing up the rear. Behind them the river sank back into its bed, muttering, and the first light came gray and clean over the water.
