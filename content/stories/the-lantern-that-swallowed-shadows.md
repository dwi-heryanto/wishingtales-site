The attic smelled of cedar and old rain. Elara climbed the last step and stopped, letting her eyes adjust to the grey light that leaked through a single dusty window. Boxes leaned against each other like tired people. A dressmaker's dummy wore a hat with no crown. Somewhere behind her, the ladder creaked as her grandmother called up from the kitchen.

"Don't move the blue trunk. The lid's cracked and everything in it is older than you and twice as stubborn."

"I won't," Elara called back. She stepped over the trunk anyway.

At the far end, under the slope of the roof, sat a wooden crate with a rope handle. Elara tugged it out. Inside, wrapped in a cloth gone soft as flour, was a lantern. Not glass and brass like the one on the porch. This one was iron, dark and pitted, with a small door on one side and no chimney at all. When she lifted it, the shadows in the corner of the attic did not stay where they were.

They leaned toward it. That was the only word she had. The dark under the eaves thinned and stretched, like taffy pulled toward a hand. Elara held the lantern up and turned it slowly. The shadows followed, sliding across the floorboards, gathering at the base of the iron like water finding a drain.

She felt no wind. The window was shut. Dust hung in the air, perfectly still.

"Gran?" she said, and her voice came out smaller than she meant it to.

Footsteps on the ladder, slow and careful. Her grandmother's head appeared through the hatch, grey hair pinned back, one hand on the frame. She looked at the lantern in Elara's grip and her face did something Elara had never seen it do. It went still, the way a pond goes still before a stone hits it.

"Where did you find that?"

"In the crate. What is it?"

Her grandmother climbed the rest of the way and stood, brushing dust from her knees. She did not reach for the lantern. She kept a step of distance between herself and it, and that distance was the strangest thing of all, because Elara had never known her grandmother to be afraid of anything.

"It's a shadow lantern," she said. "My mother had it. Her mother before that." She wiped her hands on her apron. "We don't use it. Put it back."

"But what does it do?"

"It keeps things." Her grandmother turned toward the ladder. "Come down. I've got soup on and it won't stir itself."

Elara put the lantern back in the crate, cloth and all. But on her way past, she let her fingers brush the iron door, and the shadows in the attic shivered, and the air went cold against her palm.

***

The soup was barley and carrot, and her grandmother ate hers standing at the counter, which was how Elara knew something was wrong. She always sat.

"You're not eating," Elara said.

"I'm thinking." Her grandmother set down her spoon. "You want to know about the lantern. I can see it on you like a stain."

Elara said nothing. Outside, the wind pressed against the kitchen window and the porch light swung on its hook.

"Sit down, then." Her grandmother pulled out the chair across from her and lowered herself into it, slowly, one hand flat on the table. "The lantern holds a shadow. Not the dark a thing casts at noon. The part of a person that walks behind them. If you put a living shadow in there, it stays. It keeps."

"Keeps what?"

"A piece of them. The way they laughed. The way they stood. The way they said your name." She turned her bowl a quarter turn, then back. "My mother put her sister's shadow in it, the winter the fever came. Her sister died in the spring. But my mother had her voice in that lantern for forty years. She'd open the little door and hear her sister singing, and she'd weep, and then she'd close it and go on with her day."

Elara looked at her hands. "That's horrible."

"It's both things." Her grandmother's voice was quiet. "Don't mistake me. It's a mercy and it's a hunger. The lantern takes more than it gives. My mother got thinner every year, and the shadow in the glass got fainter, and at the end they were both almost nothing."

"So why keep it?"

"Because you don't throw away the only picture of a face you loved." Her grandmother looked up, and her eyes were bright and tired. "You keep it in the attic where it can't do harm, and you tell your granddaughter to put it back."

Elara stood and carried her bowl to the sink. The porch light swung once more, and she watched the porch's shadow lean, just slightly, toward the house, toward the attic, toward the iron crate and the dark thing waiting in it.

"Gran," she said. "I put it back."

"I know you did." Her grandmother's spoon clinked against the bowl. "That's not what worries me."

Her grandmother fell on a Thursday, in the garden, between the beans and the fence. Elara found her there with the watering can still upright in her hand, its spout dribbling into the dirt, and for one long second she thought the old woman was only resting. Then the hand let go of the can and the metal rang on the path.

The ambulance came. The neighbors came. A woman from the church brought a casserole in a dish Elara would have to remember to return. By Sunday the house had gone quiet again, and the quiet had a shape to it now, the shape of a bed with someone small in it and a chair beside it that Elara sat in until her legs went numb.

The doctor used careful words. Her grandmother used none at all. She slept most of the afternoon, and when she woke she asked for water, and then for the window open, and then she looked at Elara for a while without saying anything.

"You've got that face on," she said at last.

"What face?"

"The one like you're doing sums." Her grandmother's voice was thin as paper. "Put the sums down, girl. They don't add."

Elara sat with her hand in her grandmother's hand and did the sums anyway. The nights got longer. The lamp on the nightstand burned lower. And up above them, in the attic, in the iron crate, the dark thing waited with its patient appetite and its small trapped light that had once been a woman's whole face.

It could hold her. Elara had seen it hold. A shadow in the glass would be a piece of her grandmother that the illness could not touch, could not thin, could not take. She would still have the shape of her, the way her shoulders set when she was stubborn, the way her head tilted when she listened.

On Tuesday night Elara climbed the ladder. The crate opened at her touch now, as if it knew her. The lantern's glass was cool, and the wick inside had grown a small blue bud of flame that had not been there before.

"Please," she whispered, and did not know if she was speaking to the lantern or to the sleeping woman below.

She carried it down in a blanket. Her grandmother's shadow lay across the quilt, thin and wavering, and Elara lifted the lantern and let the flame lean toward it. The shadow did not pull away. It came like something tired coming home, and the glass drank it, and the room got brighter by exactly the amount of dark that had left it.

***

Her grandmother woke at dawn and said she felt lighter. She ate half a piece of toast and asked about the beans. Elara laughed, and it came out wrong, too loud, and she covered her mouth.

For three days it was almost good. The old woman sat up. She told a story about a dog she'd had at nine. She held Elara's hand and hummed something tuneless and old, and Elara told herself the sums had added after all.

On the fourth morning she took the lantern down to look at it, the way she had gotten into the habit of doing, and the shadow inside had gone faint. Not gone. Faint, like a photograph left in a window. Its edges had blurred. The stubborn set of the shoulders was only a suggestion now.

She carried it to the kitchen and set it on the table and stared at it until her grandmother's voice came from the doorway.

"So you didn't put it back."

Elara turned. The old woman stood braced against the frame, and her shadow on the floorboards was so pale it was almost not there. It moved when she moved, but slowly, a half-beat behind, like an echo.

"Gran, I can fix it. I'll put it back, it'll—"

"Look at me." Her grandmother crossed the room one careful step at a time and put her hand flat on the glass. The shadow inside drifted toward her palm and pressed against it. "It's still eating," she said quietly. "It never stopped. It just had more to eat."

"No. It's keeping you. You got better."

"I got lighter." Her grandmother's mouth bent. "There's a difference, and I know it in my bones. Every day the glass takes a little, and I give it a little, and one morning there won't be a little left to give."

Elara looked at the shadow in the lantern, blurred and thinning, and then at the shadow on the floor, pale as watered milk, and understood that she had not saved anything. She had only moved the hunger somewhere it could feed more easily.

"I did this," she said.

"You did." Her grandmother's hand found her shoulder. "And now you'll undo it, and we'll both have to live with the undoing."

Elara set the lantern on the kitchen table between them. Its glass was warm, though no flame burned inside. The shadow of her grandmother lay curled at the bottom like a sleeping fish, thin as smoke and just as restless.

"Tell me what happens if I open it," Elara said.

Her grandmother lowered herself into the chair, moving like a woman made of glass herself. "The shadow goes back where it belongs. But it's been fed on me for weeks, Elara. It's grown used to the taste. When it comes home, it'll take what's left of me with it."

"How long?"

"A night. Maybe less." Her grandmother's eyes were steady. "And if you don't open it, the lantern keeps drinking. I'll go slower, but I'll still go. And the shadow will stay in there, fading, until there's nothing left of me in it either."

Elara pressed her palms flat on the table. The wood was cool and solid, and she hated it for being so ordinary when everything else had come apart. "There has to be another way. A third door."

"There isn't one. I've looked." Her grandmother reached across and turned the lantern so the latch faced Elara. "This is the choice, and it's yours to make. I won't make it for you."

"That's not fair."

"No," her grandmother agreed. "It isn't."

Elara stood and walked to the window. Outside, the street was going about its evening business, a dog barking, a door slamming, someone laughing two houses down. All of it ordinary and oblivious. She wanted to run out into it and never come back to this room and this table and this lantern.

Instead she turned around. "If I open it, you'll die tonight."

"Yes."

"And if I don't, you'll die anyway, just slower, and the whole time the lantern will be eating you."

"Yes."

Elara's throat closed. She thought of the attic, the dust, the little glass box that had seemed like a gift. She had been so sure she was clever. "I wanted to keep you," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle.

"I know." Her grandmother held out her hand. "Come here."

Elara crossed the kitchen and took it. The hand was light as paper and warm as bread.

"I'm not afraid," her grandmother said. "I've had a long life and a good one, and I've got a granddaughter who loves me enough to do the hard thing. That's more than most people get."

"I don't feel brave."

"Brave is what you do, not what you feel." Her grandmother squeezed her fingers. "Open it."

***

Elara carried the lantern to her grandmother's bedroom and set it on the nightstand. The old woman lay propped against her pillows, hands folded, wearing the blue cardigan she always wore when she was cold. The room smelled of lavender and clean linen.

"You don't have to watch," her grandmother said.

"I'm not leaving."

"I didn't think you would." Her grandmother smiled. "Go on, then. Before I lose my nerve and start telling you what to do with my recipe box."

Elara's hands trembled on the latch. She took a breath, held it, and lifted the little brass hook. The glass door swung open with a sound like a sigh.

For a moment nothing happened. Then the shadow inside stirred, uncurled, and poured out of the lantern in a slow black ribbon. It crossed the space between the nightstand and the bed and settled over her grandmother like a second skin. The old woman gasped, her back arching, and Elara nearly slammed the door shut again, nearly reached in and pulled the shadow back and kept it, kept her, kept everything.

But she didn't. She stood with her fists at her sides and let it happen.

The shadow sank into her grandmother, and the old woman's body relaxed. Her breathing slowed. Her eyes, when they opened, were clear and bright, brighter than they had been in weeks.

"There," she said. "That's better."

"Gran—"

"Hush. Sit with me." Her grandmother patted the bed, and Elara sat, and the old woman took her hand again. "It's quiet now. The hunger's gone. I'd forgotten what quiet felt like."

They sat while the light in the window went from gold to grey to the deep blue of evening. Her grandmother talked, and Elara listened, and neither of them mentioned the lantern on the nightstand, empty now, its glass cold and clear.

"You'll be all right," her grandmother said at last. "You're the kind who figures things out. You'll make mistakes, and you'll fix them, and you'll be all right."

"I don't want to be all right without you."

"Nobody ever does." Her grandmother's hand tightened once, then loosened. "But you will be. That's the whole point of you."

Outside, the first star came out. Elara watched it through the window and held her grandmother's hand until the hand went cool and still and the room was very quiet. Then she got up, closed the window, and sat back down. She stayed until morning.
