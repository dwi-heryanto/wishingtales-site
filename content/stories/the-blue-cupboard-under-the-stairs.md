The house smelled like cedar and old rain. Mina stood in the hallway with her suitcase handle still warm in her hand. Boxes were stacked in the front room, each one labeled in her mother's blocky handwriting. Nothing had her name on it yet.

"Your room's the small one at the top of the stairs," Mom said. She was already unwrapping a lamp from newspaper. "Grandpa slept there when he was a boy."

Mina climbed the stairs slowly. The steps creaked in a pattern, like someone counting. Her room had a slanted ceiling and a window that looked out at the apple tree. She set her suitcase on the bed and felt like a guest who had arrived too early.

Downstairs, she found a cupboard tucked under the staircase. It was painted a deep blue, the color of a lake at dusk. The paint was chipped along the edges, and a small brass keyhole sat above the handle.

She tried the handle. It didn't move.

"That's been locked since I was a girl," Mom called from the kitchen. "I don't think there's even a key anymore."

Mina pressed her palm flat against the blue wood. It was cool, and she thought she felt something shift, like a breath held and let go. She pulled her hand back. The cupboard stayed shut.

That night, she lay in Grandpa's old bed and listened to the house. The pipes ticked. A floorboard sighed. Every sound felt like a language she didn't speak yet.

***

Mina woke to a click. It was small and precise, like a fingernail tapping glass. Moonlight lay in a stripe across the floor, and she followed it down the stairs without turning on a light.

The cupboard door stood open. Just a crack, but open.

She knelt on the hallway rug and pulled it wider. Inside, the shelves were lined with small objects: a thimble, a bird's egg, a folded map, a single earring. Each one had a paper tag tied on with string. Mina leaned closer.

"Grandpa, age six," she read on a little clay horse. "Grandma, age twelve," on a silver button. "Aunt Rosa, age four," on a red marble.

Her fingers moved along the shelf until they found a smooth grey stone. It was the size of a plum and fit perfectly in her palm. The tag read: "Grandpa, age nine."

She turned the stone over. It was warm, as if someone had been holding it a moment before. The cupboard clicked again, soft but unmistakable.

"Mina?" Her mother's voice came from the top of the stairs. "What are you doing down there?"

Mina closed her fingers around the stone. "Nothing," she said. "Just looking."

The cupboard door swung shut without a sound. The keyhole glinted in the dark.

Mina found her mother in the kitchen, unpacking plates from a box. Newspaper rustled. The kettle was starting to hum.

"Mom, did Grandpa ever keep things in the blue cupboard under the stairs?"

Her mother set down a plate and thought about it. "When I was your age, that cupboard was always locked. I used to rattle the handle just to hear it."

"Was there a key?"

"If there was, I never saw it." Mom smiled a little. "But Grandpa told me stories about it. He said it was a remembering cupboard. He said every family needs one place for the things that are too small to keep and too big to throw away."

Mina's hand went to her pocket. The stone sat there, round and warm.

"Did you believe him?" she asked.

"I wanted to." Mom folded the newspaper flat. "I asked him once what was inside, and he said, 'Ask it nicely when you're ready.' I never figured out what he meant."

The kettle clicked off. Steam climbed toward the ceiling.

"Why do you ask?" said Mom.

"No reason," Mina said, and went upstairs to bed, and lay awake for a long time with her hand closed around the stone.

***

In the morning Mina went straight to the cupboard. The house was quiet. Her brother was still asleep, and the boxes in the hall stood where they had stood all week.

She knelt and set the stone on the rug. "I brought it back," she told the door. "It's yours."

The cupboard did not move.

Mina tried the handle. She tried pushing, then pulling. She slid the stone along the crack at the bottom. Nothing clicked. The wood was cool now, and the keyhole was only a dark little hole.

"Come on," she said. "I'm giving it back."

She sat down hard on the rug. Maybe she had broken it. Maybe the cupboard only opened at night, or only once, or only for Grandpa.

Then she remembered what her mother had said. Ask it nicely when you're ready.

Mina stopped pulling. She put her hands in her lap and looked at the cupboard instead of fighting it. It was old blue paint, worn pale at the corners. Someone had painted it carefully a very long time ago.

She waited. Her knees went stiff. The clock in the hall ticked.

Click.

The door swung open a finger's width. Mina breathed out slowly, and the cupboard opened the rest of the way, as if it had only been waiting for her to be quiet.

Mina got up on her knees. The cupboard stood open, dark and patient, and the little shelves inside were lined with soft cloth. She found the empty hook at the front where the stone had hung. The tag still swung there, "Grandpa, age nine," in careful pencil.

She set the stone on the shelf and looped the string back over its hook. "There," she whispered. "Home again."

The stone sat where it belonged. For a moment nothing happened. Then a small drawer underneath the bottom shelf slid out by itself, smooth as a breath. Inside lay one tag, blank and new, with a short piece of string already tied.

Mina picked it up. The paper was clean and pale, waiting. She turned it over twice, then found the stub of pencil Grandpa had left in the corner of the shelf.

"Mina," she wrote, and underneath, "age nine."

Her hands were steady. She looked around the room for something small, something that was hers and not the house's. Her pocket held a green glass marble from the jar in her new bedroom, the one she had chosen first. She hooked the tag through the marble's string and hung it on the empty hook beside Grandpa's stone.

The cupboard gave a soft click, like a door shutting gently somewhere far off. The drawer slid closed. The door swung to, but it did not lock. Mina put her palm flat on the blue paint and felt it warm, the way a stone feels after the sun has been on it.

The rug was still rumpled where she had sat. She smoothed it down with her foot and went to find her brother.

***

Theo was in the hallway, sliding in his socks. He stopped when he saw her face. "What?" he said.

"Come here," Mina said. "Quietly. You have to be quiet."

She led him to the cupboard under the stairs. He crouched beside her, all knees and elbows, and peered at the blue door. "It's just a cupboard," he said.

"Put your hand on it," Mina told him. "And don't pull. Just listen."

Theo put his hand on the paint. He was never still, not for anything, but he went still now, his head tilted. The hall clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs a window rattled in its frame.

Click.

The door swung open a finger's width. Theo gasped and grabbed Mina's sleeve. "It opened! Did you see? It opened by itself!"

"I saw," Mina said. She showed him the shelves, the tagged things, the stone with Grandpa's name. "These are ours now," she said. "Yours and mine. But you have to listen to it. It doesn't like being yanked."

Theo nodded slowly, serious for once. He touched the green marble on its hook. "That's yours," he said.

"Yes," Mina said. "And there's room for yours."

Down the hall, Mom called that dinner was ready. The two of them got up, and Mina closed the cupboard door with two fingers, and it shut soft as a book. Walking to the kitchen, she did not feel like a guest anymore. The floorboards creaked under her feet, and they sounded like her own.
