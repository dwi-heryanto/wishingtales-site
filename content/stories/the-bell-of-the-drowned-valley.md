The rain had stopped before dawn, but the river kept rising. Mara stood at the edge of the yard with her boots sinking into mud, watching the brown water swallow the lower pasture fence post by post. Tomas came up beside her, breathing hard from the run down the hill.

"Dad's not back," he said.

"He went to move the sheep to the high field. He had time."

"The bridge is gone. I saw it from the ridge. Just the pilings sticking up."

Mara looked across the water. The far bank was a smudge of green and grey, and somewhere beyond it her father was probably doing what he always did, cursing quietly and getting on with it. But the river had already taken the low road, and if it kept climbing it would take the village too. Once, the old people said, a bell had warned them. The chapel bell, in the valley that the dam had drowned a hundred years ago. It had rung the night the water came, and then it had never rung again.

"The chapel's still up," Mara said. "The tower, anyway. I've seen it from the hill when the water's low."

"You want to go out there?" Tomas's voice cracked. "In that?"

"Someone has to warn everyone. The miller, the school, everyone downriver. Nobody knows how fast it's coming."

They took the flat-bottomed boat from the shed. Mara rowed first, because her arms were steadier, and Tomas sat in the bow with a lantern that was useless in the daylight but made him feel better. The current shoved them sideways, and twice they spun, and once a whole tree went past them end over end, roots streaming like hair.

The chapel rose out of the water ahead. Only the top of the stone wall showed, and the tower, square and grey, with a dark arch at its base. Water lapped at the sill of the arch. Tomas shipped the oars and they drifted in, ducking low.

Inside, it was cold. The floor was gone, replaced by black water that smelled of old stone and weeds. The bell hung high above them in the tower, green with verdigris, its rope dangling down and ending just above the waterline. It did not move. The air was so still that Mara could hear her own heartbeat.

"It's dead," Tomas whispered. "It's just a dead bell."

"No," said a voice from the dark corner. "It's waiting."

The man who stepped out of the shadow was thin, and his clothes were the colour of river silt. Mara could see the wall through him, a little, where the light came in. He wore a rope over his shoulder, the way bellringers did.

"You're the ringer," Mara said. Her mouth was dry. "The one from the old stories."

"I rang it the night the valley drowned. Everyone got out but me. I stayed to keep ringing, and the water came up the stairs." He looked at the bell. "It hasn't rung since. It won't ring for just anyone."

"What does it need?" Tomas asked.

The ghost turned his pale eyes on them. "Someone who has lost something to the water. And someone willing to give something back."

Mara felt the cold go through her, deeper than the chapel cold. She knew what she had lost. Everyone in the village knew. But she did not say it, not yet.

"Tell us," she said. "Tell us what you mean."

The ghost's voice was quiet, like water over stones. "The bell needs a price. One of you must stay here until the water goes down. A night in the cold and the dark, with me."

Tomas stepped forward, his boots sloshing on the flooded floor. "I'll do it. I'm not afraid of the dark."

Mara grabbed his arm. "No. It has to be me."

"Why?" he said, turning to her. "I'm the one who—"

"Because I'm the one who lost something to the water." Her voice came out steady, but her heart was pounding. "You know that, Tomas. You know who we lost."

Tomas's face went pale in the lantern light. "Mara, no. Not you."

"It has to be me," she said again, and this time she looked at the ghost. "Will it work? If I stay?"

The ghost studied her for a long moment, his eyes like two stones in a stream. "It will work. But the night is long, girl, and the water is still rising. I can't promise you'll want to stay till morning."

"I'll stay," she said. "I've got a reason."

Tomas started to argue, but she cut him off. "You need to go back. Row to the village and warn them. Get everyone to high ground. That's what matters now."

For a second, Tomas looked like he might refuse. Then his jaw set, and he nodded. "I'll come back for you at first light. I swear it."

"I know you will." She hugged him quickly, the cold water up to their knees. "Go."

He climbed back into the boat, and she watched him row away until the lantern was just a speck on the dark river. Then she turned to the ghost. "All right. What do I do now?"

"You wait," he said. "And you listen."

***

The water crept higher as the night went on. Mara sat on the steps of the bell tower, her feet submerged, her arms wrapped around her knees. The ghost stood beside her, not quite solid, his shape wavering whenever the lantern flickered.

"Tell me about the valley," she said, to keep her teeth from chattering. "Before the flood."

The ghost smiled, a thin, faraway thing. "It was green. The fields ran down to the river like a quilt. My father kept sheep on the north slope, and I rang the bell for church on Sundays and for warnings when the water was high. Everyone knew the sound."

"What happened the night it flooded?"

"The storm came fast. The river rose in the dark, and the bell was the only thing that could wake the village in time. I rang it for an hour, two hours. The water took the lower houses first, then the chapel. I kept ringing until it came up the stairs and I couldn't stand anymore."

Mara was quiet for a moment. "You saved them."

"I tried." He looked at her. "You lost your mother to the river, didn't you? The flood three years back."

She nodded, not trusting her voice.

"I'm sorry," he said. "The river takes what it wants. But it also gives, sometimes. You'll see."

By midnight, the water had reached the third step. Mara stood up, her legs stiff with cold. The ghost pointed to the top of the tower. "If it gets much higher, you'll need to climb."

The water reached the fourth step, then the fifth. Mara grabbed the rope and pulled herself up the spiral stairs, her wet shoes slipping on the stone. The ghost followed without climbing, his feet never touching the steps, his light casting long shadows up the curved wall. "Keep going," he said. "The bell rope is at the top. You'll have to pull hard. It hasn't been rung in a hundred years."

By the time she reached the belfry, the water was lapping at the top stair. The bell hung above her, green with age, its mouth wide and dark. The rope dangled from its wheel, stiff and frayed. Mara grabbed it with both hands and pulled. Nothing happened. She pulled again, throwing her weight backward, and the bell gave a low, rusty groan but did not swing. "Harder!" the ghost called from below. "You have to mean it!"

Mara thought of her father on the far bank with the sheep, of Tomas rowing alone in the dark, of the village sleeping under the hill. She thought of her mother, whose name she still whispered sometimes when the river was loud. She pulled with everything she had, and the bell swung. The clapper struck once, a deep, cracking note that rolled out over the water and into the night. Then again. And again.

The sound was not beautiful. It was harsh and broken, like a voice that had forgotten how to speak. But it carried. Mara heard it echo off the hills, and she knew the village would hear it too. She rang it until her arms burned and her breath came in sobs, and the water kept rising, swirling around her knees, then her waist.

Then the tower shuddered. A crack split the stone wall beside her, and a chunk of masonry fell into the water below. "Get out!" the ghost shouted. "The tower's going!" Mara dropped the rope and ran for the stairs, but the water was too high now, and the steps had crumbled. She turned back as the bell swung one last time, and the floor beneath her tilted. The tower groaned and collapsed inward, and Mara fell into the dark water, down into the flooded chapel, where the ghost's light still glowed.

***

She came up gasping in the nave, the water now up to her chest. The tower was gone above her, open to the sky, and rain fell in cold needles on her face. The ghost stood on the flooded altar, his form flickering like a candle in wind. "The bell rang," he said. "They heard it. But the chapel is filling fast. You have to get out."

"How?" Mara choked. "There's no boat. The window's too high." She was shivering hard now, her fingers numb. The ghost looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face changed, softened. "I've been waiting a hundred years for someone to ring that bell," he said. "Waiting for someone to free me. You did that. Now let me do something for you."

He raised his hands, and the water around Mara began to churn. A wave lifted her, not violently but firmly, and carried her toward the high window. She grabbed the sill as the ghost's light blazed brighter, brighter than it had all night. "Go!" he shouted, and she felt his power push her through the opening, out into the rain and the river.

She hit the water hard and went under. For a moment there was only cold and dark and the roar of the flood. Then a hand grabbed her collar and hauled her up. "Mara!" Tomas's voice was raw with fear. "I've got you!" He dragged her into the boat, and she lay there coughing, staring up at the ruined chapel as its walls slid beneath the water. The ghost's light pulsed once, twice, and then went out.

By dawn, the rain had stopped. The village had heard the bell and moved to high ground, and her father and the sheep were safe on the ridge. Mara sat wrapped in a blanket, watching the river creep back into its banks. Tomas sat beside her. "The bell's gone," he said. "The whole chapel's gone."

Mara nodded. She thought of the ghost, of his stories and his tired eyes, and she knew he was not gone. He was free. The river had taken what it wanted, and given back what it could. She reached out and took her brother's hand, and they watched the sun rise over the drowned valley together.
