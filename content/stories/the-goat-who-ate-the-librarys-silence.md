Mira stood on tiptoe at the Maple Street Library desk, holding a pinch of quiet between her fingers. It looked like a snowflake, but it felt like a yawn. "Is this enough?" she whispered.

Mrs. Pemberton, the librarian, nodded. Her glasses were so thick they made her eyes look like two curious fish. "A pinch a day keeps the hush happy," she said. "Go on, then."

The velvet box sat on the shelf behind the desk, small and purple, with a silver clasp. Mira lifted the lid. Inside, the hush lay like a sleeping cat, soft and gray and breathing very, very slowly. She dropped the pinch in. The hush sighed and curled tighter.

"Why does it need feeding?" Mira asked.

"Because silence gets hungry," said Mrs. Pemberton. "Without a pinch of quiet every morning, the hush would thin out and the library would get noisy. Then where would we be?"

Mira thought about the town square, which was always loud, and the bakery, which was louder, and her own house, which had three brothers in it. "I like it here," she said. "It's the only place I don't have to shout."

Mrs. Pemberton smiled. "That's why you're my helper. Now, the box needs to stay closed tight. No drafts, no fingers, and definitely no goats."

"Goats?" said Mira.

"We had an incident in 1974," said Mrs. Pemberton darkly. "We don't talk about it."

Mira closed the lid and clicked the clasp. The hush made a tiny snore. Outside, the morning sun was warming the sidewalk, and the library was so quiet she could hear her own heartbeat. She liked that sound. It made her feel brave, like a drum keeping time.

***

The front doors banged open so hard the doorknob left a dent in the wall. A goat the color of burnt toast galloped in, wearing a red collar with a bell that jangled like a fire alarm.

"Biscuit, no!" yelled a boy from the street, but Biscuit was already inside.

Mira jumped back. "Mrs. Pemberton!"

The goat skidded on the polished floor, legs going four different ways. Then it spotted the velvet box. Before anyone could move, Biscuit grabbed the box in his teeth, shook it like a dog with a sock, and swallowed the whole thing. Box, clasp, hush, and all.

The library exploded with noise.

Books fell off shelves. The photocopier started printing pages nobody had asked for. A globe rolled across the floor and knocked over a stack of encyclopedias. Every sound in the building seemed to get bigger, as if the walls had turned into megaphones.

"My hush!" cried Mrs. Pemberton. "He ate my hush!"

Biscuit burped. It was a small, polite burp, but it echoed off the ceiling like thunder.

Mira pressed her hands over her ears. "What do we do?" she shouted.

The boy from the street stumbled in, out of breath. "I'm so sorry!" he said. "He slipped his rope. He's been acting strange all morning."

"Strange how?" said Mrs. Pemberton.

"He ate a whole bag of dried apricots and then he got very, very fast."

Biscuit burped again. This time a tiny puff of gray floated out of his mouth and hung in the air like a smoke ring. It shimmered, then faded.

Mrs. Pemberton grabbed Mira's shoulder. "The hush is still in him," she said. "It has to come out somewhere. We need to lure it back into a new box before sunset, or the silence will be gone for good."

Mira looked at the goat. Biscuit looked back with yellow eyes and a guilty expression. Then he turned and trotted out the door, bell jangling.

"Follow him!" said Mrs. Pemberton. "And take this." She pressed a small empty jar into Mira's hands. "For the hush. And Mira? You'll have to be the one who speaks up. Goats don't listen to whispers."

Mira's stomach flipped. Speaking up was not her best thing. But the library was her quiet place, and now it was loud, and someone had to fix it. She tightened her grip on the jar and ran after the goat.

Mira caught up with Mrs. Pemberton on the library steps. The librarian was holding a tall glass of lemonade and fanning herself with a seed catalogue.

"Rule one," Mrs. Pemberton said. "The hush follows kindness. It won't be caught or grabbed. It has to want to come back."

"And rule two?" Mira asked.

"Rule two is sunset. At sunset the hush goes wild and hides in the walls for a year. Then nobody can read a book in peace until spring." She pointed down Maple Street. "Go. And mind the burps."

Mira ran. Biscuit was easy to follow, because every few steps he let out a small, glowing burp. Each one floated up like a gray soap bubble and popped with a tiny ping. A trail of them drifted down the sidewalk like breadcrumbs made of smoke.

At the corner, Mr. Okafor was watering his petunias. "Did a goat come by?" Mira called.

"He did," said Mr. Okafor. "He ate my newspaper and burped out a little cloud. Very rude. Very shiny."

Mira kept running. The burps led past the bakery, where the baker leaned out the window. "Your goat just burped at my bread," she said. "The bread went quiet for a second. It was lovely."

"Sorry!" Mira shouted. "I'm getting him back!"

The trail turned toward the town square. Mira could hear music. It was not good music. It was the sound of forty kazoos playing different songs at the same time. Her heart sank. It was the Annual Kazoo Parade, the loudest day of the year.

***

In the middle of the square stood the old wooden bandstand. On top of it, forty kazoo players marched in a circle, tooting and honking. And right in the middle, on the bandstand railing, stood Biscuit.

He was stuck. One hoof was wedged between two railings, and he was bleating so loudly that even the kazoos could not drown him out. Every bleat came with a puff of gray hush that floated up and vanished into the noise.

Mira pushed through the crowd. "Excuse me! Sorry! Goat emergency!"

She climbed the bandstand steps. The kazoo players kept marching around her, tooting. Biscuit rolled his yellow eyes and pulled at his hoof.

"Easy," Mira said. "Easy, Biscuit." Her voice came out small. The kazoos were so loud. She wanted to cover her ears and go home.

Then she remembered Mrs. Pemberton's words. Goats don't listen to whispers. She took a breath and spoke up, loud and clear. "Everybody stop! Please!"

The kazoos faltered. One by one they stopped. The square went quiet, and in the quiet, Biscuit stopped struggling.

"That's it," Mira said, softer now. "Good goat." She reached out and gently lifted his hoof free. Biscuit leaned his head against her arm and let out one last, tiny burp.

The kazoos were silent. The crowd stood still. Mira looked at Biscuit, and Biscuit looked at her. A soft gray wisp drifted out of his nose and curled around her wrist, warm as a breath on a cold window.

"He's still got some hush in him," Mira whispered. "It's just scared out of him."

She thought about the velvet box in her pocket. Empty. She thought about the hush. It didn't like noise. It didn't like grabbing. It liked quiet, and it liked kind. The idea arrived like a small bell ringing in her chest: the hush followed kindness.

Mira sat down on the bandstand boards. She patted her knee. "Come here, Biscuit."

The goat stepped close and put his chin on her shoulder. He smelled like grass and library dust. Mira hummed a song her grandmother used to sing, low and slow, about a boat on a sleepy river. She didn't sing it loud. She sang it the way you'd sing to a baby bird.

The gray wisps came back. They drifted out of Biscuit's ears and his nose and the corners of his mouth. They swirled around Mira's humming like fireflies. One wisp landed on her hand. Another curled into her hair.

The crowd watched. A kazoo player lowered his instrument.

"Keep going," said Mrs. Pemberton from the bottom of the steps. She was holding the velvet box open.

Mira kept humming. The hush gathered in a soft gray cloud above Biscuit's head. It pulsed in time with her song. Then, slowly, it flowed down into the box.

Mrs. Pemberton snapped the lid shut. "Got it," she said. "Every last pinch."

Biscuit let out a happy little sneeze.

***

On Saturday morning, the Maple Street Library opened its doors. A new sign hung by the entrance: WELCOME, BISCUIT. Below it, in smaller letters: PLEASE DO NOT EAT THE HUSH.

Inside, in the corner by the picture books, was a goat-sized reading nook. It had a rug, a water bowl, and a cushion shaped like a hay bale. Biscuit was already curled on it, chewing the corner of a board book about tractors.

"He's a library goat now," said Mrs. Pemberton. "He keeps the children company while they read. It's very calming."

Mira fed the hush its morning pinch. The gray wisp settled into the velvet box like a cat into a sunbeam.

"You spoke up yesterday," Mrs. Pemberton said. "In front of the whole square. That took courage."

Mira shrugged. "I was scared. My voice went small at first."

"Small voices still count," said Mrs. Pemberton. "And you found the loud one when you needed it."

A boy came in and asked for a book about volcanoes. Mira pointed him to the shelf. Then a girl asked for a book about goats. Mira pointed her to Biscuit.

At closing time, Mira stood by the door and watched the sunset through the window. The library was quiet. Not the hush-in-a-box quiet, but a friendly quiet, full of breathing and pages turning.

"Same time tomorrow?" asked Mrs. Pemberton.

"Same time tomorrow," said Mira. And this time, her voice came out just right.
