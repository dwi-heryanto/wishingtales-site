The rain started at dinner, soft at first, then loud as handfuls of pebbles on the window. Maya’s spoon was halfway to her mouth when the lights flickered once, twice, and went out. The whole kitchen dropped into gray. Thunder rolled across the sky like a heavy cart.

“Maya?” Leo’s voice was small from the other side of the table.

“I’m right here,” she said. “Don’t move. You’ll knock your milk over.”

A chair scraped. Their mother’s phone lit up on the counter, a little white rectangle in the dark. “Stay put, both of you,” Mom said. “I’ll check the breaker.” Her footsteps went down the hall.

Leo made a sound like a hiccup. Maya heard him slide off his chair and felt his hand find her sleeve. “It’s too dark,” he whispered. “I can’t see anything.”

“You can see me,” Maya said. “Sort of.”

“No, I can’t.”

She reached out and found his shoulder, then his hand. His fingers were cold. “Okay,” she said. “We’re going to find a flashlight. You hold on to me and we’ll go together.”

“What about dinner?”

“Dinner’s a lost cause.” She stood up, keeping his hand in hers. “Come on. Step where I step.”

They shuffled past the table. Leo’s foot bumped a chair leg and he gasped. Maya squeezed his hand. “That was just a chair. Chairs aren’t scary.”

“It sounded scary.”

“Everything sounds scary in the dark,” she said. “That’s the dark’s whole trick.”

Lightning lit the kitchen white for one second. Leo’s face was pale and his eyes were wide. Then the dark came back, thicker than before. He pressed his face into her arm and didn’t let go.

***

In the hallway, Maya ran her free hand along the wall. The pictures were there, the light switch was there, and then the little table where Mom kept her keys. No flashlight. She opened the drawer underneath. It rattled with batteries and rubber bands and takeout menus.

“Find it?” Leo asked.

“Not yet.” She dug deeper. Her fingers closed on something round and cold. “Got one.”

“A flashlight?”

“A candle.” She pulled it out. It was short and white, with a crooked wick. “It’s better than nothing.”

“But we need a flashlight.”

“We need light,” Maya said. “A candle makes light.”

They checked the kitchen drawers together. Maya kept one hand on Leo’s shoulder so he wouldn’t drift away. In the junk drawer they found a box of matches, two dead batteries, and a screwdriver. No flashlight. In the cabinet under the sink, there was a plastic bag of birthday candles, all pink and tiny.

“Those won’t last,” Leo said.

“No,” Maya agreed. “But they’re something.”

They went to the living room. The couch felt huge and dark, like a sleeping animal. Maya knelt and felt under it. Dust, a penny, a hair tie. Then her knuckles hit something metal. She pulled out a flashlight the size of her thumb, the kind that hung on a keychain.

“Is it working?” Leo asked.

She clicked it. Nothing. She clicked it again. A weak orange glow, no bigger than a coin, flickered on her palm.

“It’s a baby flashlight,” Leo said.

“It’s a light,” Maya said. “We have a candle and a baby flashlight. That’s two lights.”

Leo thought about it. “That’s almost a real light.”

“Exactly.” She stood up and held the candle out to him. “You carry this. I’ll carry the flashlight. We’ll go back to the kitchen and make a plan.”

The kitchen was the warmest room, even dark. Maya set the candle on the table and struck a match. The flame caught, small and orange, and both of them leaned in like it was a fire on a beach.

“There,” Maya said. “Now we have light.”

Leo set the baby flashlight beside it. The two glows made a little circle on the table, and the rest of the kitchen went soft and far away. Rain tapped the window. The fridge hummed, then stopped, then hummed again.

“What do we do now?” Leo asked.

“We wait,” Maya said. “And while we wait, we tell stories.”

“What kind?”

“Any kind. You start.”

Leo thought hard. “Once there was a boy who had a dog named Thunder. Thunder was scared of storms.”

“A dog scared of storms?”

“Yes. So every time it rained, the boy sat with him and told him jokes.”

“Did it work?”

“Sometimes,” Leo said. “The dog only laughed at the knock-knock ones.”

Maya laughed. The candle flame bent sideways, then stood up straight again. She told one about a cat who could talk but only ever said the word Tuesday. Leo told one about a sandwich that wanted to be a kite. The rain got louder, then softer, like it was listening.

“Your turn again,” Leo said. “Make it a good one.”

Maya looked at the candle. It had burned down to a small puddle of wax. The wick leaned over, and the flame was the size of a pea.

“Maya,” Leo said. His voice had gone thin.

“I’m here.”

“The light’s almost gone.”

“It’s still here,” she said. “Look.”

But the flame dipped, and the kitchen got darker, and Leo’s face went small and far away. He rubbed his eyes with his sleeve. Then his shoulders started to shake, and he cried the way little kids cry when they are trying not to.

Maya didn’t tell him to stop. She didn’t say it was silly. She slid her chair around the table until it bumped his.

“Hey,” she said. “Hey, Leo.”

“I want the lights on,” he said. “I want Mom.”

“I know.”

“It’s too dark.”

Maya looked around the kitchen. The baby flashlight was dead now, used up. The candle was almost gone. She had no matches left that she could find, and no more candles, and no way to make the power come back.

So she did the only thing she could think of. She reached over and took his hand.

“Squeeze,” she said. “As hard as you want.”

He squeezed. His hand was hot and small and shaking. Maya held on.

“Remember when Gran stayed over?” she said. “And the lights went out at her house?”

Leo sniffed. “She wasn’t scared.”

“She wasn’t,” Maya said. “She said dark is just the world with its eyes closed.”

The candle guttered. The flame sank low, blue at the bottom, then almost nothing. Leo’s grip tightened.

“Don’t let go,” he whispered.

“I won’t,” Maya said. And she meant it.

The candle flame leaned sideways and nearly died. Maya watched it, and in that little wobble of light she remembered something. Gran had taught them a game at her house, the night the wind took the wires down. Leo had been small then, small enough to sit in Gran’s lap. The whole house had gone dark, and Gran had said, “Now we play the listening game.”

“Leo,” Maya said. “Gran’s game. The listening one.”

He lifted his head off his arms. “What listening one?”

“You close your eyes,” Maya said. “And you listen. Really listen. Then you say one thing you heard. Something true.”

“That’s not a game,” Leo said. “That’s just sitting.”

“It is a game,” Maya said. “Because you have to guess whose sound it is. And you get a point if it’s a sound nobody else heard.”

Leo wiped his face with his sleeve. He was still holding her hand. “Okay,” he said. “Okay. You go first.”

Maya closed her eyes. The kitchen felt bigger with her eyes shut. She listened past her own breathing. There was the tick of the clock on the wall. There was the rain, still going, softer now. And under the rain, a slow drip from the gutter, counting itself out on the porch step. “The gutter,” she said. “Dripping.”

“That’s one point,” Leo said. “My turn.”

He was quiet for a long time. Maya could feel him listening, his small hand tight around hers. Then he said, “The house is creaking. Like it’s settling into a chair.”

“One point,” Maya said.

“Maya,” he said. “What if I hear something scary?”

“Say it anyway,” Maya said. “Then I’ll say something too. We take turns. That’s the rule.”

So they took turns. Leo heard the refrigerator humming, even without power, a thin low note. Maya heard a car go by on the wet road, swishing. Leo heard the rain get slower. Maya heard Leo breathing. She didn’t say that one out loud. She kept it for herself, the way you keep a warm stone in your pocket.

***

The candle was a stub now, sitting in a puddle of its own wax. Maya watched it and thought about endings, and how Gran had never seemed afraid of them. Then the light came back.

It happened all at once. The refrigerator coughed and started humming for real. The clock on the wall blinked 12:00, 12:00, 12:00. Above the table, the bulb flickered twice and then held steady, and the kitchen was just the kitchen again, yellow and ordinary.

“The power’s back!” Leo shouted. He let go of her hand and jumped up. “Maya! We can watch something!”

“We can,” Maya said. She stayed in her chair. Her hand felt cold where his had been.

Leo ran to the doorway, then stopped. He turned around. The candle was still burning on the table, a small orange dot in all that electric light. It looked almost silly now, like a flashlight left on in the daytime.

“Should we blow it out?” he said.

Maya looked at the candle. She thought about the listening game, and the drip in the gutter, and how she had held on when she didn’t know what else to do. “Not yet,” she said. “Let’s leave it a minute.”

“Why?” Leo said.

“Because we earned it,” Maya said. “And because Gran would.”

Leo came back and sat down. He pulled his chair close, the same way she had pulled hers close to his. For a while they just watched the flame, the two of them, in the bright safe kitchen. It burned down to almost nothing. Then it went out on its own, with a little curl of smoke that smelled like birthdays.

“Okay,” Leo said. “Now we can watch something.”

“Okay,” Maya said.

But neither of them moved for one more minute. They sat in the light, remembering the dark, and it felt like something they had made together, the two of them, out of almost nothing at all.
