Pepper the cat had lived outside the bakery for three winters. He knew every warm brick of its wall. The baker's wife, a round woman with flour on her sleeves, left him scraps every evening. "Here you are, old fellow," she would say, setting down a saucer of milk and a torn piece of bread. Pepper would wait until she went back inside, then eat slowly, keeping one eye on the door.

The bakery smelled of yeast and cinnamon. Pepper liked to sit on the windowsill, where the heat from the ovens seeped through the glass. From there he could watch the people come and go. Children pressed their noses to the window, and Pepper pressed his nose back. Sometimes the baker's wife laughed and tapped the glass. "You're better than a sign," she told him. "People stop to see the cat."

At night, Pepper curled in a wooden crate behind the shop. The crate had an old blanket in it, and the wall kept off the wind. He could hear the baker's wife singing as she cleaned up. Her voice was low and scratchy, like a radio with a bad signal. Pepper would purr until he fell asleep. He did not think of himself as a stray. He thought of himself as the bakery cat.

One morning, a van pulled up outside. Two men carried out the ovens' big mixing bowls and stacked them in the back. The baker's wife stood in the doorway, wiping her hands on her apron. "It's time to go, Pepper," she said quietly. She did not look at him. Pepper watched the van drive away. The windowsill was still warm. He sat on it and waited for the evening.

***

The evening came, and no one brought scraps. Pepper waited on the windowsill until the streetlights buzzed on. The bakery door was locked. Through the glass, he could see empty shelves and a broom leaning in the corner. He meowed once, then again, but the shop stayed dark.

For three days, Pepper kept to his routine. Each morning he sat on the windowsill. Each evening he checked the saucer, which was still there, dry and dusty. On the fourth day, a new sign went up in the window: FOR SALE. Pepper did not know what the words meant, but he knew the smell of the baker's wife was fading from the doorframe.

A woman with a clipboard came and looked at the shop. She did not notice Pepper. "Needs a lot of work," she said to the man with her. "But the location is good." Pepper slipped behind the crate and watched them go. The street felt louder now, and colder. He missed the singing.

That night, he curled in his crate and listened for the scratchy voice. Instead, he heard a bus sigh to a stop, and a dog bark far away. The blanket smelled of flour and old smoke. Pepper pressed his nose into it and closed his eyes. He would wait. The baker's wife had always come back before. She would come back again.

Pepper woke to the rattle of a paper bag. Someone was crouched by his crate, small and quick, setting down a tin plate.

He stayed still and watched through the gap in the wood.

The girl had dark hair tied back with a red band. She wore a coat too big for her. She poured something from the bag onto the plate. It smelled of fish and warm rice.

"There you are," she said softly. "I know you're in there."

Pepper did not move.

"It's all right," she said. "I'll leave it here. You don't have to come out."

She stood up and walked to the corner of the street. Then she stopped and waited. Pepper waited too. The plate steamed in the cold.

At last he crept out. He ate fast, watching her the whole time. She did not come closer. After a while she waved, just once, and went away.

The next day she came again. Same paper bag. Same tin plate. This time she sat on the step across the road.

"My name's Mira," she said. "You don't have to tell me yours."

Pepper licked his paw and pretended not to listen. But he did listen.

When she reached out her hand, he backed away under the crate. Trust was a thing you earned slowly, like a warm spot on a cold night.

***

The third evening, Pepper followed her. He did not plan to. His paws simply decided before his head did.

Mira walked six streets and turned down a lane with bicycles leaning against a wall. She went up a narrow stair and opened a door. Pepper sat on the wall outside and looked in.

The room was small and very tidy. One chair, one lamp, one bed. A bowl with an apple in it. A row of books stacked on the floor like bricks.

An old woman sat by the window, knitting. "You're late," she said.

"I was feeding the bakery cat," said Mira.

"The one that won't come near you?"

"He will," said Mira. "One day."

The old woman laughed. "You keep saying that."

Pepper watched Mira set the table. She set two places, and then she set a third, small one, and put a bit of fish on it.

"Who's that for?" asked the old woman.

"In case he changes his mind," said Mira.

Something in Pepper's chest went warm, then tight. He jumped down from the wall and walked back the way he had come, all six streets, to the crate behind the bakery. The corner was his. It smelled of flour and old smoke, and he knew every crack in it. He slept there that night, curled small, and told himself he was glad to be home.

But the plate Mira left was always empty by morning.

The wind changed on a Thursday. Pepper felt it in his whiskers before he saw a single cloud. All afternoon it pushed at the crates and rattled the bakery's back door, and by evening the sky had gone the colour of wet slate.

Rain came sideways. It drummed on the lids of the bins and turned the alley into a shallow river. Pepper squeezed into the doorway of the shuttered shop across the street, where the step was dry and the wind could not reach him.

He washed his paws and settled down. Then he washed them again. The doorway was warm enough, and safe, and empty.

"You all right in there?" called a man from an upstairs window. "Mind the draught."

Pepper blinked up at him. The man closed the window.

Back at the bakery corner there would be flour dust and quiet. Here there was only the rain. Pepper tucked his nose under his tail and thought about the small plate with the bit of fish on it, set down every evening whether he came or not. He thought about Mira's voice, low and patient, saying, *One day.*

A shutter banged somewhere down the street. The rain kept on. Pepper stood up, shook himself, and found he was already facing the wrong way for home.

***

Morning came grey and rinsed clean. Puddles lay across the cobbles like pieces of fallen sky. Pepper trotted six streets with his tail up, past the fountain, past the shop with the painted sign of a fish.

The old woman's door was open to the air. Mira sat on the step with a broom across her knees, sweeping water off the stones.

She looked up. "Hello," she said, very quietly, as if a loud word might blow him away.

Pepper walked to the step. Then he walked up it, one paw at a time, and sat down beside her.

"You came," said Mira.

He leaned against her knee. Her hand came down, slow and unsure, and rested on his back. He did not move away. She smelled of soap and bread, and her fingers found the spot behind his ear that had been waiting all his life.

"Well," said the old woman from the window. "I said you kept saying it."

"He changed his mind," said Mira.

Pepper purred. It started deep in his chest and rolled out of him like a small engine, and once it had started it would not stop. Mira laughed, and the sound was better than any warm doorway.

That evening she set the small plate by the fire instead of on the step. Pepper ate, then curled on the rug with his chin on her foot, and the lamp burned low, and the house was quiet, and it was home.
