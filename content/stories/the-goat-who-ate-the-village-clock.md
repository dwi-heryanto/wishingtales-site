The trouble began, as most trouble in Pemberly Bottom began, with Barnaby.

He was a goat of no fixed opinions and one fixed appetite. That morning he stood in the belfry yard with the town clock's pull-rope in his mouth like a strand of particularly chewy spaghetti. The rope had held the great brass hammer that struck the hours. Barnaby chewed. The rope parted. The hammer swung once, wildly, struck the bell a single flat clang, and then hung there, useless.

Above him, the clock face read 4:17. It was seven in the morning.

Lila was crossing the square with a tray of pie tins when she heard the clang. She looked up. She looked at Barnaby. She looked up again.

"Oh no," she said. "Oh, no, no, no."

Barnaby swallowed something with a metallic clink. He regarded her with the serene expression of a creature who has done exactly what he intended.

"What did you just eat?" Lila demanded.

He did not answer, being a goat. But when she climbed the belfry stairs two at a time and pressed her face to the inside of the clock face, she found the answer. The numeral seven was gone. Where it had been, there was a clean brass-shaped hole and a small smear of goat spit.

The square was filling up. Mrs. Hartley came out of the bakery with flour on her elbows. The butcher arrived with his apron still tied. Somebody's grandmother was already sitting on the fountain edge, which was her habit whenever anything interesting happened.

"It's four in the morning," said the butcher, squinting at the clock. "Why is it four in the morning?"

"It's seven," said Mrs. Hartley. "I've had two batches out since six. It cannot be four."

"The clock says four," the butcher said, with the patience of a man who trusts machinery over his own stomach. "Seventeen minutes past."

"The clock is wrong," said Lila, coming down the stairs. "The clock is wrong and the goat is why."

Everyone turned to look at Barnaby. Barnaby, sensing an audience, did a small hop and ate a dandelion.

"That's the mayor's goat," someone said.

"That's everyone's goat," said Mrs. Hartley. "That's the problem."

***

By eight o'clock, half the village had gathered in the square, and the mayor had climbed onto the fountain to address them. He was a round, important man named Alderman Crumb, and he wore his chain of office even to buy bread, on the theory that people should know who they were dealing with.

"Friends," he said, spreading his hands. "This is a crisis."

"It's a goat," said the butcher.

"It is a crisis," the mayor repeated, louder. "Today is the pie contest. The pie contest begins at noon. It has begun at noon every year for sixty years. I will not have it said that Pemberly Bottom cannot tell the time."

A murmur went through the crowd. The pie contest was serious business. Last year's winner had been Mrs. Hartley, and the year before that, and the year before that. It was a fact the mayor did not enjoy and mentioned often.

Lila was trying to edge out of the square. She was the clockmaker's apprentice, and the clockmaker had gone to the coast for his sister's wedding, leaving her with a list of instructions and a key. She had not expected a goat.

"Lila!" the mayor called. "Lila, come here."

She came, because there was no polite way not to.

"You fix clocks," he said.

"I'm learning to fix clocks."

"You fix them," he said firmly. "You're the only one who can. I need that clock working before noon. The whole village depends on you."

"It's not the mechanism," Lila said. "The mechanism is fine. The goat ate the number. You can't fix a number by fixing a gear."

"Then find the number," said the mayor. "Find it, make a new one, I don't care. Before noon, Lila. Before noon."

He climbed down and pressed through the crowd with the air of a man who has delegated a problem into nonexistence.

Lila went back up the belfry stairs with a lantern. The clock face from the inside was a beautiful piece of work, all brass and careful teeth. It was also, she now saw, missing more than she had thought. The seven was gone, yes. But so was the four, and the two, and the nine. Four holes in all, arranged around the dial like gaps in a smile.

She sat down on the dusty step and put her head in her hands.

"He's been busy," she said to nobody.

Down in the yard, Barnaby burped a small, satisfied burp that carried all the way up the stairs. It sounded, Lila thought, extremely smug.

***

Lila found Barnaby in the shade of the lych-gate, chewing something with a slow, thoughtful rhythm. The goat looked up at her with yellow eyes that held absolutely no apology.

"You have eaten the number seven," she said. "And the four. And the two. And the nine."

Barnaby swallowed. A small brass corner, which was not part of any number Lila recognized, disappeared down his throat.

"That's not even a numeral," she said. "That's trim. You're eating the trim now."

The goat took a step toward her, tilted his head, and pressed his forehead gently into her hip. It was the exact gesture of a creature who knew he had done something magnificent and expected to be thanked for it. Lila scratched the spot between his horns before she could stop herself, and hated that it worked.

"If I give you a nice length of rope," she said, "will you promise not to eat anything else?"

Barnaby considered this. Then he ate the corner of her apron.

By the pump, the village had split into committees. Widow Fairweather had produced a sundial from her garden and set it on a barrel. It cast a shadow that moved at a speed nobody could agree on. The baker's boy had rigged a water clock from a bucket and a nail, and it was already dripping onto the baker's good shoes. The baker was making his feelings known at volume.

"It's four o'clock somewhere," said Old Tam, who had given up entirely and was eating his lunch.

"It is not four o'clock," said Widow Fairweather. "The sun says a quarter to ten."

"Your sun is a garden ornament."

"Your bucket is leaking on my shoes," said the baker, to nobody in particular.

Lila stood in the middle of this with a rope in one hand and a mouthful of apron thread. She tried to think like a clockmaker instead of a girl with a goat problem. A clock, her master always said, is only a promise that something will happen at the same time every day. Nothing in that promise said brass. Nothing in that promise said numbers.

"We could just guess," said the baker's boy, and everyone turned to look at him with the terrible interest of people who have run out of better ideas.

"We will not guess," said Lila, and was surprised by how steady her voice came out. "Nobody guesses. I'll fix it. I just need..." She looked down. Barnaby was no longer beside her. "Where is the goat?"

Every head in the yard turned at once, the way a flock of birds turns.

Barnaby was standing on the barrel, silhouetted against the sun, wearing the sundial like a hat.

***

The chase went through the vegetable garden and over the low wall and past the pie tables, where the contest entries sat cooling under cloth. Barnaby moved with the unhurried joy of a creature who had never once been caught and saw no reason for that to change today. Lila, who had been caught many times, ran anyway.

He did not head for the meadow. That was the thing she noticed first. He went along the eastern wall, nose to the stones, following something with the intent, weaving certainty of a dog on a scent. Lila slowed down and looked where he was looking.

There was a glisten on the wall. A thin, sticky thread of it, catching the morning light.

"Honey," she said.

Barnaby reached the clock tower door, which someone had left open in the confusion, and went up the stairs. Lila went up after him, two at a time, and arrived in the belfry in time to watch the goat stretch his neck, take the great brass numeral twelve in his teeth, and pull.

The twelve did not come free. Barnaby braced his hooves on the ledge, and the whole face of the clock groaned. Then the twelve let go with a sound like a spoon leaving a jar, and the goat sat down hard on his backside with the number in his mouth.

At the top of the dial there was now nothing. No twelve. Only a smooth place where the brass had been, and above it the morning sky, which is not a number and cannot be read as one.

Lila sat down on the step, because her legs had decided it for her.

"That's all of them," she said. "Twelve, nine, four, two, seven. That's the whole top and the whole bottom of the dial. I can't make new brass before noon. My master's tools are in the next valley."

Barnaby chewed, swallowed, and licked his lips. Then he walked past her, calm as a Sunday, and licked the wall beside the stairs. He licked it twice. He was not licking the wall. He was licking a thin shining line of honey that ran along the top of the skirting, from the doorway, up the stairs, and along the ledge to exactly where the twelve had been hung.

Lila stood up very slowly.

Someone had drawn the goat a map.

She put her finger to the honey and tasted it. It was good honey, dark and floral, the kind that came from one particular hive. She knew that hive. Everyone in the village knew that hive, because it sat at the bottom of the mayor's garden, and the mayor was very proud of it, and the mayor had never once given a jar of it to anybody.

She looked at the trail it had left. It ran out the door and down the stairs and away toward the eastern wall. Beyond the wall it went past the pie tables, past the pump, toward a house with a green door.

"Well," said Lila, to the goat and the empty sky where the twelve had been. "That's not a clock problem at all."

Barnaby, who had eaten a great deal of brass and was feeling it, lay down on the dusty boards and put his chin on his hooves. He watched her with yellow eyes that had, she thought, been waiting all morning for somebody to be clever.

***

Lila went down the clock tower stairs two at a time. Barnaby came after her with the unhurried confidence of a creature who did not believe in time. The honey trail was easy to follow. It caught the light in thin amber threads, and where the boards were worn, the honey had pooled in the grooves and held the dust like a fly in a web.

Out in the square, the argument had grown teeth. The baker's wife was holding two sundials, one in each hand, and declaring that neither of them agreed with the other. A man from the mill wanted a water clock. Old Perrin said he could tell time by his knees, which had never been wrong, and the miller said his knees were wrong every morning of his life.

"None of that will matter," Lila said, "if I'm right." She did not stop to explain. She followed the honey past the pump, past the pie tables with their empty places where the entries would go, to the green door at the bottom of the mayor's garden.

The door was not locked. The mayor was not a man who expected to be caught. Inside, the kitchen smelled of honey and of something burnt, and there was a whole shelf of jars, each one labeled in the mayor's careful hand. The lids were off two of them. A dish on the table held the sticky remains of what had clearly been a deliberate arrangement, a little path of sweetness laid down and drawn away again.

"You're feeding him," Lila said.

"I'm encouraging him," said the mayor, from the doorway. He had his ceremonial chain on and his hat in his hands, and he was trying to look like a man who had simply come home for breakfast.

"You're feeding him brass and honey so he'll eat the clock."

"The clock is a municipal embarrassment," the mayor said. "It gains four minutes a week. Four minutes! My mother's rhubarb pie came out of the oven and missed its moment by four minutes, and I have never had a single blue ribbon in nineteen years of trying, and I am the mayor."

Barnaby walked past them both, put his front hooves on the table, and began, calmly, to eat what was left of the honey. The mayor watched him with the expression of a man whose accomplice has just become a witness.

***

"The contest starts at noon," Lila said. "That's in an hour and a half. We can still do it."

"The clock face is blank at the top," the mayor said. "The numbers are in a goat. There is no noon."

"There's always noon," said Lila. "It just needs somebody to say when." She turned and walked back out into the square, and the mayor followed her, because he was a coward and also because he was curious.

The villagers were still arguing when she climbed onto the pump. "Listen," she said. "The mayor fed the clock to his goat so the pies would be late. He's confessed. He'll be confessing again in a minute, louder." The mayor opened his mouth and then closed it, because Barnaby had come out of the green door behind him and was standing at his heel like a dog who had decided to keep him.

"We can't fix the numbers in time," Lila said. "But we don't need numbers. We need a bell." She pointed at the baker's wife. "Your oven bell. The miller's dinner bell. The old ship's bell from the boathouse. We hang them all on the eastern wall, and when the last one rings, the contest begins."

"And when the pies are done?" said the baker's wife.

Lila looked down at Barnaby, who had found a bit of rope lying by the pump and was chewing it with great contentment. "Him," she said. "He's the only one in this village who's never once been wrong about when something's finished."

So they hung the bells, and they put the rope in Barnaby's reach, and they waited. At what everyone silently agreed was noon, the bells went up in a clatter that set every pigeon in the village into the air. The pies went in. Barnaby chewed his rope in a slow, steady rhythm, and every time he finished a section, the baker's wife pulled the next pie from the oven at exactly the right moment.

The mayor stood by the wall with his hat in his hands and his chain hanging crooked. When the last ribbon was handed out, he did not say a word. Lila took him a slice of the winning rhubarb pie, because she was not cruel. He ate it standing up, and then he went and fetched a ladder and began, without being asked, to take the clock face down.
