Mara woke because the light had changed. Her room above the lamplighter's shed had one small window, and through it the first lantern on the marsh road always cast a thin gold stripe across her blanket. That stripe was gone. The dark in the room was not the soft dark of a clouded night; it was the flat, total dark of a lamp that had gone out.

She was out of bed before she was properly awake, feet finding the cold boards, hands finding the coat she had left on the chair. Down the ladder she went, past her father's empty cot, past the cold hearth, and out into the yard where the smell of the marsh met her like a wet hand on the face.

The first lantern stood at the edge of the road, its iron cage black. The wick inside was dry, she could see that much even in the dimness, and the oil cup was full. Someone had let it burn down and not fed it. Her father never did that. Her father had lit these lanterns every night for thirty years and had never once let a wick go dry.

On the workbench in the shed, pinned under the oil jug, was a scrap of paper in his hard, square hand. Mara held it to the window.

*Wicks must be fed before dawn. All of them. Do not follow me into the marsh. I am sorry.*

She read it three times. The paper shook, so she put it down and pressed both palms flat on the bench until the shaking stopped. The spare flame lived in a clay jar with a brass lid, kept always burning, fed always with a drop of oil. She took it down from its shelf. The jar was warm. The flame inside leaned and righted itself, small and stubborn as a held breath.

Outside, the marsh breathed out across the road. The second lantern waited in the dark a hundred paces on, and beyond it the third, and the fourth, and the long line of them all the way to the far bank. And somewhere out in all that wet blackness was her father, walking away from her with a note left behind.

"You are not to follow," she said aloud, to the note, to the dark, to nobody. Her voice sounded thin. She put the jar in her coat pocket, took the long lighting pole from its bracket, and stepped onto the road. The first lantern was behind her, dark. The second was ahead. She went on.

***

The second lantern stood where the road bent, and the road here was narrow, with black water on either side and reeds that whispered when there was no wind to whisper them. Mara set the pole against the cage and lifted the little door. The jar came out of her pocket, warm as a live thing. She tipped the brass lid and let the flame touch the wick.

It caught. Gold light spilled out across the road and the reeds and the black water, and for a moment the world was ordinary again. She breathed out.

Then the light showed her how far the dark went. Past the second lantern, the road was only a paler line in the blackness. Beyond that, nothing at all. The marsh had never looked so wide. It had never looked so much like a mouth.

"You are doing it wrong," said a voice.

Mara spun. The reeds stood empty. The water stood empty. Nothing moved but the flame in the lantern, and the flame did not flicker.

"Who is there?" she said. Her hand found the pole and held it like a staff, which was foolish, she knew, but it was what she had.

"You light them one by one and think that is a chain," the voice said. It came from everywhere and from nowhere, low and patient, like water finding a crack. "One by one. As if the dark were a tide you could hold back with a row of candles."

"Show yourself."

"I am showing myself. I am all around you. You are standing in me."

The flame in the second lantern guttered then, though no wind touched it, and the light pulled in tight around the cage as if something were pressing on it. Mara's breath came short. She wanted to run back the way she had come, back to the shed and the cold hearth and the note she had left on the bench. Instead she made herself stand still and look at the dark.

"You know my name," she said. It was not a question. The voice had said *Mara* once already, softly, under the words, the way a person says a name they have known a long time.

"I have known it since before your father knew it," the voice said. "He has been feeding my lanterns a long while now. Did he never tell you what they are for?"

The reeds bent, all together, away from the road. Something moved out in the water, slow and certain, and the second lantern's light shrank another hand's width.

"He told me they are to keep the dark out," Mara said.

A long pause. The marsh dripped and settled. Then the voice laughed, very quietly, and it was the worst sound Mara had ever heard, because it was almost kind.

"Oh, child," it said. "No. They are to keep it fed."

Mara walked faster after that, because standing still with the voice in her ears was worse than moving. The third lantern stood at the bend where the road gave up and the marsh took over, its post leaning like a tired old man. Its flame was still alive, but it burned low and blue at the edges, the way a candle does just before it drowns in its own wax. She fed it from the brass cup, and the light steadied, and she let out a breath she had not known she was holding.

A woman sat on the stone at the base of the post. She had been there the whole time, Mara realised, and she had not seen her until the flame caught. The woman wore a shawl the colour of wet bark and her hair was braided with reeds. Her face was neither old nor young, but patient, the way a snare is patient.

"You are the lamplighter's girl," the woman said. "I wondered if he would send you."

"He did not send me," Mara said. "He went into the marsh and left a note."

"Yes." The woman smiled. "He would. He always thinks he can carry it alone."

Mara's hand stayed on the lantern's chain. "Carry what?"

"The rest of the night," the woman said. "There are four more lanterns between here and the far post, and the wicks will not last unless someone walks them. I can walk them for you. I have walked them before, for other lamplighters, when the night was too heavy for one pair of hands."

"What do you want?"

"A small thing. Your father's name, and your own. Say them to me, and I will light every lantern from here to the marsh edge, and you can go home and sleep until morning."

The third lantern guttered, though there was no wind. Mara thought of her father's note, the cramped letters, the way he had written her name as if his hand were shaking. She thought of the voice in the reeds, which had known her before her father did, and she understood that names were not small things at all.

"No," she said.

The woman's smile did not change. "You are sure? It is only a name. He will still be your father. You will still be you."

"No," Mara said again, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I will walk them myself."

The woman stood, and the reeds around the stone bent away from her as if they had been waiting for her to move. "Then you should know what you are walking into," she said. "Because your father did not tell you, and he should have."

***

"The lanterns are not a fence," the woman said. She walked beside Mara along the raised path, her bare feet silent on the boards. "They are a payment. The marsh is a debt, child, and the dark is what is owed. Every night it comes up out of the water and asks to be paid, and every night the lanterns pay it."

Mara kept her eyes on the fourth lantern ahead, a small orange eye in the grey. "Paid with what?"

"With light. With warmth. With the hours your father stood out here feeding them, night after night, so the water would not rise into the village." The woman's voice was almost gentle. "The dark is not a beast to be kept out. It is a hunger. The lanterns feed it, and it goes back down satisfied. That is the bargain. That is what your father has been doing since before you were born."

"Then why did he go into the marsh?"

"Because the debt has grown. The wicks are thin, the oil is low, and a man's hours run out. He went to give it something the lanterns cannot."

Mara stopped walking. The fourth lantern was close now, and beyond it the path curved away into the reeds. "What did he give it?"

"He offered himself," the woman said. "His name. His memory. Whatever the dark asked, so that it would not come for the village instead." She looked at Mara with something that might have been pity. "If you walk the rest of the lanterns, it will take its price from you. It always does. The dark does not lend."

Mara thought of her father's face, the way he hummed while he trimmed the wicks. She thought of the note on the bench, the last word of it smudged where his thumb had pressed too hard. "Then I will pay it," she said. "But I will not give it my name. Names are not for paying."

The woman tilted her head. "Then it will take something else. Something you have not thought to guard."

"Let it try," Mara said, and she lifted the brass cup and walked toward the fourth lantern, and the dark leaned in close to watch her go.

The path to the fourth lantern was a thin ribbon of mud between black water. Mara walked it with the brass cup held out before her, and the flame burned straight up, steady, as if it had no idea it was small. Behind her the third lantern stood lit, a yellow bead on a dark string. Ahead, the fourth waited.

She reached it and set the cup down on the stone shelf. Her hands were shaking, but the wick caught on the first try. Light spread out in a low ring, pushing the reeds back into shape. For a moment the marsh looked like a place instead of a mouth.

Then the dark leaned in.

It did not come as a shape. It came as a coldness at the back of her neck, and a sound like a held breath. The flame guttered, though there was no wind.

"You are brave," the dark said, in a voice like water under a boat. "Your father was brave too. He stood here and made his offer, and I took it, and he walked home lighter than he came."

"He walked home empty," Mara said. "That is not the same."

"It is what he chose." The cold moved around her, curious. "Now you choose. What will you give me to carry this flame to the last lantern?"

"Nothing," she said. "I will carry it for nothing."

"Then I will take nothing," said the dark. "But nothing is a heavy thing when it is a person. I will take what he kept of you. The part of him that knows your face. That is the price of the last lantern."

Mara looked at the flame. It was very small. The last lantern was far, out where the path gave up and the reeds closed over.

"Take it, then," she said. "But I will hate you for it, and I will not forget that I hate you."

"That," said the dark, almost kindly, "is yours to keep."

She lifted the cup and walked. The cold went with her, and somewhere behind her eyes something was being untied, gently, like a knot in a shoelace. She felt it go. She did not know what it was until it was gone.

The last lantern stood at the edge of the world, or what passed for it. She set the cup down. The wick caught. And the night, all at once, was full of small yellow lights, one after another, down the whole length of the marsh path, and the dark had nowhere left to stand.

***

Morning came grey and ordinary, the way mornings do after a long night. The marsh smelled of mud and cut reeds. Mara walked home with the empty cup, and her boots were heavy with water.

The cottage door was open. Her father sat on the bench outside, wrapped in his coat, staring at the path as if he were waiting for someone he could not quite remember.

"Da," she said.

He looked up. His eyes were the same. That was the worst part. They were the same kind eyes, and they did not know her.

"Morning," he said, politely. "Are you the lamplighter's girl?"

Mara stood very still. The cold she had carried all night settled into her chest and made a home there.

"I am," she said.

"I thought so." He nodded, pleased with himself. "I have been sitting here trying to think of her name. It is a good name. It has a river in it."

"Mara," she said.

He said it back, testing it. "Mara. That is right." He frowned. "I am sorry. I should know you better than that."

"You will," she said. She sat down on the bench beside him, close but not touching. "I will tell you."

He looked at the lanterns, still burning low in the daylight, a string of small suns across the marsh. "Did you light all those?"

"I did."

"All of them?"

"All of them." She pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them. "It was a long night. There was a woman out there who wanted my name. I did not give it to her."

He laughed, a short surprised sound, and she heard in it the shape of the laugh she knew. "Good," he said. "Names are not for giving away."

"No," Mara said. "They are not."

She looked at the path, at the lanterns, at the grey water going gold at the edges. Then she turned to her father, who was watching her with the patient attention of a stranger.

"I will start at the beginning," she said. "My name is Mara. I am your daughter. And I am going to tell you about the night I lit every lantern in the marsh."

He settled back on the bench, and she began.
