The rain had been falling since the van pulled onto the gravel. By the time they got the mattresses inside, the cardboard boxes were going soft at the corners. Ivy stood in the hallway of the rented house on Marrow Lane and watched water run down the inside of the window. Sam came past her with a lamp in each hand, his hood still up.

"This place smells like a library that got wet," he said.

"It smells like nothing," Ivy said. "That's what's wrong with it."

Sam set the lamps down and looked at her. "You're doing the thing."

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're counting." He flopped onto his mattress in the front room, which they had agreed would be the front room because it had the only working outlet. Ivy hadn't agreed, exactly. She had said fine.

The house was narrow and tall, like something built between two other things that had been knocked down. The floors sloped. The stairs had a handrail that ended too soon. Their mother had signed the lease from a photograph, and in the photograph the house had looked like a place you could be happy in. Rain did that to photographs. It made them look like somewhere else.

Ivy went to the kitchen for a glass of water. The tap ran brown, then clear. She drank it anyway.

And then, from somewhere above her, a voice said: "Ten."

She set the glass down very carefully. The rain went on against the window. She waited, and the house waited with her, and nothing else happened. She went to the foot of the stairs and looked up into the dark.

"Sam?"

"What?" he called, from the front room.

"Did you say something?"

"I said what?"

She stood there a moment longer. Then she went and got her phone and turned on the torch and climbed the stairs. She was thirteen and not a coward. She had already decided that whatever it was, it had been the pipes. The pipes in a rented house could say anything. Pipes said ten all the time.

At the top of the stairs there was a landing with three doors off it. All three were open. The rooms behind them were empty except for dust and a dead moth on the sill. The voice did not say ten again.

When she came down, Sam was asleep with his mouth open. She lay on her own mattress in the dark and tried to remember whether the voice had sounded like a person or like a recording of a person. It had not sounded like either. It had sounded like the house itself clearing its throat.

***

In the morning the rain had stopped and the house was worse in the light. Ivy noticed things she hadn't seen the night before. There was a hook in the hallway where a coat had hung long enough to leave a clean patch. A number of small marks ran along the skirting board. A smell in the back bedroom was like a candle that had been blown out a long time ago.

She started counting doors without meaning to. It was the thing Sam said she did. Front door, back door, cellar door, the door under the stairs. Upstairs, three bedrooms and a bathroom and the cupboard on the landing. The kitchen had a door with glass in it. The front room had a door that stuck.

Ten. She counted again, and got ten again.

Sam came in from the yard with mud on his shoes. "There's a shed," he said. "It's got a padlock on it. Old padlock, though. I could probably get it."

"Sam."

"What?"

"Listen." She put her hand flat on the wall. "Last night I heard a voice say ten. I thought it was the pipes. But there are ten doors in this house."

Sam looked at her for a moment, then went and counted the doors himself. She watched him do it. She watched his mouth move. She watched him come back to the kitchen with his face changed.

"Okay," he said. "That's weird. But doors are a normal thing to have. You can make anything match if you want it to."

"I didn't want it to."

"You kind of did, though." He said it without malice, which was the worst way to say a thing. "You count stuff. You always count stuff."

"I'm going to read in the back bedroom," she said.

"Suit yourself."

She took the back bedroom because it was the only room that got sun. She sat on the floor with her book and the window open. It was quiet for a long time. A car went past. A dog barked twice and then thought better of it.

Then, from the hallway, softly, in a voice that was neither hers nor Sam's: "Nine."

Ivy did not move. The book slid from her knee. She sat there with her heart going and listened to the house be empty. After a while she heard Sam call out from the kitchen, easy, unbothered, "Did you say something?"

Ivy went to the kitchen doorway. Sam had found a bag of crisps and was eating them over the sink. He dropped crumbs into the drain like the drain was a mouth he was feeding.

"It said nine," Ivy said.

"Said what now?"

"The house. It counted down. Eight doors, then nine. Now it's at nine, going down."

Sam turned the tap on and washed the salt off his fingers. "I didn't hear anything. And I don't want to hear anything, because we've got school in the morning and this is the first night and I'd like to sleep." He dried his hands on his jeans. "You do this. You wind yourself up and then you can't sleep and then you wake me up at two in the morning."

"I won't wake you up."

"You always wake me up."

He went past her down the hallway to his room. Ivy stood in the kitchen a moment, then followed. The hallway had gone dark at the far end where the bulb was still warming up, and she did not like the dark end of the hallway. Sam's door was the third on the left. He opened it, went in, and said, "Night then," and pushed it shut.

It did not click. It made a sound she had not heard a door make before, a soft wet sound, like a tongue on an envelope. Ivy stood in the hall and watched the door settle into its frame. It became, she would swear later, a little more flush than a door ought to be. The paint line around it was even all the way round. There was no gap at the bottom for light or air.

"Sam?"

Nothing.

"Sam, open the door."

The handle turned freely under her hand. The door did not move. She put her shoulder to it and it was like pushing a wall. "Sam!" She hit the panel with the flat of her hand, once, twice. "Sam, say something!"

From inside, muffled, annoyed, the way he was always annoyed: "What? I'm right here. What?"

"The door won't open."

A pause. She heard him get up, heard the bed complain, heard his feet on the boards. Then she heard him push the door from the other side, and heard it not move.

"Ivy. Ivy, this isn't funny."

"I know it isn't."

"Open the door."

"I'm trying."

She tried the handle again. She tried lifting it. She tried putting her foot against the wall and hauling, and the door sat in its frame like it had been there a hundred years, like it had never been open at all. Behind her, from the far end of the hall, from the front room where the boxes were still stacked against the wall, the voice said, gently, like a person being patient with a child:

"Eight."

***

Ivy did not scream. She wanted to. What she did instead was go to the kitchen, open the drawer, and take out the butter knife, which was the only thing in the house with an edge. On the way back she passed the front room and did not look into it.

The butter knife was useless. She knew that. She knelt at Sam's door and slid it into the seam at the lock side. She worked it up and down, and the paint came away in a curl, and the blade bent, and the door held. Sam was talking on the other side, fast, the way he talked when he was frightened and pretending not to be.

"It's cold in here," he said. "Ivy, it's really cold. And the window's shut but it's like there's a draught coming from under the bed."

"Get on the bed. Get right in the middle of the bed and put the blanket over your head."

"Why?"

"Because I'm asking."

A pause, and then the creak of the mattress and the rustle of the blanket. "Okay. Okay. I'm on the bed. Ivy, why am I on the bed?"

"Because I'm going to get you out and I don't want you looking at anything."

She put the knife back in the seam and leaned her weight on it. Something in the door gave, not the lock, but the wood. It was a long splintered complaint. She got the blade in deeper and levered, and the paint split down the frame with a sound like a page tearing. The door swung in an inch. Then three. Then she had it half open and the cold came out at her like a cellar in winter.

Sam was on the bed with the blanket over his head, a lump of blanket and shivering. She caught his shoulder through it and pulled and he came. The blanket came off, and he was white and his lips were grey. He had been crying, though he would deny that for years.

"Seven," said the voice, from the hallway, from just outside the door, so close it might have had its face to the paint.

Sam heard it that time. She saw him hear it. His eyes went to the doorway and stayed there.

"Ivy," he said. "What is that?"

"It's the house," she said. "It's counting."

"Counting what?"

She didn't answer, because she didn't know yet. Whatever she said next, he would remember, and she wanted to be right.

The hallway was colder than it had been. Ivy stood with the butter knife still in her hand and Sam pressed against her side. The voice did not speak again, but she could feel it waiting, the way you feel a held breath in a room. "We have to find out what it wants," she said.

"It wants to count," Sam said. His voice was scraped raw. "Let it count."

"It's counting us." She said it before she had thought it through, and knew it was true the moment it was out. "Every number is a door, Sam. Every door it shuts is a person."

He looked at her, and she watched him decide not to cry again. "Then we open a door," he said.

The stairs down to the front room were narrow, and the wallpaper had a damp bloom along the skirting. Ivy went first. The voice had gone quiet, but the house had not. Something ticked in the walls, slow and regular, like a clock with a bad heart. In the front room the rain had stopped and the streetlamp outside threw a long yellow shape across the floor. The shape fell on a book.

It lay open on the mantel, where no book had been when they carried boxes past it an hour before. Leather, cracked at the spine, the pages soft with age. Ivy lifted it down. The first page said, in a hand that leaned like a fence in wind: *Entries.*

Below that, line after line of names. Each one had a date and a number. *Edith Marlow, 1902, nine. Thomas Reed, 1903, eight. The Bellamy children, 1911, seven.* The numbers went down and down, and the names went on, and the ink changed from brown to black to a blue so fresh it looked wet.

"Ivy," Sam said. "Look at the last one."

The last written line was a name neither of them knew, dated fourteen years ago, with the number *one* beside it. And under that, on a fresh page, in a hand that was not quite the same, the ink still shining: *Ivy. Sam.* No numbers yet. Just the names, waiting.

"It writes us down before it takes us," she whispered.

The voice spoke from the doorway behind them. "Six."

Ivy turned. The front door stood open to the wet street, and the voice had come from the dark beyond it, and the dark had come in. She felt the cold of it on her face, and she understood, all at once, what the counting was for. It was not counting doors. It was counting down to the last name on the page.

She set the book on the mantel and stood in front of it. Her hands were shaking, so she pressed them flat against her thighs. "Five," said the voice, and it was closer now, and it was patient.

"No," Ivy said.

The word fell into the room and the ticking in the walls stopped. "No," she said again, louder. "You don't get to count us. You don't get to write us down and count us like doors. I'm answering you. I'm the one who answers." She picked up the pen from the mantel, though she had not seen a pen before, and she drew a line through her own name. Then through Sam's. The ink smeared under her thumb and her hand came away black.

For a long moment nothing happened. Then the book on the mantel closed itself, gently, the way a hand closes, and the voice said nothing at all.

***

They did not sleep. They sat on the stairs with their backs to the wall and their shoes on. Sam held the butter knife because Ivy let him, and the house stayed quiet. The ticking did not come back. The cold drew out of the rooms slowly, like water leaving a boat, and by the time the windows went grey the air was only air.

At dawn they went through every room and opened every door. The house let them. The front door swung wide on a wet street and a sky the colour of dishwater. The smell of rain and bins and someone's early toast came in, and Ivy breathed it like it was the first air she had ever had.

"We're leaving," she said.

"What about the book?" Sam asked.

She had thought about that all night. "We leave it. We leave everything. We tell the landlord the roof leaks and we go." She did not say what she was afraid of, which was that the book would find its way into her bag if she touched it again.

They packed in ten minutes. At the door Sam stopped and looked back at the hallway, at the doors all standing open, and she saw him count them under his breath. "It's not counting anymore," he said.

"No," she said. "Come on."

They were halfway down the path when she remembered her phone charger, still plugged in beside the bed. Sam said he would wait, and she went back alone, and the house was just a house, cold and empty and smelling of old carpet. She pulled the charger from the wall and turned to go.

The book was on the bed. Open. She had not left it open, and she had not left it on the bed, and she stood very still and looked at it.

The old names were all still there, struck through or faded. The fresh page was turned. At the top, in a hand that leaned like a fence in wind, someone had written a new line:

*Ivy. Sam. Ten.*

The ink was still wet. She touched it, and it came away on her finger, and she knew it was not ink at all, but something the house had made to look like it.

Outside, Sam called her name. She closed the book and put it face down on the bed. She walked out without looking back, and the front door shut behind her with a small, polite click, like a lock, or a count beginning.
