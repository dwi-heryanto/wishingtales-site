The brass bell above the door rang twice on Tuesday afternoon, the way it always did when someone came in from the lane. Juno looked up from the counter, where she was sorting three pencils by length. A tall man stood in the doorway, holding his hat in both hands like a bowl of soup he was afraid to spill.

"Good afternoon," he said. "I have lost my shadow."

Juno looked at the floor behind him. A shadow lay there, neat as a coat on a hook, stretching from his boots to the rolled paper in the window.

"Your shadow is right there," she said.

Oda came through from the back room, wiping her hands on her apron. "Come in, Silas Corrigan," she said. "Sit down before the light moves."

The man sat on the stool by the counter. His shadow sat with him, patient and grey.

"I am the lamplighter for the lower lane," he said. "I light the lamps at dusk and put them out at dawn. It is a good job. I have done it for years."

"And the shadow?" Oda asked.

Silas turned his hat over in his hands. "Last night I lit the last lamp and turned round, and my shadow was thin. Like it had been stretched. It used to be a wider shadow than that. I know it did."

Juno watched the shadow on the floor. It looked ordinary to her. Grey, quiet, the right size for a man with a hat.

Oda said nothing. She took the brown paper from the drawer and set it on the counter, and she did not reach for the pencils yet.

***

"When did you first notice the thinness?" Oda asked.

Silas frowned at his hat. "I could not say exactly. It crept up, like weather."

"Who walked beside you when the shadow was wide?"

His hands stopped moving. "My brother. He walked the lane with me, evenings. He carried the ladder."

Juno leaned forward. "So the shadow is still there," she said. "It has not gone anywhere. It is just smaller. We should draw where it is, and show him it is fine."

Oda shook her head slowly. "No."

"But it is right there on the floor," Juno said. "I can see it."

"You can see the shadow that is left," Oda said. "He is asking about the one that went." She set the paper square. "A map of where a thing sits tells you nothing about where it walked off to."

Juno's ears went hot. "Then where did it go?"

"That," said Oda, "is what you must draw."

Silas looked from one of them to the other. "I only want it back," he said. "Or to know it is well."

Juno took the black pencil in her hand and found it heavier than usual. Outside, the lane was going gold at the edges, and the far end of it was already a little different from the morning.

"Where did he go?" Juno asked. "Your brother."

Silas did not answer for a long moment. "Away," he said at last. "Just away."

Juno pulled the brown paper square toward her and began with the black pencil, because black was for what is there. She drew the shop first, a small box, and put the bell in as a dot above the door. Then Corvid Lane went out from it in a long slow curve, the bakery squared off with its chimney, the bus stop a tick at the corner.

Silas leaned over the counter. "That is my lane," he said, sounding surprised, as if he had never seen it flat before.

"It is your lane," Juno said. "Now hush." She was not being rude. Drawing took her whole face, and Oda had told her often enough that a mapmaker who talks draws a map with holes in it.

The black line ran past the bakery and kept going, thinner and thinner, until it stopped at the edge of the paper. That was the far end of the lane, where the road would not hold still long enough to be set down properly.

Then Juno picked up the red pencil. Red was for what is not.

She drew a small mark in the gutter outside the shop, then another, then another, walking them down the lane in a trail of little red hooks. Each hook leaned the way a person leans when they are going somewhere and not looking back. The trail crossed the bakery, passed the bus stop, and gathered at the far end of the lane in a knot the size of a thumbprint.

"That is where it went," Juno said.

Oda came to look, wiping her hands on her apron. "You have drawn a path," she said. "Good. Now fold it and give it to him."

Silas took the map in both hands. The fold lines made it a strange shape, like a wing.

"I have not been down that end in years," he said quietly.

***

The lane at dusk was all long light and low gold. Juno walked beside Silas with the map open between them, and the red marks led on ahead like small dropped berries.

"Left at the bakery," she said.

The baker was pulling in his board and nodded to them both. Silas nodded back, slow, as though he were remembering how.

"Here," Juno said. "Next red mark. By the bus stop."

A woman waited there with a bag of shopping. Silas lifted his hat to her. She said his name and asked how he was keeping, and he said he was keeping, and they both seemed pleased about it.

Juno checked the map. The red trail bent away from the road now, off the pavement, into a strip of grass that ran along the back of the lane where nobody walked much. The grass was wet and the light was going.

"It goes off the path," she said. "Is that all right?"

"It is all right," Silas said. He was walking faster now, not slower.

The red marks ended at the thumbprint knot, and the knot was on a lamppost. It stood at the very far end of Corvid Lane, where the houses gave up and the hedges got tall. The lamppost was old iron, bent a little, and the glass of its lamp was grey with dust. No light came out of it.

Silas stopped. He put one hand flat against the cold post.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, there you are."

Juno looked down at the grass, then up at the dark lamp, and could not see any shadow there at all, except her own and his, stretching out long in the last of the evening.

Silas kept his hand on the lamppost. His shoulders went up and down, slow, like he was breathing in something he had missed. "I used to light this one last," he said. "Every night. My brother, Tam, he walked home this way from the late shift. He said my lamp was the only thing that told him he was nearly home."

Juno waited. The dusk was turning the hedges blue. "Then he moved away," Silas said. "Three towns over. It was a good job, it was the right thing. But after that I stopped coming down this end. I told myself it was out of the way. I let the lamp go out."

"And your shadow?" Juno asked.

Silas looked down at the grass. "That's the thing," he said. "I didn't lose my shadow. I lost the shadow that walked beside me. Tam's. The one that used to stretch out long and thin on this path while we talked about nothing." His voice was quiet. "It was never mine to keep. But I missed it something awful."

Juno thought of the map in her hand, the red line ending at the thumbprint. "So the map brought you back to the last place you saw it," she said. "To the place where you stopped lighting the lamp."

Silas nodded. He reached into his coat and brought out a small matchbox. "I still carry these," he said. "Every night. Habit, I suppose."

He struck a match and held it up to the grey glass. The flame wobbled, then caught the wick inside. Light spilled out, warm and yellow, and the lamppost hummed like it was waking up. Shadows jumped back from the grass, and for a moment Juno saw two of them, hers and Silas's, and a third one, fainter, stretching off toward the hedges. Then it was just the two of them again.

Silas smiled. "There," he said. "Now he can find his way, if he ever comes back this way."

***

Juno walked home with the map folded in her pocket. The lamplight followed her a long way down Corvid Lane, and the bakery window was warm and gold. She did not hurry.

The shop was dark when she got there, except for the small lamp over the counter. Oda was already upstairs. Juno slid the map out of her pocket and laid it flat on the counter. The black lines and the red trail looked ordinary now, just pencil on brown paper. She could see where her own thumb had smudged the edge.

She put the map in the drawer with the others. Then she opened the tin and set the black pencil in its slot, and the red one next to it. The blue pencil lay in its own little drawer, untouched. She closed the drawer and heard the soft click.

From upstairs came the sound of Oda's chair, and the kettle starting to sing. Juno turned off the lamp and stood for a moment in the dark shop, where the bell hung quiet above the door. Somewhere out at the end of the lane, a lamppost was burning, and someone might be walking toward it.
