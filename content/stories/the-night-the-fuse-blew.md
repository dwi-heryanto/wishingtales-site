The ice came down first, a dry ticking against the windows that Maya mistook for sand until she pressed her palm to the glass and felt the cold bite through. Then the wind rose, and the power failed with a soft, apologetic click, as if the house itself had decided to stop trying.

For a moment nobody moved. Maya stood at the sink with a dish towel in her hand, listening to the silence spread from room to room. Bee was on the floor with her crayons, and she looked up, her mouth a small round O.

"The lights went out," Bee said, as if this were a fact worth announcing.

"I noticed." Maya set the towel down. "Cole?"

Her brother was already in the hallway, phone flashlight on, its beam swinging across the walls. "Breaker's fine," he called. "It's the whole street. I can see the Johnsons' place—dark too."

The house had gone quiet the way a held breath goes quiet. Maya could hear the tick of the ice against the glass, the creak of the radiators cooling, and under that, a deeper hush, as though the night had leaned in close.

"It's getting cold," Bee said. She had pulled her knees up inside her sweater.

Maya crossed to the thermostat and tapped it. The display was blank. "It's electric," she said, more to herself than to anyone. "The furnace won't run without power."

Cole came back into the kitchen, his jaw set. "How long do these usually last?"

"Hours. Maybe a day." Maya opened the cabinet under the sink and pulled out a box of candles, half a pack of tea lights, and a flashlight that flickered when she shook it. "We need to get warm and stay warm."

Bee's teeth had started to chatter, a small, rhythmic sound. Maya knelt beside her and rubbed her arms through the sweater. "We're going to make a nest," she said. "Like a fort. You'll like it."

Cole was already in the living room, pulling cushions off the couch. The windows there were the old kind, single-paned, and the cold was coming through them like a held hand. He taped a blanket over the worst one with strips of duct tape, his movements quick and sure.

"We should all sleep in one room," Maya said. "Body heat."

"Agreed." Cole didn't look at her. He hadn't looked at her much since he'd come home three days ago, and the silence between them had its own temperature, colder than the house.

They worked without talking, gathering what they needed. Candles went on the coffee table, along with the good wool blanket from the hall closet and the sleeping bags from the garage. Bee dragged in a pile of pillows and arranged them in a circle. The ice kept ticking, a patient, insect sound.

"It's like the house is counting," Bee said. "Tick, tick, tick."

"That's the ice," Maya said.

"I know." Bee nestled into the pillows. "But it sounds like counting."

***

By eight the living room had become a small island of light. The candles threw soft, jumping shadows, and the blankets were piled high in the middle of the floor. Maya had found the old camping lantern in the basement, and it burned with a steady glow that made the corners of the room recede into darkness.

Bee was in the nest, wrapped in two sweaters and a sleeping bag, her face flushed with the warmth of the blankets. Cole sat on the edge of the couch, holding his hands out toward the candles.

"You're shaking," Maya said.

He pulled his hands back. "It's cold."

"It's not that cold yet." She sat down across from him, cross-legged on the floor. "Cole."

He didn't answer. The lantern hissed softly.

"You've been shaking since you got here," she said. "Before the power went out."

Cole looked at the window, at the blanket taped over it, at the black square of glass where the ice was building. "I'm fine."

"You're not." She kept her voice low so Bee wouldn't hear. "You haven't said two words to me since you walked in. You haven't said anything about where you've been."

"I told you. A friend's place."

"For four months?"

He was quiet. The candle flames leaned as if someone had breathed on them, then straightened. Cole rubbed his hands together, and the tremor in them was visible even in the dim light.

"It's not your business," he said finally.

"You're my brother." Maya pulled her knees up. "That makes it my business."

Bee stirred in her nest, murmuring something in her sleep. Both of them looked at her, and the silence stretched out, filled with the tick of the ice and the small sound of Bee's breathing.

"We need more heat," Maya said at last, because it was easier than the other thing. "The candles aren't enough. The temperature's going to drop."

Cole nodded, grateful for the change of subject. "What do you suggest?"

Maya looked around the room, at the furniture, at the old wooden kitchen chairs stacked in the corner, the ones their mother had painted blue one summer and never finished. "Those," she said. "We can burn those."

Cole followed her gaze. "Mom's chairs."

"Mom's chairs or frostbite." Maya stood up. "Help me carry them in."

He hesitated, then got to his feet. His hands were still shaking, but he picked up a chair and carried it to the fireplace. The wood was dry and old, and it caught quickly, sending up a bright, hot flame. The light pushed back the darkness, and for a moment the room felt almost warm.

Bee woke up and watched the fire with wide eyes. "It's pretty," she said.

"It's warm," Maya said. "That's better."

Cole sat down again, closer to the fire this time, and held out his hands. The shaking didn't stop, but the flames were bright, and the ice kept ticking, and the three of them stayed there together, waiting for the cold to break.

***

Bee had gone quiet, which was worse than the crying. Maya knelt beside her and saw the color of her sister's lips, a thin, wrong blue, like the edge of a frozen puddle. She touched Bee's cheek and it felt like the inside of the freezer.

"Cole." Her voice came out flat. "Look at her."

He looked. Something in his face changed, the way a door changes when it finally gives. "Okay," he said. "Okay. Blankets. All of them. Every blanket in the house, and the coats, and the towels, whatever's clean."

They worked by candlelight. Maya pulled the quilts off her own bed and the heavy wool one from the hall closet. She took the fleece their mother kept folded on the couch for exactly nothing, it turned out, because nobody ever used it. Cole dragged the mattress off the spare bed and laid it in front of the hearth. Together they built a nest, a low mound of blankets and pillows and old coats. Maya carried Bee into the middle of it and wrapped her until only her face showed, small and pale and watching.

"Is it a fort?" Bee asked.

"It's a fort," Maya said. "Best fort in the county."

"I'm the queen."

"You're the queen." Maya tucked the fleece under her chin. Her hands had gone clumsy, the fingers slow to obey. She had read once that cold made you stupid before it made you dead, that people took off their coats in blizzards because they felt warm. She understood now how that could happen. The fire was so bright it was easy to believe in it.

Cole brought in an armful of the blue chairs. Their mother had painted them one July, back when the kitchen was going to be yellow, and then the summer ended and the paint stopped halfway up the legs, so every chair wore a blue skirt over bare wood. He set them down and stood looking at them.

"We don't have to," Maya said. "I could look for the axe, get some of the fence—"

"The fence is frozen green. It won't burn." He tested a chair leg with his thumb. "These will. They're dry as paper."

"Mom's going to cry."

"Mom's going to be glad we're alive." He said it lightly, but he didn't look at her when he said it. He broke the first chair against the hearthstone, and the crack was loud in the small room, and Bee flinched inside her nest and then settled again.

One by one the blue legs went in. The fire took them greedily. Maya watched the paint blister and curl and go black, and she thought about that summer, about her mother on the porch with a brush in her hand and paint on her elbows, laughing at something.

***

The room warmed slowly, the way a body warms, a degree at a time. Maya sat with her back against the couch and Bee's nest against her side, close enough to feel her sister breathing. Cole fed the fire and did not sit down. Between trips to the pile he stood with his hands out, and Maya watched them and could not stop watching them.

The shaking was not cold. She knew cold now; cold was slow and heavy and made you want to lie down. His hands shook fast and fine, like a struck wire, and his jaw was tight, and he kept checking the windows as if something out there were waiting to be let in.

"You're not shaking from the cold," she said.

He pulled his hands back from the flames. "I'm fine."

"You're not, though." She kept her voice low so Bee wouldn't hear. "You've been like this since you got here."

"I've been like this a while." He sat down at last, heavily, on the floor across from her, the fire between them. "You want to know why I left?"

"You left because of me." It came out before she could stop it, and it came out hard, three years of it packed down tight. "You couldn't stand being in the house with me. That's what Dad said. He said I ran you off."

Cole looked at her for a long moment. The fire snapped. "Dad said that?"

"He said it plenty."

"Maya." He rubbed his face with both hands. When he took them away he looked older than she remembered, and tireder. "I didn't leave because of you. I left because of him. The doors got slammed. The walls got hit. The next thing was going to be me, or worse, it was going to be you. And I'd be the one doing it, because that's what he was teaching." His voice was steady in a way that cost him something. "I left so I wouldn't turn into him."

She sat with that. The wind leaned on the windows. Snow ticked against the glass like small fingers.

"I thought you hated me," she said.

"I never hated you." He shook his head slowly. "I should've written. I should've called. I told myself you were better off, that he'd be easier on you if I wasn't there to fight with. That was stupid. I know it was stupid."

"Yeah," Maya said. Her throat hurt. "It was."

He almost laughed. "Okay."

Bee stirred and murmured something about horses. Maya pulled the blanket up over her shoulder, and the fire burned, and the blue paint turned to ash one curl at a time.

***

The fire had eaten the last chair down to a red-eyed crouch. Maya fed it the final splinter and watched the glow sink into the ash. Cold pressed in from the windows again, patient and thorough, like water finding a boat's seams. Cole was asleep sitting up, his head tipped back, Bee curled against his side under two blankets and a coat. Maya touched Bee's cheek. It was cool but not cold. Still, the room had lost its battle with the night. The thermometer by the door read forty-one degrees and falling.

She pulled on her boots and both sweaters and her father's old canvas coat from the hook. It smelled of sawdust and gasoline and a time before everything went wrong. "Cole," she said. His eyes opened fast, the way they did when he was already halfway to a fight. "Fire's out. Wood's in the shed. I'm going."

He sat up, scrubbing his face. "I'll go."

"You're the one who got Bee to sleep. You stay." She found the flashlight on the shelf and clicked it. The beam was weak, yellow, honest about its limits. "It's twenty feet. I'll be back before you miss me."

He looked at the door, then at Bee, then at her. Something passed over his face, an old argument with himself. "Take the big shovel. If the lock's frozen, hit it."

"I know how to hit things," she said, and went out.

The night hit her like a wall. Wind came down the hill behind the house and shoved snow across the yard in long white ropes. The flashlight beam carved a tunnel through it and showed her nothing else. She kept her eyes on the shed, a black shape at the edge of the light, and walked. Her fear was an old companion. It had lived in her chest since she was small. It told her there were things in the dark that wanted her. It was wrong, she knew. The dark was just dark. But knowing a thing and feeling it were two different houses.

The shed door was frozen. She kicked it once, twice, and it gave with a crack. Inside, the woodpile leaned in neat rows, dry and pale. She loaded her arms until her back complained, then loaded one more log on top. Going back was harder. The wind was in her face now, and the snow stung her eyes, and the flashlight beam seemed shorter than before. She counted her steps. Ten. Twelve. The porch light, she told herself. The porch light would be there.

It was. A small yellow eye in the storm, exactly where it should be. She climbed the steps and shouldered the door open, and the cold came in with her, and Cole was there to take the wood from her arms.

***

The wood was pine and it burned fast and bright, filling the room with a smell like summer. Maya fed the stove until its belly glowed. Heat rolled out across the floor in waves, and the frost on the inside of the windows began to weep and run. Bee woke around three and asked if it was morning. Cole told her it was almost morning, which was a kind of truth. She drank warm water from a mug and fell asleep again with her hand in Maya's.

They sat together in the orange light and said nothing for a while. The storm loosened its grip on the house. The wind dropped to a mumble, then to a hush. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked twice and settled.

"You should go back to sleep," Cole said.

"I'm not tired." Maya watched the fire. "Are you staying? After this?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he said, "I was going to leave again. In the morning. I had it all figured out." He turned his mug in his hands. "I don't want to do that anymore."

"Then don't."

"It's not that simple. He'll come back from the motel eventually. He'll be sorry and then he'll be sorry in the other way." Cole's jaw worked. "I can't promise I'll be good at this. I've been gone a long time."

"You don't have to be good at it," Maya said. "You just have to be here."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw the boy he used to be under the man's face he'd grown. "Okay," he said. "I'll be here."

Outside, the sky was turning the color of old iron. The first gray light came through the frost and made the room strange and soft. Maya got up and checked Bee's hands. Warm. Her lips were pink again. The thermometer by the door read fifty-eight and climbing. The furnace kicked on with a shudder, and the power came back with a click and a hum, and the refrigerator started singing in the kitchen like nothing had happened.

Bee opened her eyes. "Did I miss it?"

"Miss what?" Maya asked.

"The morning."

"No," Maya said. "You're right on time."

Cole stood and went to the window and wiped a clear space with his sleeve. The world outside was white and still and clean. He didn't say anything, but he didn't move away from the glass either. Maya watched his reflection in it, standing where the dark had been, and she felt the long silence between them finally begin to thaw.
