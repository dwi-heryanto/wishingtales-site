The brass bell above the shop door gave a small, polite ring. Juno looked up from the tin of pencils she was sorting. A man stood on the mat, holding his hat in both hands like it might try to escape.

"Good morning," said Oda, without looking up from her ledger. "What have you lost?"

The man cleared his throat. "It's Tuesday," he said. "I've lost Tuesday."

Juno stopped sorting. She had heard customers ask for keys, for dogs, for the sound of a name. Nobody had ever asked for a day of the week.

"I see," said Oda, and wrote something in the ledger. "Juno will draw you a map."

"But I don't know where it went," said the man. "That's the trouble. It was here on Sunday, and then Monday happened, and now it's gone."

Oda finally looked up. "Your name?"

"Pemberton. Mr. Pemberton."

"Juno," said Oda, "the brown paper. The good one, not the roll with the crease."

Juno fetched the paper and laid it flat on the counter. Mr. Pemberton watched her with wide, hopeful eyes. She felt the familiar itch in her fingers, the one that meant a map was waiting.

"Where do I start?" she asked.

"At the beginning," said Oda. "Or at the bakery. Whichever is closer."

***

Juno drew the lane first. Corvid Lane ran from the bus stop to the far end, which was never the same twice. She put in the bakery, the bench, the lamp post with the crooked top. Black pencil, for what is there.

Then she stopped. "What else?" she said.

Mr. Pemberton leaned over the counter. "That's very nice," he said. "But where's Tuesday?"

"That's the question," said Oda, from her chair. "Juno, leave a square empty. Where Tuesday should be."

Juno frowned. "But I don't know where it should be."

"Neither does he," said Oda. "That's why it's empty."

Juno picked up the red pencil. Red was for what is not. She drew a neat square in the middle of the lane, right beside the bakery, and left it blank inside.

"It looks like a hole," said Mr. Pemberton.

"It is a hole," said Juno. "That's what's missing."

Oda nodded slowly. "Now the blue," she said.

Juno's hand hovered over the blue pencil. "But I'm not allowed."

"Not allowed to use it," said Oda. "But you can draw the square for it. One line, blue, around the empty space. That's not using it. That's making room."

Juno drew a thin blue border around the empty square. It looked like a window waiting for someone to look through.

"Good," said Oda. "Now walk it."

Mr. Pemberton held the map at arm's length, the way people hold things they are not sure of. Then he put on his glasses, and then he took them off again.

"Right," he said. "After you, young mapmaker."

Out on Corvid Lane the wind smelled of bread. It always did, Juno had decided. It was one of the lane's habits. Mr. Pemberton walked beside her with his coat buttoned wrong, one side higher than the other, and did not notice.

"The square's just past the bakery," Juno said, checking her map. "We go left at the bins."

"There's a bakery?" said Mr. Pemberton, craning his neck.

There was. Or there had been. The sign above the door said BREAD AND CAKES, with the words flaking off like old pastry. Underneath, painted in smaller letters, someone had written TUESDAYS ONLY. The window was grey with dust. A spider had built a very thorough web across the door handle, and the spider's heart did not look in it.

"Closed," said Mr. Pemberton. He sounded genuinely puzzled, as if the shop had shut while his back was turned.

Juno pressed her nose to the glass. Inside, shelves stood bare. A till sat open and empty. On the counter, a single loaf tin, so old the tin had gone soft at the corners.

"My map says the empty square is right here," she said. "Beside the bakery."

"Beside it," repeated Mr. Pemberton. He looked at the shut door. He looked at the lane. Then he took off his glasses and cleaned them on his sleeve, and put them back on, and looked again.

"I know this shop," he said, slowly, like a man reading a word in a language he had forgotten he spoke.

***

"You know it?" said Juno.

"I brought someone here," said Mr. Pemberton. "Every Tuesday. She had a bun with the currants picked out, because she said the currants were too busy." He laughed, a short surprised laugh. "Too busy. What a thing to say."

"Who was she?"

Mr. Pemberton was quiet for a moment. "My daughter," he said. "When she was small. When we lived near the lane. Then we moved, and she grew, and Tuesdays got busy with other things." He turned the map over in his hands. "I suppose I lost the Tuesday. Not the day. The day's still there, obviously. I mean mine."

Juno looked down at the map. She was not sure what she expected to see, but what she saw made her grip the paper tighter.

The red square she had drawn was changing. It had been neat and empty. Now thin red lines were creeping out of it, the way frost creeps across a window. They ran to the bakery door. They ran along the counter where the till sat. They curled up toward the empty loaf tin, and stopped there, and waited.

"Your red lines are moving," she said. "Red's for what is not. They're showing what's missing."

Mr. Pemberton looked at them for a long time. His mouth went a bit crooked.

"Well," he said. "I'll be." He folded the map carefully along its own creases and handed it back. "And what now, mapmaker?"

Juno looked at the door with its thorough spider. Then she looked at the red lines, which were all pointing the same way, at the one thing on the map she had not drawn.

"Now," she said, "we knock."

Juno knocked three times. The door swung open on a woman with flour on her eyebrows and a rolling pin in her hand.

"We're closed," she said. "Have been for years."

"You're not closed," said Juno. "You're just waiting."

The baker stared at her. Then she stared at Mr. Pemberton, who was holding his hat like a nervous visitor.

"I remember you," she said slowly. "You used to come in with a little girl. Tuesdays. You always bought the same loaf."

Mr. Pemberton nodded. "The Tuesday bread," he said. "My daughter called it that. She's grown now. Moved away."

"I've been baking it every Tuesday," said the baker. "Just in case. Nobody ever asks."

Juno held up the map. The red lines had turned into a single red arrow, pointing straight at the baker's oven.

"Well," said the baker. "You'd better come in, then."

Inside, the bakery was dusty and warm. A tray of rolls sat cooling on the counter, though the oven was cold. The baker dusted off a chair for Mr. Pemberton.

"I can't promise it's as good as it was," she said.

"It will be," said Mr. Pemberton.

The baker cut a slice and handed it to him. He took a bite, and his eyes went wide and wet at the same time.

"That's it," he said. "That's the one."

Juno watched the red lines on the map fade to nothing. Then she folded it up and put it in her pocket.

The baker untied her apron. "I suppose I'd better open up," she said. "Just for this morning."

***

Back at the shop, the brass bell was quiet. Juno set the map on the counter and smoothed it flat. The red lines were gone. Only the black pencil marks were left, ordinary and true.

Oda was sorting pencils into the tin. She didn't look up.

"Did he find it?" she asked.

"He found the bread," said Juno. "And the baker found someone to ask."

"Good," said Oda. "Put your pencils away."

Juno slid the black and red pencils into the tin. They clinked against the others. She reached for the blue pencil, then stopped herself.

"Not yet," she said quietly.

Oda smiled at the tin. Then she reached into her apron pocket and pulled out a small, paper-wrapped parcel.

"What's that?" asked Juno.

"A sharpener," said Oda. "For the blue pencil. When you're ready."

Juno held the parcel. It was lighter than it looked.

"Thank you," she said.

The shop settled into its evening quiet. Somewhere on Corvid Lane, a bakery door stood open for the first time in years, and the smell of Tuesday bread drifted down the street. Juno put the parcel in her pocket, next to the map, and waited for the next customer.
