Mara woke because her blanket had stopped being warm. She sat up. The window was silver with frost, and the whole village lay quiet outside, not a single chimney puffing. Even the weather vane on the barn roof hung still.

She ran to the kitchen. The stove was stone cold, but Grandma's old copper kettle sat on it, humming. A thin line of steam curled from its spout, though no fire had touched it all night.

"Grandma?" Mara whispered. No answer. Grandma's bed was empty, the quilt folded back, her slippers gone.

The kettle hummed louder, a low buzzing tune. Then it spoke, in a voice like a whistle through a keyhole. "The warm night was taken. The wind stole it and knotted it high. Go past the orchard. Follow my steam."

Mara pulled on her coat and boots. She lifted the kettle by its handle. It was warm, warmer than any kettle should be on a cold stove. Steam curled forward like a pointing finger.

Outside, the village shivered. Windows were dark. The baker's cat sat on a doorstep with its fur puffed up, and it mewed at Mara as she passed.

"I know," she told it. "I'm going."

The kettle tugged her toward the orchard gate. Behind the last apple tree, a path she had never noticed before wound up the hill. The steam pulled her along it.

***

The path climbed through frost-bitten grass. The kettle's steam stretched ahead like a silver thread, and Mara followed it up the hillside. Her breath came out in white puffs, but the kettle stayed warm in her hands.

At the top stood a thorn tree, black and twisty against the pale sky. High in its branches, something shivered. It looked like a bundle of air, if air could be bundled, and it glowed faintly gold. Warmth rolled off it in waves, but the wind whipped around the tree, snatching the warmth away before it could reach the ground.

"That's the warm night," Mara breathed.

The kettle's steam pointed straight at the bundle. Then the kettle spoke again, its voice small. "Wind knots what it steals. You must loosen it. But the wind will not let you near."

Mara took a step toward the tree. A gust shoved her back, cold and sharp. She tried again, and the wind swirled around her, scattering the steam into rags.

"Please," she called to the bundle. "Come down!"

The bundle shivered harder. The wind laughed, a thin whistling sound. Mara set the kettle down and rubbed her cold hands. The thorn tree's branches creaked, and the bundle swung just out of reach, glowing like a lost lantern.

"I'll think of something," she said. The kettle's shine had already begun to dull in the cold.

Mara tried a song first. It was one her grandmother sang while kneading dough, slow and warm and full of long vowels. She sang it toward the thorn tree with her chin lifted. The bundle of warm night swayed, as if listening. Then a gust tore the song out of the air and flung it down the hill in pieces. "Rude," Mara said.

She tried offerings instead. A wool mitten, a heel of bread, a smooth grey stone from her pocket. Each one she laid on the cold ground beneath the tree. The wind rolled the stone away and flipped the mitten inside out. The bread it simply left, which felt worse than taking it.

"What does wind even want?" Mara asked.

The kettle sat beside her, dull as a pot now, its steam gone thin. "Warmth," it said. "Wind is always cold and always hungry. It stole the warm night to keep itself company."

"Then I'll give it something else to hold." Mara gathered dry grass and twisted it into a little nest. She held the nest up high. The wind snatched it, shook it, and dropped it in the stream. Mara sat down hard on the frozen ground.

"I'm not strong enough," she said.

"No," the kettle agreed, in its small honest voice. "Not that way."

Far down the hill, a dog barked. Then someone was coming up the path, a girl in a patched coat, leading a shaggy grey dog and a flock that spilled around her like wool. The dog trotted straight to Mara and sniffed her boots.

"You're the girl with the talking kettle," the girl said. "I'm Tansy. I keep the sheep on this hill, and my sheep won't settle. Something up here is wrong."

***

Mara showed Tansy the bundle glowing in the thorn tree. Tansy squinted at it for a long moment, then nodded, as if she had expected exactly this.

"My grandmother says warm air is like milk," Tansy said. "You can't grab it. You have to let it come to you."

"How?"

"You brew it." Tansy pointed at the cold spring bubbling out of the hill's foot. "Water from there. It's the coldest in the valley. Cold water pulls warmth to it, slow and steady. My grandmother does it with bread dough. Same idea."

Mara stared at her. "You want me to make tea out of the night."

"Warm tea," Tansy said. "For a cold wind. Makes sense to me."

The kettle made a small hopeful sound. Together they carried it down to the spring and filled it to the brim. The water was so cold it ached Mara's fingers. Tansy built a little fire from dry twigs, patient as a shepherd, and Mara set the kettle over it.

"Now we wait," Tansy said. "And we don't snatch."

They waited. The wind circled them, tugging at their coats, whistling to be let in. Mara kept her hands still. Slowly the kettle began to hum, then to glow, then to shine again, brighter than before. Steam rose from it in a soft plume, and the plume did not scatter. It drifted toward the thorn tree, straight and sure, like a thread.

The bundle in the branches leaned down to meet it.

"It's working," Mara whispered.

"Don't get greedy," Tansy said. "Brewing takes the whole night."

Mara looked up at the dark branches and the shivering gold. "Then we climb."

Tansy went up first, roped to Mara with a length of shepherd's cord. "Test every branch," she said. "If it bends, it bends. You want to know before it breaks."

The thorn tree was older than the village and twice as stubborn. Mara climbed slowly, and the kettle swung from her belt, still steaming. Below her the fire was a small orange dot.

Halfway up, the wind found them. It shoved at her shoulder. Her foot slipped off a branch and she swung out over the dark with her heart in her mouth. The shepherd's cord pulled tight and held.

"Got you," Tansy called. "Come back to the trunk. One branch at a time."

Mara hauled herself in. Her hands were shaking, so she pressed them flat against the bark until they stopped. "I'm all right."

"You're not," Tansy said. "But you're climbing anyway. That's the useful part."

Higher up, the thorns grew close and pale, and the bundle of warm air shivered in the topmost fork. It looked like a lantern with no frame, a knot of soft gold, humming as if it were dreaming of summer. The kettle's steam reached toward it and the gold leaned down.

Then the wind kicked the branch. Mara grabbed for the trunk and the kettle struck the wood hard. A crack ran through the clay, and hot water began to leak out.

She froze. There was enough brew left for one cup, maybe. Not enough to climb down and up again.

"Mara!" Tansy's voice came up through the dark. "What's broken?"

"The kettle." Mara's voice cracked too. "If I hold it together, I can't climb. If I climb, it empties."

A long pause. "Then don't climb," Tansy said. "Sit still and finish."

Mara looked at the gold above her, so close, and at the branch that would not hold her weight twice. She pressed the crack with her thumb, cupped her other hand under it, and climbed one last branch with no hands free at all. Her arms ached and her boots scraped on the bark. The kettle poured warmth onto her sleeve.

At the fork, the shivering bundle bowed. Mara tipped the last of the tea into it and let go of the crack.

***

The warm air drank. That was the only word for it. The gold swelled, softened, and unknotted itself like a rope loosened after a long day.

Then the night changed. Warmth came down through the branches in a slow wave. It smelled of cut hay and rain and something sweet. Far off, the wind stopped shoving and began to breathe.

Mara sat in the fork with her cracked kettle in her lap, and all around her the thorn tree went quiet. The cold left her fingers. Her shivering stopped.

"You did it," Tansy said, climbing up beside her. "And you're still in one piece."

"The kettle isn't." Mara held it out. The crack ran from rim to belly, and the last of the water dripped from it.

Tansy turned it over. "My grandmother mends pots with pine sap and a hot stone. It isn't pretty, but it holds tea. I'll show you in the morning."

They climbed down and walked back through the orchard. Every window had light in it now, and every chimney had smoke. Somewhere ahead, a door banged open and a woman laughed at the warmth.

The village smelled of tea. Someone, it seemed, had been too glad to sleep to stay in bed.

Mara set the kettle on the stove and it hummed, faint and scratchy, but humming. She did not mind the crack. She had climbed the tree with no hands free and had not let go.

When the sky went grey at the edges, she asked Tansy to stay for breakfast. "I owe you a cup," she said. "A whole one."
