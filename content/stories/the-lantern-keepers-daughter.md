Mira's father kept the lanterns. Every evening he climbed the mountain path and lit them one by one, all the way to the summit. The lanterns glowed like a string of fallen stars, and the village slept easy beneath them.

But that morning, Papa could not get out of bed. His cough shook the whole room. Mira brought him tea and pulled the blanket to his chin.

"The lanterns," he whispered. "Who will light the lanterns?"

Mira's stomach went tight. She had never climbed the path alone. She had never climbed it at all.

"I will," she said.

Her mother stopped in the doorway. "Mira, it's dark up there."

"I know." Mira's voice came out smaller than she wanted. "But the village needs the light."

Papa's hand found hers. "Take the long match. And take this." He pressed a small brass whistle into her palm. "If you're ever truly lost, blow it. I'll hear."

Mira tucked the whistle into her pocket. She kissed his forehead and went to the door.

At the edge of the village, she looked up. The mountain was a black shape against the stars. The path curled into it like a thread. She could not see the first lantern.

She lit her own small lantern and stepped onto the path.

***

The dark was not empty. It was full of small sounds. A twig snapped under Mira's boot, and she jumped. Wind brushed her cheek like a cold hand.

"It's only wind," she told herself. Her voice sounded loud and alone.

The path climbed between tall pines. Their branches whispered together. Mira kept her eyes on her lantern's little circle of light.

She walked and walked. The village lights fell away below her. The dark grew thicker.

Then two eyes glowed beside the path. Mira froze. The eyes blinked, and a shape stepped out. It was a fox, red as a coal, with a white-tipped tail.

"Good evening," said the fox.

Mira's breath caught. "You can talk."

"I can," said the fox. "And you can climb. But you're going the wrong way, aren't you?"

"I'm lighting the lanterns. My father is sick."

The fox sat down and wrapped its tail around its paws. "The lanterns won't light tonight. Not unless the moon wakes up."

"The moon?" Mira looked up. The sky was full of stars, but no moon. She hadn't noticed before.

"She's asleep," said the fox. "In a silver pool at the top. Everything's dark until she wakes."

Mira's heart beat fast. "How do I wake her?"

"That's the puzzle," said the fox. "I know the way up. I could guide you. But you have to trust me, and the path is steep."

The old path looked blacker than ever. Mira thought of her father's whistle in her pocket.

"All right," she said. "Lead the way."

The fox turned and trotted into the dark. Mira held her lantern high and followed.

The fox left the path without warning, slipping between two grey rocks. Mira stopped. “Wait. The path goes straight up.”

“The path goes straight up to the bridge,” said the fox. “And the bridge is broken. We go around.”

Mira looked at the dark gap between the rocks. She thought of her father’s warm hand on her shoulder. Then she followed.

The way was narrow and full of roots. Her lantern lit only a few steps ahead. The fox’s tail bobbed like a small red flame. After a while, the ground opened into a clearing with a low cave mouth.

“Someone’s home,” said Mira. A thin line of smoke curled out of the rock.

Inside, an old woman sat by a fire. Her hair was as white as the moon should have been. “You’re late,” she said. “The moon went to sleep at dusk. She does that when nobody watches.”

“Can you wake her?” asked Mira.

“Me? No.” The old woman stirred the fire. “But you can. Here’s the riddle. What is full when it is empty, and empty when it is full?”

Mira frowned. “That doesn’t make sense.”

“Riddles aren’t supposed to make sense,” said the fox. “They’re supposed to make you think.”

The old woman handed Mira a small silver cup. “Take this. You’ll know when to use it.” Then she pointed deeper into the cave. “That way. Don’t stop until you reach the top.”

Mira turned to thank her, but the old woman was already asleep. The fire crackled. The fox nudged her hand. “Come on. Night’s not getting any shorter.”

They left the cave and climbed again. The path turned to gravel, then to stone. Mira’s legs ached. Her lantern flickered. She said the riddle over and over under her breath. Full when empty. Empty when full.

***

At the top of the gravel path stood a bridge. It was made of old rope and crooked planks, and it swayed in the wind. Below it, a gorge dropped into blackness. Mira could hear water far down, rushing like a crowd of whispers.

“The bridge is rickety,” said the fox. “But it’s the only way.”

Mira took one step onto the first plank. It creaked. She froze. “What if it breaks?”

“It won’t,” said the fox.

“How do you know?”

“I don’t,” said the fox. “But I know you can cross it. That’s different.”

Mira looked at the fox. Its eyes were bright and calm. She thought of her father, who climbed this mountain every night without a fox. He must have been afraid too. Maybe being brave meant going anyway.

“Stay close,” she said.

The fox stepped onto the bridge beside her. “One plank at a time. Don’t look down.”

Mira held her lantern in one hand and the rope rail in the other. The bridge tilted. Her foot slipped. She caught herself. The fox pressed against her ankle, warm and solid. “You’re all right. Next plank.”

Halfway across, the wind gusted. The bridge swung sideways. Mira’s lantern nearly went out. She stopped and breathed. The riddle came back to her. Full when empty. Empty when full. She didn’t understand it, but she held it like a hand.

“Keep going,” said the fox. “You’re almost there.”

Mira took the last steps. Her boots hit solid rock. She fell to her knees, shaking. The fox sat beside her and licked her hand. “See? You trusted me.”

“I trusted myself,” said Mira. She stood up. Above them, the mountain peak was silver with starlight. Somewhere up there, the moon was waiting.

The path ended at a wide flat rock. In the middle of it, a pool of water lay still as glass. The moon floated in the pool, round and white, but its eyes were closed. It was asleep.

“There it is,” whispered the fox. “Fast asleep. It’s been like this for three nights.”

Mira knelt by the pool. The water was cold, and the moon’s light rippled under her hand. “How do I wake it?”

“With the riddle,” said the fox. “Full when empty. Empty when full. What is it?”

Mira thought hard. Full when empty. Empty when full. She looked around the summit. Rocks, stars, the pool. Her lantern. The lantern was full of light, but the glass around it was empty of everything else. No, that wasn’t it.

Then she thought of her father. Every night he filled the village lanterns with oil. When the lantern was empty, he filled it. When it was full, it gave light. But the riddle said full when empty, empty when full. That was backwards.

“Maybe it’s not about filling,” she said slowly. “Maybe it’s about being empty so something else can come in.”

She looked at the moon in the pool. The moon was full of light, but it was empty of waking. It was empty of morning. It was empty of being seen.

“The moon is full of light,” she said, “but it’s empty of the sky. It needs to be empty so the night can fill it.”

“Say it to the moon,” said the fox.

Mira leaned over the pool. “You are full when you are empty,” she said. “And empty when you are full.”

For a moment, nothing happened. Then the moon’s eyes opened. They were silver, and they looked right at her. The pool shivered. The moon rose out of the water, slow and bright, and lifted into the sky. The whole mountain turned silver.

“You did it,” said the fox.

Mira laughed. She felt warm all over, even in the cold wind. “We did it.”

***

The fox led her down the mountain by a shorter path. Her lantern was still lit, but now the moon lit everything else. The rocks were soft gray. The trees had shadows. Mira wasn’t afraid.

At the bottom, the fox stopped. “I’ll leave you here,” it said. “Your village is that way.”

“Will I see you again?” asked Mira.

“Maybe,” said the fox. “When you need a guide.” It turned and trotted into the dark. Mira watched until she couldn’t see it anymore.

Her house was quiet. Her father was awake, propped on his pillow. He saw her lantern first, then her face. “You went up,” he said. “You went all the way up.”

“I did,” said Mira. She sat on the edge of his bed. “The moon was asleep. I woke it.”

He didn’t laugh. He just looked at her. “How did you know what to do?”

“A fox helped me,” she said. “And the riddle. Full when empty. Empty when full. It was about the moon, but it was about me too. I was empty of courage, so I had to fill myself up.”

Her father smiled. “You were never empty of courage,” he said. “You just had to find it in the dark.”

Mira looked out the window. The moon shone over the village. The lanterns would be lit tomorrow. She would light them. And she would not be afraid of the dark anymore.

“Go to sleep,” said her father. “You’ve earned it.”

Mira lay down in her own bed. The moon was still there, watching. She closed her eyes and felt brave.
