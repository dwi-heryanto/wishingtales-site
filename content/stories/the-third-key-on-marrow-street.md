Ivy first noticed the lock because it was new. The old brass padlock on the clockmaker's shop had hung there for as long as she could remember, green with weather, its keyhole stuffed with cobweb. Now a steel lock the color of a gun barrel held the door shut, and the hasp had been screwed in fresh, the wood around it pale where the drill had bitten.

She stood on the pavement with her school bag sliding off one shoulder and stared at it. The shop window was filmed with dust, but behind the dust she could see a light. Not daylight. A low amber glow, steady, the kind a lamp makes when someone has turned it down but not out.

"That's not right," she said to nobody. The street was empty at four in the afternoon, which was normal for Pellam Row. It was a short street of narrow shops and one pub, and most of them shut by three.

That night she came back. From the corner by the bakery she could see the light through the window, warmer now, moving slightly as if something inside were breathing. She counted the windows above the shop: all dark. The flat had been empty since before she was born, or so her mother said.

At home she asked over dinner. "Who owns the clockmaker's now?"

Her mother didn't look up from the potatoes. "Nobody. It's been shut for years."

"There's a light on."

"Faulty wiring, probably. Old buildings do that." Her mother's voice was flat in a way that meant the subject was closed. Ivy watched her stab a potato and decided not to push.

The next morning she went back with her phone and took a photograph of the lock. The steel was new enough to reflect the grey sky. She tried the handle. It didn't budge. She put her ear to the glass and heard, very faintly, a ticking. Not one clock. Many. A soft dry rustle of them, like rain on a tin roof, all slightly out of step.

***

Mrs. Halloran ran the bakery and knew everything about Pellam Row, so Ivy started there. The shop smelled of yeast and burnt sugar, and Mrs. Halloran was sliding a tray of buns onto the rack.

"The clockmaker's?" Mrs. Halloran wiped her hands on her apron. "That's Mr. Fenn's, isn't it? He owns half the street."

"Mr. Fenn died two years ago," Ivy said.

"Did he?" Mrs. Halloran looked genuinely surprised, then recovered. "Well, his family, then. Some nephew. I don't keep track."

"There's a light on at night."

"Is there?" The baker turned to the till and began counting coins with great care. "Can't say I've noticed. You want a bun, love?"

Ivy took the bun because refusing would have been strange, and ate it on the pavement outside. She tried the newsagent next. Mr. Okafor was stacking magazines and did not meet her eyes.

"The clock shop? That's council property now. They're assessing it."

"The council put a new lock on?"

"Could be. Health and safety." He shrugged and went back to his magazines. "Ask your mother."

Her mother had already given her answer, and it had been a door closing. Ivy tried the pub. The landlord, a thin man called Bev, was polishing glasses and listening to the radio.

"Clockmaker's?" He set the glass down. "That's the old Weaver place. Belongs to the Weaver family. Always has."

"But there's nobody called Weaver here."

"There's nobody called Weaver here that you'd know," Bev said, and then, as if he had said too much, he turned the radio up.

Ivy stood in the middle of Pellam Row and looked at the shops. Every one of them had given her a different owner. Fenn. The council. The Weavers. None of them had looked her in the eye. The street felt suddenly like a room where everyone had agreed on a story and she was the only one who hadn't been told.

That evening she stood across from the shop and watched the amber light move behind the glass, and she made a decision. Whatever was in there, it was not faulty wiring, and the people of Pellam Row knew exactly what it was.

The back of the shop gave onto a cobbled yard, and the yard gave onto a window that had been painted shut for so long the paint had cracked into a map of itself. Ivy worked the blade of her pen along the seam until a whole gray strip lifted away. The sash went up with a groan she felt in her teeth.

She went through feet first and hung a moment, then dropped onto a floor that was surprisingly clean.

And then she stood still, because the ticking was everywhere.

Clocks covered the walls. Clocks lay on the bench, clocks crowded the shelves three deep, and every one of them was going. Brass pendulums swung. A hundred small hands moved in their separate rhythms, and all of them, every face she could reach, pointed to the same time: four minutes past two.

"That's not possible," Ivy said, out loud, because the room was too quiet in every other way and she wanted to hear a voice in it.

She turned a slow circle. A longcase clock in the corner stood taller than her, its case dark with oil and years. Its face was four minutes past two. Beside it, a little carriage clock in a leather box, four minutes past two. A shelf of tin alarm clocks, all wound, all waiting at the same minute as though the whole room were holding its breath before a bell.

Dust lay thick on the bench and thin on the floors. Somebody walked here. Somebody wound these.

"Hello?" she called.

A lamp burned on the bench end, turned low, the amber light she had seen from the street. Beside it lay a pair of small pliers, a pot of oil, and a folded cloth with a watch laid open on it, mid-repair.

"You could have knocked," said a voice behind her.

Ivy spun. In the doorway to the front of the shop stood an old woman in a gray cardigan, holding a cup of tea in both hands as if the night were cold. She did not look frightened. She looked tired, and faintly amused, the way grown-ups look at weather.

"The door was locked," Ivy said, which sounded foolish the moment it was out.

"The window wasn't." The woman came in and set her cup down among the clocks. "That's on me. I've been meaning to fix the catch since spring."

"Who are you?"

"Edith Weaver." She said it plainly, no drama in it. "My father kept this shop. I've kept it since."

Ivy looked at the hundred faces, all agreeing. "They're all wrong. Every clock in here says four minutes past two."

"They're not wrong," Edith said. "They're stopped. Well, stopped on purpose. They're wound, and they run, and every morning I set them back where they belong."

"Why?"

Edith picked up the opened watch, turned it over in her fingers. "Because that's the minute the fire started. Nineteen sixty-one. My father was up late in this room with a lamp and a bad wire, and he smelled smoke before anyone on the row did. He went down the street banging on doors in his nightshirt. Woke the Fenns. Woke old Bev's mother. The whole terrace got out. He lost the shop, mind. Half of it burned. But nobody on Pellam Row died that night."

Ivy's hands had gone still at her sides. "So the clocks…"

"Are how we remember it," Edith said. "A minute past two is when he started knocking. Four minutes past is when the last of them was out. I keep the hour. It's not a mystery, girl. It's a memorial."

***

Ivy stood in the lamplight with her back to the longcase clock and tried to fit the street she knew into the story she had just been handed.

"The neighbors," she said slowly. "They knew. All of them. Bev, and the woman at the bakery, and the man at the ironmonger's. They all told me different things."

"Did they." Edith's mouth twitched. "What did they say?"

"Fenn's boy said it belonged to the council. The baker said the Weavers. Bev said the Weavers too, but he turned the radio up after."

"Ah." Edith set the watch down. "That'll be the pact, then. They've all promised not to say. Not to strangers, and not to me, in case I take it into my head to stop."

"Stop what?"

"The winding." Edith gestured at the room, at the hundred patient faces. "I'm not as quick as I was. Twice a week I come and wind them and set them back. It takes me most of a night now. So the row does it with me. The baker's lad does the shelves. Bev does the longcase, on account of his reach. The Fenns bring the oil. Nobody says a word about it, and nobody has, in forty years. It's just the thing Pellam Row does, quietly, when the shutters are down."

Ivy thought of the street in daylight, ordinary and a little shabby, and then of this room at night, humming with its single stubborn minute. "Why not just say? Why the lies?"

"Because it's not a story for the telling," Edith said. "If you explain a thing, people file it away and forget it. If it's a secret, they keep it alive." She looked at Ivy properly for the first time, taking in the muddy knees, the pen in her fist. "And because the ones who come asking, like you, they're the ones who might carry it after I've gone."

"Me?"

"You climbed through my window," Edith said. "That's either a burglar or a keeper. You haven't taken anything, so." She held out the pot of oil, small and warm in her palm. "The longcase wants a drop on the third arbor. Fetch the stool."

Ivy did not move. "You're not angry."

"I'm old," said Edith. "Anger's a young person's luxury. Take the oil."

And Ivy took it, and climbed the stool, and leaned into the dark of the tall case where the pendulum swung its slow brass breath, and learned the small careful work of keeping a minute from slipping.

Edith didn't explain the rest that night. She sent Ivy home with oil on her fingers and a warning not to come by the front. "The window's easier," she said. "Less explaining."

Ivy came back the next evening anyway. She told herself it was to return the stool she'd borrowed, though she hadn't borrowed it. The shop door was open a crack, and inside she heard voices, low and familiar. Mr. Pellow from the bakery. Mrs. Ash from the corner house. The butcher's boy, who was not a boy anymore but still got called that.

She stood in the doorway and listened.

"The escapement's losing four minutes a day," Edith was saying. "I can't get my hands into it anymore. The joints."

"Tell me what to do," said Mr. Pellow. "I've got flour on my hands and nowhere to be."

Ivy stepped inside. The clocks ticked around her, all those hands pointing at the same impossible hour, and the neighbors turned as if she'd caught them at something. Mrs. Ash had a cloth in her hand. The butcher's boy held a tiny screwdriver like it was a spoon.

"You're all in on it," Ivy said.

"We're not in on anything," said Mrs. Ash, too quickly. "We just look in. Now and then."

Edith sighed. "Sit down, Ivy. You've earned the sitting." She nodded at the others. "She climbed through the window. She oiled the longcase. She's one of us whether we like it or not."

The story came out in pieces, each neighbor adding a bit. Fifty years ago, the clockmaker, Edith's father, had been working late in this shop when a fire started in the chandler's next door. He'd heard it before anyone else, because he knew the sound of a clock running fast, and the heat had made the chandler's wall clock race. He'd run down the street banging on doors, and the whole row had gotten out before the roofs went. The chandler's didn't survive. The clockmaker's did, barely, and he spent his last years repairing the clocks of everyone who'd lost one.

"When he died, we didn't know what to do with the shop," Mr. Pellow said. "Edith was gone. We couldn't just let it rot."

"So you kept it," Ivy said.

"We kept winding them," said Mrs. Ash. "Once a week. Somebody different every week. That's why the stories never matched. We each only knew our bit."

"And the lock?"

"We changed it when the old one rusted," said the butcher's boy. "Didn't want anyone poking about. Not out of meanness. Out of…" He shrugged.

"Out of keeping," Edith said. "There's a difference between a door locked to hide something and a door locked to hold something together."

Ivy looked at the clocks. Fifty years of ticking, and every one of them still set to the same hour, the hour the fire had started. It wasn't a memorial to a man. It was a memorial to a night when the street had listened.

***

Edith found Ivy in the back room the next week, winding the longcase with the careful half-turns she'd been taught. The old woman watched from the doorway for a while before she spoke.

"You've got a feel for it," Edith said. "Most people rush the last turn. You don't."

"My hands are small," Ivy said. "It's easier to be careful."

"That's not why." Edith came in and sat on the stool, her knees cracking. "I'm going to tell you something, and then I'm going to ask you something, and you can say no to the asking."

Ivy set the winding key down.

"The others are getting old," Edith said. "Mr. Pellow's hands shake. Mrs. Ash can't climb the stool. The butcher's boy is moving to the city in the spring. In a few years there won't be enough of us to keep the rounds."

"So you want me to take a turn."

"I want you to take the list." Edith pulled a folded paper from her cardigan pocket. It was soft as cloth from years of folding. "Every keeper has kept the list. Who winds which clock, which week, what needs oil, what needs a new spring. My father started it. I've added to it. Now I'm asking you to hold it."

Ivy took the paper. It was heavier than it looked, all those names and dates in different hands, some faded, some fresh.

"Why me?"

"Because you noticed the lock," Edith said. "Because you didn't take anything. Because you came back." She stood, slowly. "And because when I'm gone, someone has to know which window sticks and which clock lies about the hour."

Ivy looked at the list. Her name wasn't on it yet. There was a space at the bottom, under the butcher's boy's neat print.

"I can't promise I'll be good at it," she said.

"Nobody's good at it at first," said Edith. "You get good by doing it badly for a while."

Ivy found the pen on the workbench, the same one she'd used that first night. She wrote her name in the space, small and careful, and Edith nodded as if a clock had just chimed the right hour.

"Tuesday next," Edith said. "The bakery clock first. Mr. Pellow will show you."

And Ivy folded the list into her pocket, and went out through the back window because the front door was still locked, and walked home under the streetlamps with the weight of it against her hip, ticking.
