Dad came home from Grandpa's house with a cardboard box. He set it on the kitchen table and pulled out a bowl. It was blue, with a chip on one side and a crack like a hair.

"Look what I found in the back of the cupboard," he said. His voice was soft, like he was holding something breakable.

Rosa picked it up. The bowl was lighter than it looked. The blue was faded, and the chip was rough under her thumb. "This is junk," she said. "Why did you bring it home?"

Dad didn't answer right away. He took the bowl back and turned it over. "It was Grandpa's," he said.

"So?" Rosa crossed her arms. "Grandpa had lots of stuff. We don't need a broken bowl."

Mum came in from the garden with mud on her knees. She saw the bowl and smiled. "Oh, I remember that," she said. "Your grandpa ate his soup from it every night."

Rosa frowned. "It's still junk," she said. But Dad was already filling the sink, washing the bowl with careful hands.

***

The bowl sat on the counter for two days. Rosa walked past it and didn't look. Then Mum put it on the top shelf, next to the good plates.

On Thursday, Mum made soup. She ladled it into the blue bowl and set it in the middle of the table. "Who wants to try Grandpa's bowl?" she asked.

Ben, who was six, raised his hand. "Me! Me!"

"It's just a bowl," Rosa said. "It doesn't make the soup special."

Mum smiled and served the soup. Ben slurped from the blue bowl. Dad took a spoonful from it too. They passed it back and forth, laughing.

Rosa ate her soup from a white bowl. It tasted fine. But she watched the blue bowl go around the table, and something in her chest felt tight.

"Rosa, do you want a turn?" Dad asked.

"No," she said. "I'm fine."

After dinner, she put her white bowl in the sink and went upstairs. She didn't look at the blue bowl on the top shelf.

On Saturday morning, Ben climbed onto a chair and reached for the blue bowl. He wanted to sail his little wooden boat in it.

“Ben, get down,” Rosa said from the doorway. “You’ll break something.”

“I’m being careful,” Ben said. He set the boat in the bowl’s wide mouth and gave it a push. The boat tipped, and Ben’s favorite marble — the green one with the white swirl — rolled out of his pocket and dropped in.

“Now look what you did,” Rosa said.

Ben fished the marble out. He held it up, and his eyes went wide. “Rosa. It’s warm.”

“It’s not warm. You’re imagining it.”

“Feel it.” He pressed the marble into her hand.

The marble was warm. Not hot, not from the sun. Warm like a pocket. And it smelled faintly of tomatoes and cut grass. Rosa knew that smell. It was Grandpa’s garden, the little patch behind his house where he grew fat tomatoes and let the mint run wild.

She dropped the marble. “It’s just a bowl,” she said, but her voice came out small.

Ben picked the marble back up and sniffed it. “It smells like Grandpa’s house,” he said happily. “Like the green part.”

“Give it here.” Rosa took the marble and rubbed it on her sleeve. The smell stayed. She put it on the counter and stepped back.

“Can I do it again?” Ben asked.

“No,” Rosa said. “Go play outside.”

Ben shrugged and went. The marble sat on the counter, warm and green, smelling of summer. Rosa stared at the blue bowl on its high shelf. The chip on its rim looked like a little smile now. She did not like that at all.

***

That night, when the house was quiet, Rosa came down in her socks. She carried the blue bowl to the sink and filled it to the brim with cold tap water.

“There,” she whispered. “You’re a bowl. You hold water. That’s all.”

The water sat in the bowl, plain and clear. Rosa waited. Nothing happened. She felt silly, standing in the dark in her socks.

Then she lifted the bowl and drank.

It was lemonade. Cold, sweet, with the sharp bite of real lemons and a little too much sugar, the way Grandpa always made it. Every summer, he squeezed the lemons on the back step and let her stir. She could almost hear him: “More sugar, Rosie. The secret is more sugar.”

Rosa set the bowl down hard. Water slopped over the rim and ran down the drain.

“Stop it,” she said to the bowl. Her voice cracked. “You’re not him. You’re not.”

She sat down on the kitchen floor with her back against the cupboard. Tears came, hot and sudden, and she pressed her face into her knees so no one would hear. She missed Grandpa so much it felt like a stone in her chest. The bowl was making it worse. The bowl was making her remember everything, all at once, and she couldn’t put it down.

A floorboard creaked. Dad stood in the doorway in his old gray shirt. He didn’t say anything. He just came and sat on the floor beside her and put his arm around her shoulders.

“It tastes like his lemonade,” Rosa said into his sleeve.

“I know,” Dad said quietly. “It always did.”

Dad reached up and lifted the blue bowl off the counter. He turned it over in his hands.

“See this chip?” he said, pointing to the jagged bite on the rim. “That happened the night I was seven. I dropped it. Grandpa said it made the bowl easier to hold.”

Rosa wiped her nose on her sleeve. “You dropped it?”

“I was trying to help with the dishes.” Dad smiled a little. “He never got rid of it. Every night, that bowl sat right there in the middle of the table. Soup, stew, cold noodles. Didn’t matter. He said food tasted better out of a bowl with a story.”

Rosa looked at the chip. It was smooth and pale, like a little scar.

“When we cleaned out his house,” Dad went on, “I found it in the cupboard. I almost threw it in the box for the charity shop. Then I thought about all those nights. Him in his chair. Me across from him. The bowl between us.”

“So you kept it,” Rosa said.

“I kept it.” Dad set the bowl on the floor between them. “I don’t know if it’s magic, Rosie. Maybe it’s just a bowl. But when I eat from it, I feel like he’s still at the table.”

Rosa traced the chip with her finger. “I don’t want to feel that.”

“I know,” Dad said gently. “It’s hard. But you don’t have to use it. It can just be here.”

Rosa looked at the bowl for a long moment. The kitchen was quiet. Then she said, “Maybe not every night.”

“Maybe not,” Dad agreed. He stood up and held out his hand. “Come on. Let’s put it back on the shelf for now.”

***

The next morning, Rosa came down to the kitchen before anyone else. The blue bowl was still on the shelf, chipped and ordinary. She took it down and set it on the table. Then she poured her cereal into it. The milk went in next, cold and white. She sat down and took a bite.

The cereal tasted like cereal. That was all. No lemonade. No garden. Just cornflakes and milk. Rosa felt something loosen in her chest.

Ben shuffled in, his hair sticking up. “Is that Grandpa’s bowl?” he asked.

“Yep,” Rosa said.

“Can I have mine in the green one?”

“Sure.”

Mum came in and poured her coffee. Dad followed, yawning. They all sat down together. Ben told a long story about a dream he’d had, something about a giant snail and a skateboard. Mum laughed so hard she had to put her cup down. Dad looked across the table at Rosa and smiled.

After breakfast, Rosa carried her bowl to the sink. She rinsed it carefully and dried it with the good towel. Then she put it back on the table, right in the middle.

“Not on the shelf?” Dad asked.

“Not on the shelf,” Rosa said. She looked at the chip. It caught the morning light. “It looks better here.”

Dad nodded. “It does.”

Rosa poured herself a second glass of water and sat back down. The blue bowl sat in front of her, empty and waiting. She thought about tomorrow’s breakfast. She thought about Grandpa. The stone in her chest was still there, but it was smaller now. It felt less like a stone and more like a smooth, warm pebble she could carry in her pocket.

“More sugar, Rosie,” she said softly to the bowl. “The secret is more sugar.”

The kitchen smelled like toast and coffee. Outside, the sun was coming up over the fence. Rosa smiled.
