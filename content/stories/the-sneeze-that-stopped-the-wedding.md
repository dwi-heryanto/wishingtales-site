Bertie was the youngest page boy at Willowbrook Hall, which meant he was also the shortest, the squeakiest, and the only one who had to stand on a stool to reach the good china. Today his job was simple. Carry two gold rings on a velvet cushion, walk slowly, and do not sneeze.

"Do not sneeze," said Mrs. Pemberly, the housekeeper, tapping the cushion with one finger. "Can you manage that, Bertie?"

"I never sneeze," said Bertie. Then he sneezed. The cushion tipped, and both rings plopped straight into the bowl of punch.

"Fish them out!" cried Mrs. Pemberly. "Quickly, before they turn the punch gold!"

Bertie rolled up his sleeve and fished. The rings were cold and slippery, and one of them slid down his arm and out again. The choir, practicing in the corner, stopped singing to watch. The flower arranger stopped arranging. Even the cat stopped licking its paw.

"Again," said Mrs. Pemberly, when both rings were safely back on the cushion. "Walk to the door and back. No sneezing."

Bertie walked. He held his breath. He thought about dry things: toast, sand, the inside of a biscuit tin. He reached the door and turned around, proud as a pigeon.

Then the flower arranger's lilies sent up a puff of pollen, and Bertie sneezed so hard his cap fell off.

The rings went up. The rings came down. One landed in the punch again. The other landed in the choir's hymn book, right on the word "amen."

"Well," said Mrs. Pemberly, "at least we know where they are." She sighed a long, whistly sigh. "Rehearsal is over. Go to bed, Bertie. Tomorrow you will not sneeze."

Bertie went to bed, but he did not sleep much.

***

The wedding day arrived sunny and loud, with church bells and folding chairs and a great many hats. Bertie stood at the front in his little blue coat, holding the cushion with both hands. The rings sat on it like two small, sleepy suns.

"Steady," whispered the groom, who was Bertie's uncle. "You're doing grand."

"I'm holding my breath," Bertie whispered back.

"All of it?"

"All of it."

The organ started. The doors opened. Everyone turned to look at the bride, who was Bertie's aunt, and who was smiling so wide it made the whole church feel warmer.

That was when the feather arrived. A long gray plume had come loose from a lady's hat in the third row, and the breeze from the open doors carried it down the aisle. It drifted. It swooped. It settled, light as a whisper, right on the tip of Bertie's nose.

Bertie's eyes went cross. His nose itched. He tried to think of toast, sand, biscuit tins.

"Don't," he said to himself. "Don't, don't, don't."

The feather tickled.

"Ah," said Bertie. "Ah. Ah. AHHHCHOO!"

The cushion flew up. The rings flew higher. Bertie clapped both hands over his mouth and watched them spin through the air like two tiny golden bees, and the whole church gasped at once.

One ring shot straight up in the air and came down with a soft plop into the wedding cake. It sank until only a little gold curve showed, like a worm coming up for air. The other ring bounced off a pillar, pinged off a hymn book, and disappeared into the big flower arrangement by the altar.

"The cake!" shouted a man in the back row. "It's in the cake!"

"The flowers!" shouted a lady in the front row. "Something went into the flowers!"

Nobody stayed in their seat. The whole church stood up at once, and the pews creaked like a ship at sea. Bertie's uncle hurried to the cake with his hands out, then stopped. "I can't put my fingers in there," he said. "It's a wedding cake. There are rules."

"There are no rules," said the baker, who was in the fourth row. "I made it. Dig."

So the groom dug, gently, with two fingers, while the icing sighed and slumped. In the flowers, a tall cousin was parting lilies like curtains. "Nothing yet!" she called. "Wait! There's a shine. There's a shine in the middle of the roses!"

"Which ring is that?" asked a small girl, standing on a chair.

"The big one," said the cousin. "No. The small one. Get me a fork."

"You can't use a fork on flowers," said the girl.

"Watch me," said the cousin.

***

Bertie stood by the altar with his empty cushion, and his ears went hot. He had one job. It was the only job. Hold the cushion, keep the rings, don't sneeze. He had done exactly one of those things.

Then his aunt the bride reached the altar. She looked at the cake, she looked at the flowers, she looked at her youngest page boy going redder and redder by the second. She did not look cross at all. She laughed, a big, bright laugh that rolled right down the church.

"Bertie," she said. "Have you still got the feather?"

"It's on the floor now," said Bertie. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I ruined your wedding."

"You ruined the cake," she said. "The cake was going to be ruined anyway. It's what cakes are for."

"The rings are lost," he said. "I lost the rings."

His aunt bent down so her eyes were level with his. "They're not lost," she said. "They're just early. They wanted to see the party first." She stood up and clapped her hands. "Everyone! We have a game. Two rings are hiding in this church. Whoever finds one gets a slice of cake and the first dance."

"I already found one!" said the baker, waving a fork with a ring on it.

"Then you get the first dance," said the aunt. "And you, Bertie, are on sneezing duty. If we get stuck, you sneeze again."

Bertie looked at his aunt. His ears cooled down. "I can sneeze," he said. "I'm very good at that."

The hunt went up and down the church like a happy wind. Guests crawled under pews, peeked into flowerpots, and lifted the corners of the long white carpet. Bertie stayed on the aisle with his cushion, in case the rings came home by themselves.

"Found one!" shouted the baker, and held up a ring with a bit of frosting on it. "It was in the wedding cake, next to the little sugar bride."

"One down, one to go!" called his aunt.

Then the choir master gave a shout from the front. "Here! In the hymn books! It was wedged between verse two and verse three!" The second ring came out with a page stuck to it, and the choir master had to blow the page off before he handed it over.

The aunt clapped her hands. "The rings are found! Now the vows, please." The organist played a grand, rumbling chord, and the bride and groom stepped up to the altar. Bertie carried the cushion to the front and set the rings down on it, one on each side. His hands were steady. His nose was not.

It itched. It tickled. It twitched. A tiny feather from the hat, the one that had started all this, was floating on the air again. Bertie tried to look at it sideways. He tried to think of something else. He thought of turnips. The feather landed on the tip of his nose.

"Nnnn..." said Bertie.

"Hold it, Bertie!" whispered the groom.

"...TCHOO!"

The sneeze was small, but it was aimed straight at the tall white candle on the altar. The flame bent, wobbled, and went out. A little curl of smoke went up, and the whole church went quiet.

"Oh no," said Bertie. "The unity candle. I blew out the unity candle."

The groom looked at the candle. Then he looked at Bertie. Then he pulled a matchbox out of his pocket and struck a match. "Good thing I'm a matchmaker's son," he said, and lit the candle again with a grin. "A candle just needs a second start. So do we all."

***

The ceremony went on. Vows were said. The rings went on the right fingers, and this time nobody sneezed at all. When the organ struck up the wedding march, the guests threw handfuls of paper confetti that the ushers had been saving in baskets.

Bertie stood at the church door with his cushion, and the aunt came out into the sunshine. "I've had an idea," she said, and held up a fresh handful. "Sneeze-shaped confetti. For next time."

She tore a paper twist into a tiny zigzag shape and let it flutter down. The groom laughed. "You can't plan a sneeze, Aunt."

"You can plan the confetti," she said. "You can plan the party. You just can't plan everything."

Bertie held his cushion high above his head so the paper bits would land on it, not on him. A zigzag of green paper landed on the middle of the cushion, right where the rings had been. His nose twitched once, twice, three times. He held his breath. It passed.

"Best page boy ever," said the groom, and shook his hand.

"Best sneezer," said the aunt, and kissed his forehead.

"I didn't do anything," said Bertie.

"You held the cushion," she said. "You held it high. That's the job."

Bertie walked down the church steps with the cushion up, the confetti zigzags drifting around him, and his ears warm and cool at the same time.
