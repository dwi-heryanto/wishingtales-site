The wind came over the hill like a train that had lost its track. It shoved the gate open and shook the hawthorn hedge until the berries rattled. Tilly pressed herself into the corner of the field and bleated for her mother.

No answer came. The flock had scattered down the lane at the first crack of thunder, and the ewes had run with the rest. Tilly was six weeks old, and she had never been alone before.

Rain hit the grass in hard little pins. "Mama," she called, in the small voice of a lamb. "Mama, where did you go?"

The hedge only hissed back at her. Far off, thunder grumbled and moved away. Tilly's fleece was soaked through to the skin, and her legs were trembling, and she did not know which way the farm was.

She tried walking. Three steps, then she stopped, because the darkness past the gate looked like a mouth. She tried again. The mud pulled at her hooves, and she sat down in it and cried.

It was a thin, wavering sound. A cry that had given up hope of being heard and was carrying on out of habit.

Something rustled in the ditch. Tilly went still, and her heart made a small drum of itself.

A snuffling came through the wet grass. Then a voice, dry and unhurried, said, "That is a very sad noise for such a small animal. Are you a lamb?"

"I am," said Tilly. "Are you a monster?"

"I am a hedgehog," said the voice, a little offended. "My name is Bramble."

***

Bramble pushed out of the ditch and shook himself. Water flew off his spines in tiny arcs. He was wearing a lantern hung from a strap across his back, and it swung gently and threw a warm yellow circle on the grass.

Tilly stared at the light. It was the best thing she had ever seen.

"You have a lamp," she said.

"I do. I walk the field at night and check the drains," said Bramble. "The lamp is for the holes I might fall into." He peered up at her. "And you look like a hole I might fall into. What are you doing out here?"

"I lost my mother," said Tilly. "The wind took everyone, and now I don't know where the farm is."

Bramble turned slowly on the spot, sniffing. "I know the farm," he said. "Big red barn. Warm straw. A dog who is all bark and no teeth. You belong there?"

"Yes!" Tilly stood up so fast that she slipped in the mud and had to catch herself.

"Then I will walk you home," said Bramble. "It is not far. Only past the stream and through the bramble patch." He paused, and his small dark eye blinked. "Well. Past my bramble patch. I would not go through anyone else's."

"Is it dangerous?" asked Tilly.

"The stream is a little dangerous," said Bramble. "The bramble patch is mine, so it is friendly. And the owl will be asleep, so we must be quiet."

Tilly looked at the little lantern and at the small, sturdy hedgehog under it. Then she looked at the wide dark field, which had stopped looking like a mouth and now looked only like a field.

"Lead the way," she said.

The stream ran behind the lower field, quick and black and noisy. Bramble stopped at the bank and held the lantern high.

"Stepping stones," he said. "Six of them. Count as you go and do not look down at the water."

"I always look down at the water," said Tilly.

"Then look at my lantern instead," said Bramble. He stepped onto the first stone. It barely wobbled. "One."

Tilly put a hoof on the second stone. Her legs were long and shaky after the storm. "Two," she said. The stone shifted under her and she squeaked.

"Two," agreed Bramble. "Very good. Now three."

The third stone was slick with wet moss. Tilly landed on it hard and her back hoof slid off the edge. Cold water splashed her belly. She scrambled, and her shoulder knocked the lantern clean out of Bramble's paw.

It fell. For one terrible moment the flame dropped toward the stream.

Bramble dived. He was round and slow on dry ground, but in the air he was very quick. He caught the lantern by its handle and landed on the stone with all four feet bunched together.

The flame was still there. It had gone small and blue and frightened, but it was there.

"Oh," said Tilly. "Oh, I'm sorry."

"No harm," said Bramble, but his voice came out thin. He cupped a paw around the glass. "It only got a scare. Walk on, and this time, put your hoof where I put my foot."

They crossed the last three stones one slow step at a time, and the lantern stayed lit.

***

On the far bank stood an old oak, and in the oak sat an owl.

The owl was asleep. Tilly could tell because of the soft, sawing sound coming from the hollow of the tree, like a very small person snoring into a pillow.

"We must be quiet," whispered Bramble. "Owls do not like being woken. They ask questions."

"What sort of questions?" whispered Tilly.

"The sort you cannot answer at night," said Bramble.

A feather drifted down from the hollow and landed on the path. It was grey and long and soft, and it glimmered faintly, as if it had kept a little of the moon.

Above them, one yellow eye opened. "Hedgehog," said the owl. Her voice was low and slow, not cross at all. "And a lamb. You are out late."

"She lost her flock," said Bramble. "I am taking her home. Your lantern is nearly out, though. The stream shook it."

The owl blinked her other eye open. "Then take my feather," she said. "Hold it to the glass when the flame is low. It will remember the light for you."

Tilly stepped forward before Bramble could stop her. "Thank you," she said. "We'll bring it back."

"Keep it," said the owl, and closed her eyes again. "I have plenty. And lamb, hold your head up on the path. You are taller than you think."

The snoring started up again. Bramble tucked the feather behind his ear and lifted the lantern.

"Well," he said. "That was the politest owl I have ever met."

The path ahead was a black tangle of bramble, thick as a hedge and twice as cross. Bramble held up the lantern, and the flame inside it crouched low and blue. "Feather," he said. "Now or never."

He pulled the grey feather from behind his ear and touched it to the glass. At first nothing happened. Then the feather warmed in his paw, and a small gold bead of light ran down its centre. The flame sat up. It stretched. It burned yellow again, bright enough to show every thorn.

"It worked," Tilly whispered.

"It remembered," said Bramble. "Just like the owl said."

He pushed into the brambles, holding the lantern high. Thorns snagged his spines and tugged at Tilly's wool, but the light showed the gaps between them. "Keep your head up," Bramble called back. "Like the owl told you."

Tilly lifted her chin and stepped high. A long bramble arched over the path, and she walked under it without a scratch.

"There's a gap," she said. "On your left. I can see it."

Bramble turned, and there it was: a low tunnel through the thorns, wide enough for a lamb and just wide enough for a hedgehog. "Lead on," he said.

So Tilly went first, and Bramble followed with the lantern swinging. The brambles closed behind them with a soft, scratchy sigh. Ahead, the dark began to thin. A pale shape rose out of the field: a farmhouse roof, and a barn beside it.

***

The farm gate stood open. Beyond it, a big sheep with a bell on her collar was running up and down the fence line, bleating into the dark. When she saw Tilly, she stopped dead.

"Tilly!" she called. "Tilly, is that you?"

Tilly broke into a run. "Mam!"

They met in the middle of the yard, and the mother sheep pressed her nose into Tilly's wool and would not let go. The rest of the flock crowded round, all talking at once.

Bramble stayed by the gate, lantern low. He was about to turn back into the dark when Tilly's head came up over the woolly crowd.

"Wait!" she said. "This is Bramble. He found me. He brought me all the way home."

The mother sheep looked at the small, spiny creature with the lantern. "Then you'd better come in," she said. "It's cold out there."

She nudged open the barn door with her nose. Inside, it smelled of hay and warm animals. Bramble stepped in, and the lantern lit the rafters and the straw.

"There's a corner by the feed bin," said the mother sheep. "No one uses it. You're welcome to it."

Bramble set down his lantern and looked around. A dry corner, a warm barn, and a lamb who kept looking back to make sure he was still there. He had never had a home with a roof before.

"I'll keep the lantern lit," he said. "For anyone else who gets lost."

Tilly smiled. "And I'll hold my head up," she said. "So you can find me."
