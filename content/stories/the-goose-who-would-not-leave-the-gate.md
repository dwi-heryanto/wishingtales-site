Maren found the goose at the farm gate on a Tuesday morning. It sat in the gravel with one wing crooked out, like a broken umbrella. The bird did not honk or hiss. It just looked at her with a dark eye and stayed put.

"You're not supposed to be here," Maren said. She crouched down. The goose's feathers were dusty, and one wing dragged at an odd angle. "That looks sore."

The goose tucked its head deeper into its neck. Maren held out her hand, palm up. After a long moment, the bird leaned forward and pressed its beak gently against her fingers.

"You can't stay by the gate," she told it. "The dogs will bark."

She scooped the goose up, careful of the hurt wing. It was lighter than she expected, warm and solid. The bird made a low sound, almost a sigh, and settled against her jacket. Maren carried it past the henhouse and the tractor shed. Her boots crunched on the frost.

Inside the barn, the air smelled of hay and old wood. Maren climbed the ladder to the hayloft, one hand holding the goose, the other gripping the rungs. At the top, she made a nest from loose straw and set the goose down.

"There," she said. "Quiet. Warm. No dogs."

The goose shuffled in the straw and closed its eyes.
***
That evening, Maren's father found her at the kitchen table with a bowl of crushed corn.

"What's that for?" he asked.

"A bird," Maren said, which was not exactly a lie.

Her mother looked up from the stove. "A hurt bird? We don't have room for hurt birds, Maren. The wildlife shelter takes them. They know what to do."

"She's not badly hurt," Maren said quickly. "Just tired."

"She?" Her father raised an eyebrow.

Maren said nothing. She took the bowl and slipped out the back door. The yard was dark, and the barn loomed like a big shadow. She climbed the ladder with the bowl in her teeth. The goose was awake, waiting. It ate the corn from her hand, one kernel at a time, and made small happy sounds.

"Don't worry," Maren whispered. "I won't let them send you away."

The goose nudged her palm with its beak. Maren stayed until the hayloft grew cold and the moon rose over the roof. Then she climbed down and went inside, and she did not tell her parents where the corn had gone.

Mornings became Maren's secret. She woke before the rooster and climbed the ladder with a pocket full of corn and a fistful of grass. The goose learned her footsteps and greeted her with a soft honk.

"Good morning to you too," Maren said, settling into the hay beside her. "You're getting louder. That's a good sign."

The goose ate from her hand and let Maren smooth the feathers along her back. The hurt wing stayed tucked tight against her side.

"I'm calling you Pip," Maren said one day. "Because you pip when you're happy. Listen to that."

Pip piped. Maren laughed out loud.

For four days they did this. Maren talked about school and her little brother and how quiet the house felt when her father worked late. Pip listened with her head tilted. When Maren stopped talking, the goose pressed close against her knee.

On the fifth morning, Pip was not waiting by the hay bale. She stood at the loft window, beak pressed to the glass, staring at the sky. When Maren came up, the goose turned and honked, impatient, and flapped her good wing.

"You want to go out there," Maren said softly.

Pip honked again. She paced the loft floor and pushed at the window with her chest.

"Soon," Maren said. "When your wing is strong."

Maren looked at the wing. It still drooped, and the feathers were ragged where they had healed crooked. She did not say what she was thinking.

***

On the sixth morning, Maren's little brother followed her to the barn. She did not hear him until the ladder creaked.

"What's in the hay?" he called. "Is it a cat?"

Pip honked, loud and clear, and the whole barn heard it. Boots crossed the yard. Maren's father climbed the ladder, and her mother came up behind him, and they all stood in the loft looking at the goose in the straw.

"Maren," her mother said, and her voice was patient and tired. "You know we said the shelter."

"She's better," Maren said. "She's much better. She eats and she talks and she's strong."

"Her wing isn't right," her father said gently. "Look at it. She'll never make it out there."

Maren's face went hot. "She will. She just needs time."

Her parents looked at each other. Her mother sighed. "One day, Maren. You have until tomorrow morning. If she can fly by then, she stays. If she can't, we take her to the shelter where they can help her properly."

"One day," Maren repeated.

"One," her father said. "Come on. Let her rest."

They climbed down. Maren stayed in the loft with Pip, and the goose settled against her. Tomorrow morning felt very close, and very far away.

Morning came gray and cold, and Maren was up before the sun. She carried Pip down to the yard and set her on the flat grass by the gate. "All right," she said. "Show me. Show me what you can do."

Pip stretched her neck and ran. Her good wing beat hard, once, twice, and for a moment her feet lifted off the ground. Then the hurt wing flapped weakly and she came down in a heap of feathers.

"Try again," Maren said. "Higher this time."

She tried again. And again. Maren held her hands out and lifted her as high as her arms could reach, and Pip beat and beat at the air, but the weak wing would not hold. She landed, and looked up at Maren with her bright eye, and honked softly, as if she was sorry.

Maren sat down in the wet grass. "It's not your fault," she said. "I know it isn't."

She tried everything she could think of. She ran along beside Pip, flapping her own arms, and Pip ran too, and neither of them left the ground. She climbed onto the fence post and called, and Pip stretched her wings and could not reach her. The sun climbed higher, and the shadow of the gate grew short.

By noon Maren knew. She sat with Pip in her lap and stroked the soft gray feathers and said nothing at all for a long time.

Her father came out and stood beside her. "Maren," he said quietly.

"I know," she said. Her voice wobbled. "I know."

She got up and carried Pip to the truck. The goose pressed against her the whole way, warm and heavy, and Maren held her and did not let go. "They'll fix your wing," she whispered. "And then you'll fly. Just not here."

***

That night Maren lay awake and listened to the empty loft above the barn. The house was quiet. Her mother came in and sat on the edge of the bed.

"You did a good thing," her mother said. "Letting her go where she can be helped. That's harder than keeping her."

"It doesn't feel good," Maren said.

"No," her mother agreed. "It doesn't." She kissed her and turned out the light.

Maren woke at dawn. Something had woken her, some sound out in the yard. She went to the window and looked out.

There, in the first pink light, stood Pip. Her wing was wrapped in white tape, and she was flapping both wings wide, and she was rising. Up she went, wobbling, over the fence, over the barn, higher and higher. The shelter man stood at the gate with his hands on his hips, laughing.

Maren ran outside in her bare feet. "Pip!" she shouted. "Pip!"

The goose circled once. She honked, loud and clear, the same honk she had given in the hayloft. Then she turned south and flew, small and sure, until she was only a speck against the morning.

Maren stood in the wet grass a long while. The yard was not empty anymore. The hens clucked, her father called from the porch, and somewhere a cat was singing. She found that she was smiling, and that she was not as lonely as she had been.

She went inside for breakfast.
