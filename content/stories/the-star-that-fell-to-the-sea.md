Mira was on the seawall when the star fell. It dropped out of the black sky like a spark from a fire, slow and bright, and it landed somewhere out in the bay. For a moment the whole water flashed silver. Then it was dark again, and the only sound was the tide slapping the stones.

She ran all the way home, her sandals slapping the path. Her grandmother was still up, mending a net by the stove.

"Gran," Mira said, breathless. "A star fell into the bay. I saw it."

Her grandmother set down her needle. "Are you sure it was a star?"

"It was bright and it fell," Mira said. "It hit the water near the sandbar."

Her grandmother looked at the clock on the wall. It was nearly midnight. "Then you must go and get it," she said.

"Get it?" Mira stared. "It's a star."

"Stars that fall must be carried home before dawn. If they stay in the water, they go out. Then the sky has one less light." Her grandmother stood and went to the cupboard. "Take the boat. Take your cloak. And take this." She handed Mira a small clay pot with a lid. "In case it's hot."

Mira looked at the pot. "Is it dangerous?"

"Not if you're careful," her grandmother said. "Now go. The tide turns in a few hours."

***

Mira rowed out into the bay. The oars creaked, and the water was so still she could hear her own breathing. Ahead, the sandbar glowed faintly, like a coal left in a fire.

She pulled the boat up on the wet sand. There it was, a star, no bigger than a melon, covered in soft cracks of light. It hummed, very low, like a cat. Heat came off it in waves.

Mira knelt and reached out. The heat stopped her hand before she touched it. It was too hot to hold.

"Right," she said, and took off her cloak. She wrapped it around the star and lifted. The cloak smoked a little, but it held. The star was warm and heavy, like a loaf just out of the oven.

She carried it to the boat and set it on the boards. The star pulsed, once, twice, and the whole boat lit up. Mira pushed off and took up the oars. The tide had already begun to turn.

"Hold on," she told the star. "We're going home."

The star was heavier than a melon now. It was heavier than a sack of meal, and the boat sat low in the water. Mira pulled on the oars, and the tide pulled back. Every wave that slapped the bow seemed to push her toward the sandbar instead of home.

Her arms burned. Her hands were slippery on the wood. She looked back and the harbour lights were small as pinpricks, and the sky was already grey at the edge.

"You're a heavy thing," she said to the star, and the star hummed, low and sorry.

A wave came over the side and sloshed around her feet. The star flickered. Mira shipped the oars and sat still, breathing hard. The shore was closer than the harbour. She could put the star back on the sand and row home light, and be in bed before dawn. No one would know.

She looked at the dark water between her and the harbour. Then she looked at the star. It pulsed, faint and tired, like a candle guttering.

"I can't," she whispered. "It's too far."

Her grandmother's voice came into her head, dry and certain. Stars that fall must be carried home before dawn. That was all. No one said it would be easy.

Mira put her hand on the cloak. The star's warmth came through, steady as a heartbeat. She thought of the bay going dark, of every night after this one with no star in it. Then she picked up the oars.

"Right," she said, and her voice shook a little. "One more pull."

***

Pull. Pull. The words kept time with her heart. Her grandmother had told her the story once, on a night when the fire was low and the wind was loud outside.

The first star-carrier was a girl named Senna, who lived when the world was younger and the nights were longer. A star fell into the salt marsh near her village, and Senna went out in a reed boat with no lantern.

The old ones said she carried it for a whole night, and the tide fought her, and her arms gave out twice. But she never let go. She rowed until the sky turned pink, and then she lifted the star and it rose home.

"Why did she do it?" Mira had asked.

Her grandmother had smiled. "Because the dark is for resting in, not for being lost in. Someone has to carry the light back."

Mira tightened her grip on the oars. The memory of that story felt like a hand on her shoulder. The first star-carrier had no motor, no map, no one to help. Just a reed boat and a stubborn heart.

A wave hit the bow and the boat spun. The star slid sideways, and Mira grabbed the cloak before it could tip. Water splashed her face, cold and sharp.

"Hold on," she said again, to the star and to herself. The harbour was closer now. She could see the lamp on the pier, a small yellow eye in the grey.

Her arms were shaking, but she rowed. Four pulls, then five. The star hummed louder, as if it could feel the shore.

The grey light spread across the water, and the harbour lamp blinked twice, as if waking up. Mira blinked too. Her hands were raw and her back ached, but the boat was moving. Then a wave rose up out of the dawn, tall and smooth and silent.

"No," she breathed.

The wave broke over the bow. Water poured in, and the cloak lifted, and the star slid toward the edge. Mira dropped the oars and lunged. Her fingers caught the cloak's corner, then the bundle, then she hugged it to her chest. Cold water soaked her to the knees, but the star stayed.

"Got you," she gasped. "I've got you."

Behind her, an oar drifted away, turning slowly in the swell. Mira grabbed the other one and paddled hard on one side, then the other, the way her grandmother had taught her to steer a heavy boat. The star hummed against her ribs, warm and steady. The pier lamp grew brighter. She could hear gulls now, and the slap of water under the boards.

Her grandmother stood on the pier in her thick coat, holding a lantern high. "Mira!" she called. "This way! Come this way!"

"I'm coming!" Mira shouted back. Her voice cracked, but it carried.

The boat nosed into the harbour wall with a soft thump. Mira tied the rope with fingers that would not stop shaking. Then she sat down hard on the seat and laughed, once, out of breath.

***

Her grandmother helped her up the ladder. The sky was pale gold now, and the sea had gone quiet, as if it had finished its work for the night.

"Well," said her grandmother, looking at the glowing bundle. "You did it."

"It nearly got away," Mira said.

"Nearly is not the same as did." Her grandmother nodded at the bundle. "Go on, then. Before the sun is up."

Mira climbed the harbour steps to the highest point of the wall. The star's light was soft now, almost shy. She lifted the cloak away, and the star floated up from her hands like a bubble of warm air. Up it went, past the lamp, past the gulls, until it was a small bright point among the fading others.

Then it winked, once, and settled into its place in the sky.

Mira stood there until the gold turned to blue. When she looked down, a small pebble lay in the palm of her hand, smooth and round and faintly warm, as if it had been sitting in the sun. She closed her fingers around it.

"Breakfast?" said her grandmother from below.

"Breakfast," Mira agreed.

That night she put the pebble on her windowsill. The bay was dark outside, the way it always was. But Mira lay in bed and felt the small warmth of it, and she knew the dark was only resting. Somewhere out there, a light was always being carried home.
