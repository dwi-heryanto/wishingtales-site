Sena was mending a torn hem when the light above her loom went thin. She looked up from her needle and saw a star fade, not blink, not cloud over, but dim like a candle lowered in its glass.

"Did you see that?" she asked Old Wick, who was carding wool by the fire.

The old weaver did not look up. "I saw nothing, and I see most things."

Sena set down her needle. Above the roofbeam, the sky was scattered with stars, and three of them were guttering. One winked out while she watched.

"They are going out," she said. "Stars do not go out."

Old Wick put down her wool. "Sit," she said. "There is an old story, and I have never told it to a girl who noticed."

Sena sat on the wool sacks, and the fire snapped.

"The sky is a cloth," Old Wick said. "A great woven cloth, and the stars are stitches. Long ago a Star Weaver lived on the mountain and kept it mended. Every loose thread, every pulled stitch, she set to rights. But she has not been seen for a hundred years, and thread runs out, girl. All thread runs out."

Sena looked up at the dimming sky. "Then someone must mend it."

"Someone must," Old Wick agreed, and she picked up her wool again as if the matter were closed.

Sena did not sleep that night. She packed a lantern, a loaf, and her mother's bone shuttle, and she was gone before the rooster.

***

The mountain path was long and full of small stones. Sena climbed until her calves ached and the village looked like a scatter of seeds below her.

Near the top she found a cave mouth with a curtain of ivy across it. She pushed the ivy aside and stepped in.

Inside was a loom. It was larger than any loom she had ever seen, tall as a house, and every beam was carved with stars. But the warp threads hung slack and grey, and the shuttle lay on the floor in a drift of dust.

"Hello?" Sena called. Her voice came back to her from the cave's far corners.

Nobody answered.

On the loom's bench lay a folded paper, weighted with a smooth black stone. Sena unfolded it and held it to her lantern.

The letters were thin and careful, a weaver's hand.

"The thread has run out," it said. "I have gone to find more. Mend nothing you cannot finish. A torn sky is better than a knot."

Sena read it twice. Then she looked up at the slack grey threads and the grey beams and the dust, and she felt very small in that tall cold cave.

"But the stars are going out," she said to the empty room. "Who is going to finish them?"

Sena set her lantern on the bench and climbed the loom's side like a ladder. The warp threads were cold and dry, like old grass. She had no thread of her own, so she pulled a loose end from the hem of her cloak and tried to knot it to the first star. "Just one," she whispered. "One star to hold the rest." The knot slipped. She tied it again, tighter, and wove the thread across two grey warps. For a moment a small light flickered in the pattern. Then it went out. The thread snapped, and the star above it dimmed. Two more stars beside it faded, then a third. Sena scrambled down. "No, no, no," she said, and her hands shook. The cave felt darker than before. She could hear the night wind outside, and it sounded like a sigh. "I made it worse," she said. "I made it worse." She sat on the cold floor and stared at the three empty places in the sky. Then something tapped a hoof on stone behind her.

***

Sena turned. A mountain goat stood in the cave mouth, grey as the rock and shaggy as a winter coat. Its horns curved back like two small moons. "You are the weaver's apprentice," said the goat. It was not a question. Sena wiped her eyes. "I am," she said. "And I have made a mess." The goat came closer and sniffed the frayed thread in her hand. "Bramble," it said. "I keep the upper paths. I know where the thread comes from." Sena stood up fast. "There is more thread?" she asked. "There is moonlight," said Bramble. "It pools in a spring above the cloud line. The Star Weaver fills her spools there." Sena looked at the dark sky and then at the goat. "Can you take me?" she said. Bramble turned and flicked its tail. "Follow. And do not slip." The path was narrow and pale in the dark. Bramble's hooves clicked sure and steady, and Sena put her feet where the goat put its feet. They climbed until the air tasted thin and cold. Then the path opened onto a small bowl of rock, and in the middle of it lay a spring. The water was not blue or black. It was silver, and it moved slowly, like thread unwinding from a spool. "Moonlight," said Bramble. "Take only what you can carry." Sena knelt and dipped her fingers in. The water clung to her skin, cool and bright, and when she lifted her hand a long silver strand came with it.

Sena wound the silver strand around her wrist and followed Bramble down the mountain. The thread stayed cool and bright, and it hummed a little, like a plucked string. Back at the loom, she lit her lamp and began to weave.

Her fingers were quick. The first new stars came fast, and each one popped into the dark like a spark catching. "Look," she said. "It works. It truly works."

Bramble settled by the loom and chewed quietly. "Do not hurry," said the goat.

Sena did not listen. She wove faster and faster, pulling the moonlight tight. Her shoulder began to ache, and her eyes grew heavy. Then her hand shook, and a strand slipped loose. The little patch of stars she had made sagged and dimmed.

"Steady," said Bramble. "The thread will not hold if you rush it."

"I know," said Sena, but she yawned and fumbled the shuttle. Another strand slipped. She set it down and rubbed her tired hands.

Bramble rose and came close to the loom. "Give me the end," said the goat. "I will hold it steady while you weave. A weaver needs a helper when the night is long."

Sena stared at the goat. "Goats do not weave."

"I am not weaving," said Bramble. "I am holding. There is a difference."

Sena tied the silver end gently around Bramble's horn. The goat stood still as stone, and the thread ran taut and true. Now Sena could work slowly, one stitch at a time. The stars came back, one after another, patient and bright.

***

All night Sena wove, and all night Bramble held the thread without moving. The sky above the village filled with small clear lights. Sena's back ached and her fingers grew rough, but she kept her pace slow and even. One stitch. Then another.

"How many are left?" she asked near the end.

"Three," said Bramble. "Then two. Then one."

The last star was the hardest. It sat at the edge of the dark where the cloth had worn thin. Sena breathed out and set the stitch, and the star caught and held. It glowed soft and steady, like a candle in a window.

"There," she whispered. "That is the last one."

Bramble shook the silver thread from its horn. "You did not rush it," said the goat. "That is why it holds."

The sky turned grey, then pink, then gold. Down in the village, a rooster called, and smoke rose from the chimneys. Sena untied the thread and rolled up the leftover moonlight. It was only a little, but she saved it in a small clay pot.

She walked home through the waking streets. Her mother met her at the door with a puzzled face. "You look tired, Sena. Where have you been?"

"Weaving," said Sena. "Slowly."

That morning she sat at her loom with the ordinary wool. Her hands were sore, so she worked slower than usual, and the cloth came out neater than any she had made before. She looked up at the pale morning sky, where one last star winked out, and she smiled. Then she picked up her shuttle and began again.
