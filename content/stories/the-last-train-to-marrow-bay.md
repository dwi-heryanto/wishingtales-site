The last train of the night always smelled of cold metal and someone else's perfume. Nell worked her way down the aisle with a black bag over her shoulder. She collected cups and wrappers, her mind already on the walk home. The carriage lights flickered once, twice, then settled into their tired hum.

Seat 14B was empty, but the floor beneath it was not. A satchel lay on its side, leather worn soft at the corners. Its strap curled like a sleeping snake.

She crouched and lifted it. Heavy. Not lost-property heavy, but full of something that mattered. Inside she found a folded ticket, printed on thick card, and a letter sealed with wax the colour of old blood. The ticket read: *Marrow Bay. One way. Annual service.* Under the destination, in smaller type, a warning: *Passengers are advised to travel light.*

Nell turned the ticket over. On the back, in pencil, someone had written a date from thirty years ago.

"You're not supposed to be down here after midnight."

The voice came from the far end of the carriage. An old conductor stood in the doorway, cap in his hands, hair white as platform frost. He wore a uniform that had gone out of style before Nell was born.

"I'm just finishing up," she said. "Someone left this."

He crossed the aisle slowly, his boots silent on the worn carpet. When he saw the ticket, his face changed. Not fear, exactly. Something older.

"Marrow Bay," he said. "Haven't seen one of those in a while."

"Is it a real place?"

"It's real enough." He took the ticket from her fingers and held it up to the light. "The line runs once a year. Tonight, as it happens. Takes something in exchange, though. It always does."

"What kind of something?"

"Whatever you've got that you can't get back." He handed the ticket back to her. "Best put it where you found it. Some doors are better left shut."

Nell looked down at the satchel. The letter was still inside, unopened. Someone had been meant to read it. Someone had missed their train.

"What happens to the people who ride it?" she asked.

"Some come back," the conductor said. "Some don't. The ones who do are never quite the same." He turned to go, then stopped. "You're a cleaner, not a passenger. Remember that."

He disappeared through the door at the end of the carriage. The lights flickered again, and this time they stayed dim for a moment longer than they should have. Nell stood alone in the aisle, the satchel heavy in her hands, the ticket warm against her palm.

She should have put it back. She knew that. But the letter had a name on it, and the name was not one she recognised, and the date was thirty years old. Someone had been waiting a long time.

Nell slung her bag over her shoulder and walked back to the door the conductor had used. Beyond it, the platform was empty. The clock above the ticket office said 11:58.

***

The conductor was standing by the engine, smoking a pipe that smelled of burnt sugar. Nell climbed down onto the platform and walked toward him. He did not look surprised to see her.

"You're still here," she said.

"I'm always here on the last night of the month." He tapped his pipe against the rail. "You've got the satchel."

"I've got the ticket."

"Same thing, in the end."

Nell held up the ticket. "I want to know what happens to the people who go."

"I told you. Some come back. Some don't."

"That's not an answer."

He sighed and set the pipe down on the buffer. "You're a stubborn one. You'll need that." He nodded at the ticket. "The train leaves at midnight. It stops at Marrow Bay for exactly one hour. Then it comes back. If you're on it, you come back. If you're not..." He shrugged. "Well. You're not."

"What does it take? The exchange?"

"A memory. Something you love. Something you'd rather keep." He looked at her closely. "You've got a home, haven't you? Parents? A dog?"

"A cat."

"Then you've got something to lose."

Nell thought about the letter in the satchel. The name on it. The date. Thirty years was a long time to wait for someone who never came.

"What if I don't come back?" she asked.

"Then someone else will find your satchel." He picked up his pipe. "That's how it works."

The clock above the ticket office clicked to 11:59. The train hummed, a low note that vibrated through the platform and up through the soles of Nell's shoes.

"You don't have to do this," the conductor said. "You could go home. Forget you found it."

"No," Nell said. "I couldn't."

She climbed back onto the train. The carriage was different now. The seats were plush and red, the windows curtained in velvet. A lamp burned at the far end, and beneath it sat a boy about her age, knees drawn up, watching the door. He looked at her satchel, then at her face.

"You're not the conductor," he said.

"No," Nell said. "I'm a passenger."

The clock struck midnight. The train began to move.

***

The boy shifted along the seat to make room, though there was room enough for twenty. His coat had a torn pocket, and he kept one hand in it, as if guarding something.

"I'm Ivo," he said. "Ivo Marchetti. You're Nell."

"How do you know my name?"

"It's on your jacket." He nodded at the embroidered patch the depot gave its cleaners. "N. Harker. I'm not clever, just literate."

Nell sat down across from him and rested the satchel on her knees. Outside, the platform slid away and the lights of the depot thinned into a single orange dot, then nothing. In the dark glass she could see the carriage reflected whole: the red seats, the brass lamp, and the two of them, small and pale and very awake.

"You're looking for someone," Ivo said. It wasn't a question.

"An old woman. She left this satchel on my train." Nell would not say the rest yet. "Why are you here?"

Ivo took his hand out of his pocket. He was holding a photograph, creased soft as cloth. A woman stood in front of a white house, squinting into the sun. "My aunt. She went to Marrow Bay when I was six. She sent postcards for a year, then stopped." He turned the photo over. On the back, in pencil: *The town keeps what it's given.*

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know. I've had nine years to think about it."

The train jolted. Nell grabbed the armrest, and when she looked up the curtains had changed color, from green to a dark, dusty gold. The lamp over their heads had risen two inches from its bracket. Neither of them had touched it.

"Did you see that?" she asked.

"I've been seeing it since I got on." Ivo's voice was steady, which somehow made it worse. "The carriage was blue when I boarded. Then it was green. Now it's red." He looked at his own hands. "I think it's deciding what kind of train to be. What kind of night this is."

The floor tilted gently, as if the whole carriage were a boat riding a slow swell. Through the window Nell saw no fields, no hedges, no fences. There was only a grey bright nothing, like fog lit from inside, and it moved past them without wind.

"My aunt's postcards," Ivo said. "One of them had a picture of the harbor. Boats with lamps on the masts. She wrote that the bread there was the best she'd ever eaten, and she was never coming back." He folded the photograph along its creases. "I didn't believe the last part. I still don't."

"I think you should."

Nell opened the satchel. Inside, beside the letter, was a tin of cough drops, a hairpin, and a small paper bag with one hard pear in it, still good. She showed him the ticket. Marrow Bay, 11:58, one way.

Ivo read it twice. "There's no date."

"There's never a date," Nell said. "That's the point."

The train slowed. Not the way trains slow, with brakes and a long sigh. It slowed the way a held breath slows, all at once and quietly. The windows went dark, and in the dark something pale drifted past: a signpost, or the suggestion of one, its lettering too far to read.

A chime sounded, two notes, low then high. The door at the end of the carriage swung open on a platform Nell could not remember being there before.

***

The station was small and clean and lit by gas lamps that hissed. A wooden sign hung over the platform, and the letters on it read MARROW BAY in gold that had not faded. Behind the station house stood a row of roofs, chimneys, a steeple. Beyond them, faintly, the sound of water against stone.

Nell stood in the doorway. The air smelled of salt and coal smoke and something sweet, like apples turning.

"You first," Ivo said, behind her.

"Why me?"

"Because you're the one with the satchel."

She stepped down. Her boots rang on the platform, and the sound was wrong — too bright, too long, echoing as if the platform were much larger than it looked. The stationmaster came out of the office, a thin man in a grey coat, carrying a ledger under one arm.

"Passengers," he said. He did not smile. "One at a time. The fare is not money."

"What is it, then?" Nell asked.

He opened the ledger. Its pages were not written on. They were dimpled, as if things had been pressed into them and then removed. "A memory of home," he said. "That's the toll. One each. It's taken when you step off. Nothing else will do."

Ivo laughed, one short breath. "That's not a real thing."

"Everything here is a real thing," the stationmaster said. "That's the trouble with it."

Nell thought of her father's kitchen, the window over the sink, the way the light came in at seven in summer. She thought of the hall carpet, worn in a track from the door to the stairs. Which one would go? Would she know it was missing?

"What if I refuse?" she said.

"Then you stay on the train." The stationmaster closed the ledger. "It goes back. You go back with it. But the satchel stays here, and the woman it belongs to waits another year."

"And you?" Nell turned to Ivo. "Are you getting off?"

He looked at the rooftops, the steeple, the pale lamps burning in the windows. His jaw worked. "I came nine years for this," he said. "I'll pay."

"Then go first," Nell said. "Before I lose my nerve."

Ivo stepped down. For a moment nothing happened. Then his face changed — not pain, exactly, but a searching look, a hand patting a pocket for something that had been there. He blinked at Nell as if she were a stranger he was trying to place.

"Do I know you?" he asked.

"You did," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "A minute ago."

He nodded slowly, accepting it, and walked past her toward the town without looking back. The stationmaster wrote nothing in his ledger, but the page dimpled under his thumb.

Nell stood on the step. The train hummed behind her. The satchel was heavy in her hands, and the town waited, lit and quiet, full of people who had been given away.

She stepped down.

***

The platform was empty, but the town was not. Lamps burned behind lace curtains, and somewhere a piano played the same eight bars over and over, patient as a tide. Nell walked with the satchel against her chest. The strap was worn smooth where a hand had held it for years.

At the end of the street stood a house with a blue door and a brass knocker shaped like a fish. She knew it before she knew why. The satchel had a name inked inside the flap — *M. Tallow* — and the knocker was a fish, and the house smelled of salt and cold tea.

She knocked. The door opened on an old woman in a grey cardigan, her hair pinned up with a pencil. She looked at the satchel, and her face did something complicated.

"That's mine," she said. Her voice was rusty. "I left it on the train. I was going to come back for it."

"How long ago?" Nell asked.

"A while." The woman stepped back. "You'd better come in."

The front room was crowded with clocks, none of them ticking. Mrs. Tallow cleared a chair and sat down heavily. "I came here to find my sister," she said. "She'd gone missing. The ticket was in my hand before I knew I'd bought it. I stepped off, and I paid, and I've been here ever since."

"What did you pay?"

"The way home." Mrs. Tallow smiled without warmth. "I can't remember my street. I can't remember the color of my own front door. I know I had one. That's all."

Nell set the satchel on the table. Inside were a hairbrush, a folded photograph, and a letter never sent. The photograph showed two girls on a beach, squinting into the sun.

"Your sister?" Nell asked.

"I think so." Mrs. Tallow touched the picture. "She's the one I came for. I found her here. She didn't know me. She'd paid too."

A clock on the mantel shivered, though no one had wound it. Nell felt the town pressing in, listening.

"The train leaves at dawn," she said. "I have to go back."

"Then go." Mrs. Tallow closed the satchel. "And don't look at the lamps too long. They'll make you think you live here."

***

At the station, the stationmaster waited with his ledger open. The train stood ready, its windows bright. Ivo was already aboard — Nell saw him through the glass, sitting alone, staring at his own hands as if he'd forgotten what they were for.

"The fare," the stationmaster said. "You know it now."

"A memory of home," Nell said.

"The town chooses. It's always something small. Something you'd have lost anyway, in time."

She thought of her mother's kitchen, the chipped blue bowl, the window that stuck in summer. She tried to hold it all. The stationmaster wrote her name in the ledger, and the page drank the ink.

"Step up," he said.

She climbed the steps. At the top she turned. Marrow Bay lay behind her, lamplit and quiet, and Mrs. Tallow stood on the platform with the satchel in her arms, waiting for a train that ran once a year. Nell raised a hand. The old woman did not wave back.

Inside, the carriage was warm. Ivo looked up as she sat down across from him. "Do I know you?" he asked.

"No," she said. "Not anymore."

The train pulled out. Nell pressed her forehead to the glass and watched the town slide away. She reached for the memory of home and found a gap where something had been — a kitchen, she thought, but the details were gone. She could not remember the bowl. She could not remember the window. She knew only that she had lost them, and that the loss was the fare.

The train ran through the dark. The dark was ordinary. By the time the first grey light came up over the fields, Marrow Bay was on the map again — a small dot on the line, with a name, and people who had paid to be there. Nell closed her eyes. She knew why.

When she stepped off at her own station, her mother was waiting. "You look tired," her mother said.

"Long shift," Nell said. "There was a satchel. I gave it back."

Her mother nodded, and they walked home together through the early morning. Nell did not remember the blue bowl. But she remembered the way.
