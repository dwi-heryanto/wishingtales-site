Ansel Voss had a notebook with a green cover and a pencil he sharpened with a paring knife. He counted things. Not because anyone asked him to, but because numbers stayed put when everything else in the building moved. The elevator groaned and stopped between floors. The pipes sang at three in the morning. The front door stuck in humid weather. But the stairs? The stairs were reliable. Or they had been, until Tuesday.

He was coming down from the fifth floor, where Mrs. Kowalski paid him two dollars to carry her recycling to the chute. His sneakers made a soft thud on each step, and he counted under his breath, the way he always did. "Five, four, three." At the fourth step from the bottom, his foot landed lower than it should have. Not by much. An inch, maybe two. He stopped and looked down. The step looked normal. Gray concrete, a black rubber tread worn smooth in the middle. He stepped back up and down again. Same dip. He crouched and pressed his palm against the tread. Solid.

"You're blocking the stairs," said a voice behind him.

Ansel turned. A girl about his age stood on the landing above, arms folded. She had a canvas bag over one shoulder and a scab on her knee that she hadn't bothered to cover. He'd seen her before, in the hallway, but they'd never spoken.

"I'm counting," he said.

"Counting what?"

"The steps. This one's wrong." He pointed. "It's lower than it should be. By about an inch and a half."

The girl came down and stood beside him. She didn't laugh. That was the first thing Ansel noticed about Ivy Chen. She looked at the step the way he did, like it was a problem to be solved and not a joke to be made. "Show me," she said.

He did. She stepped up and down three times, then squatted and ran her finger along the edge of the tread. "It's not just lower," she said. "It's wider. The gap between this step and the next one is bigger than the others."

Ansel pulled out his notebook and wrote: *Fourth step from bottom. Lower. Wider. Check all stairs.*

"You keep a notebook?" Ivy asked.

"You don't?"

She shrugged. "I keep things in my head. But I'll help you count. I'm Ivy. I live on three."

"Ansel. Five."

They shook hands, which felt formal and a little silly, but neither of them laughed.

***

The next afternoon they started at the top of the building and worked their way down, one stairwell at a time. Ansel counted the steps between each landing and wrote the number in his notebook. Ivy counted too, her lips moving silently, and then they compared.

"Floor six to five," Ansel said. "Eighteen steps."

"Eighteen," Ivy agreed.

"Five to four. Eighteen."

"Eighteen."

They went on. The numbers matched every time, until they reached the fourth floor landing and started down toward three.

"One," Ansel said. "Two. Three. Four." He stopped. "Wait." He stepped back up and counted again. "Five. Six. Seven." He looked at Ivy. "I got nineteen that time."

Ivy's brow furrowed. "That's impossible. You can't get two different numbers from the same stairs."

"Unless the stairs are doing something," Ansel said. He wrote the two counts side by side in his notebook: *18. 19.* Then he drew a line between them and wrote: *Fourth step?*

"Let's do it together," Ivy said. She took his pencil and drew a small mark on the wall at the fourth floor landing, right at the edge of the top step. "We start here. We count out loud. Both of us. No stopping."

They started down. "One. Two. Three. Four." At the fourth step, Ansel felt the same dip, the same slight widening under his foot. "Five. Six. Seven." They reached the third floor landing. "Eighteen," they said together.

Ansel stared at the step they'd just left. "We counted eighteen, but I felt the fourth step twice."

"What do you mean, twice?"

"Like it was there, and then it was there again. Like the stair doubled under my foot." He sat down on the landing and opened his notebook to a fresh page. "The fourth step is the one that changes. Every time we count, it's the fourth step from the top of that flight that does something strange."

Ivy sat beside him, her back against the wall. "My grandmother used to say this building had a memory. She said it remembered everyone who ever lived here, and sometimes it got confused about how many of them there were."

"That's not helpful," Ansel said.

"No," Ivy agreed. "But it's something." She pulled a granola bar from her bag and broke it in half, handing him the bigger piece. "So what do we do?"

Ansel chewed and thought. "We find out what the building remembers," he said. "And why it thinks there's one more step than there should be."

The basement office smelled of wet cardboard and old cigarettes. Ansel had been down here once before, helping the super carry up a dead potted fern. Now he crouched beside a filing cabinet that had rusted shut along one seam. Ivy worked the drawer with a butter knife from her bag.

"Who keeps ledgers anymore?" she said, straining.

"The building does." Ansel pointed at the shelves. "Look. One book for every year. They go back to 1923."

The drawer gave with a shriek. Inside, spiral notebooks stacked like bricks, each spine labeled in faded ink: tenants, repairs, mail. Ivy pulled the most recent tenant book and opened it flat on the floor. Names in careful columns. Apartment numbers. Dates in, dates out.

"The fourth floor," Ansel said, tracing. "Four-A, four-B, four-C. Then four-D."

"Four-D is scratched out." Ivy's finger hovered over a line of ink so heavily worked it had torn the page. Beneath the scribble, faint as breath, a name survived: *Elsbeth Crane*. In beside it, a date from 1961. Out beside it, nothing.

Ansel's neck prickled. "No move-out date. She never left."

"People always leave," Ivy said. "Or they die."

They climbed to the lobby together. The mailboxes hung in a grid beside the front door, brass doors, tiny keyholes. Four-A. Four-B. Four-C. Then a blank plate, screwed in where a name should be, polished to nothing.

"Someone erased her," Ansel said quietly. "But they couldn't pry off the plate."

Ivy ran her thumb over the blank metal. "Or she's still getting her mail." She said it like a joke. Neither of them laughed.

The super found them there and did not seem surprised. He was a thin, tired man named Gorse who wore his keys on a carabiner. "You found the hole in the count," he said.

"You know about the extra step?" Ansel asked.

Gorse leaned against the doorframe. "Everyone who lives here long enough knows. Four-D was sealed in sixty-one. There was an accident on the stairs. After that the building stopped agreeing with itself about how many floors it had." He looked at the blank mailbox. "You want to leave it sealed. That's my advice, and I've been here thirty years."

"What happened to her?" Ivy asked.

"Nobody's sure. That's the trouble with it." He jingled his keys and walked away, and the sound of them faded up the stairs.

***

The key to four-D hung on a nail behind the furnace, tagged with a scrap of masking tape gone brown. Ansel found it on his third trip to the basement, and he did not tell Gorse.

"We shouldn't," Ivy said, at the door. But she was already holding her phone up for light, and her voice had the flat sound of someone who has decided.

The lock turned hard, then gave. The door swung inward on a room that smelled of dust and cold, and beneath the dust, faintly, of lavender.

Nothing had been packed. A table stood set for one, a plate and a cup and a folded napkin gone grey. A coat hung on a hook by the door. Books lined a shelf in the order someone had left them, and a cup of tea had dried to a dark ring at the bottom. The whole apartment held its breath, waiting for a woman to come back and finish her evening.

"It's like a photograph," Ivy whispered.

Ansel crossed to the window. Daylight pressed against the glass, but the room stayed dim, as though the light had to ask permission. He turned and saw the stair.

It stood where no stair should be, in the corner by the closet, a narrow wooden flight going down into the floor. No opening in the boards. No door. The top step simply met the floorboards and continued beneath them, and the steps were worn in the middle, as if years of feet had used them.

"That's impossible," Ivy said. "We're on the fourth floor. There's nothing under this floor but the third."

Ansel knelt and laid his palm on the top step. It was cold, colder than the room, and when he pressed, he felt the wood give slightly, like a held breath. His notebook was in his hand without his remembering pulling it out. He wrote: *four-D. stair down. no stair down.* The pencil shook.

"We should go," he said.

"We came all this way." Ivy stepped past him onto the stair. "It's just stairs, Ansel. It's the building being confused."

"Ivy."

She looked back at him, and for a moment she looked very young and very certain, and then she took the second step. The light from her phone slid down with her, and the dust in the air turned gold, and then the stairwell swallowed the gold and she was three steps down, four, and the cold came up through the floorboards like water.

"Ivy, come back."

Her voice floated up, thin and puzzled. "There's a landing. There's a door down here. Ansel, there's a door."

He heard her knock. He heard the knock answered by something that was not an echo. Then the light went out, and the stairwell was only a dark seam in the floor, and Ivy did not say anything else.

Ansel stood at the top of the extra stair for a long time. The building made its small night noises around him: a pipe ticking, the elevator settling, someone’s television bleeding through a wall. None of it reached the dark seam at his feet. He called Ivy’s name twice. The second time his voice cracked, and the darkness did not even bother to return it.

He knelt and pressed his palm to the floorboards beside the stair. They were cold enough to ache. His notebook lay open on the landing, and he read his own handwriting as if it belonged to a stranger: *four-D. stair down. no stair down.* The fourth stair was the one that doubled. He had counted it four times, and every time the building had given him an extra step, patient as a cat.

“Ivy, I’m coming,” he said.

He put his foot on the first step. The stairwell breathed out, and the air smelled of old plaster and something sweeter underneath, like a fruit left too long in a drawer. He took the second step. His phone light found nothing. The walls had gone smooth and dark, and the numbers chalked on them were not numbers he knew.

Somewhere below, a door closed softly.

Ansel stopped. He had read enough stories to know what a door closing meant. He had also read enough to know that the story did not care whether he was brave. The building was not confused, as Ivy had said. It was counting, and it had counted her in.

He climbed back to the landing and sat down hard. His hands were shaking, so he wrote in the notebook to steady them. *The stair takes one. The stair gives one. The count must balance.* He crossed out the second line. Then he uncrossed it. The building had kept a ledger for eighty years, and every ledger had a rule.

“Think,” he said out loud. His voice sounded small in the stairwell. “It’s a stair. It only works one way.”

Down. You went down it. You did not come up it, because coming up was not the direction it wanted. The fourth stair doubled. If you went down, it took you further than you meant to go. If you went up—

He looked at the seam of dark in the floor. The stair went down into the building’s throat, and there was no stair going up, not in the ordinary way. But a stair counted backward was still a stair.

“Ansel?” Ivy’s voice came up from very far away, thin and tired. “Ansel, it’s cold. It’s so cold, and I can’t find the—”

The rest of it fell into the dark.

He stood. His heart was going like a bird in a box, but his hands were steady now. He turned around and set his back to the stairwell, facing the landing, and he put his heel on the fourth step behind him. Then he climbed up by going down, one backward step at a time, counting aloud the way he had counted the first night.

“Four,” he said. “Three. Two.”

With every step the cold climbed higher, and something in his chest loosened, like a knot being untied. He thought of his mother’s kitchen, and the smell of it went out of his mind like a candle. He thought of the first day of school, and the name of his teacher slid away. He kept climbing. The stair drank what it was given and let him pass.

***

On the seventh backward step, Ansel found the landing. It was the same landing, but not the same. The walls were papered in a pattern of small grey leaves, and a lamp burned on a table by the door, and the door was open. Ivy sat on the floor just inside it with her arms around her knees. She looked up at him, and her face was pale and streaked, and she was there.

“You came down,” she said.

“I came up,” he said. “Backward.”

She stared at him. Then she laughed, a wet, cracked laugh, and he pulled her to her feet. The room behind her was full of furniture with sheets over it, and the sheets were grey with dust, and the lamp on the table had no cord. He did not look at it for long.

“What did it take?” she asked.

He opened his mouth to answer and found a hole where something had been. He knew it had been important. He could feel the shape of it, like a tooth gone. “I don’t remember,” he said. “Something. It doesn’t matter.”

“Ansel.”

“It doesn’t matter,” he said again, and he meant it. “Come on. We have to go up the right way.”

They climbed the ordinary stairs together, and the building counted them as they went. On the landing below the fourth floor, Ansel stopped and took out his notebook. He wrote: *four-D. stair down. no stair down. count balanced.* Then he drew a line under it and closed the book.

Behind them, the seam in the floor was gone. The fourth stair was only a stair. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe ticked, and the elevator settled, and the building went back to its old, patient arithmetic.

At the door of 4-D, Ivy stopped and looked at him. “You don’t remember what you lost.”

“No,” he said. “But I remember you.”

She nodded slowly. The hallway light hummed above them, steady and ordinary, and they went inside.
