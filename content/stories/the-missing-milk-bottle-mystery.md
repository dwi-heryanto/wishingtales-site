Jamie stood on the porch in bare feet, staring at the empty step where the milk bottle should have been. Every morning, the bottle arrived before sunrise, cold and beaded with dew. Every morning, Jamie brought it inside. But not today. Not yesterday, either.

“Mom,” Jamie called, “the milk’s gone again.”

Mom leaned out the kitchen window. “Third time this week. Maybe the milkman forgot.”

“He didn’t forget,” Jamie said. “He never forgets.”

The milkman, Mr. Otis, had a route so regular you could set a clock by him. Jamie had watched him once, from the window, and he always placed the bottle exactly on the third step, then whistled a little tune and drove off. So where was the bottle?

Jamie checked behind the bushes. Checked under the porch swing. Nothing. A small trail of white droplets led across the porch boards, but they were faint and already drying.

“Maybe a raccoon,” Mom said, but she didn’t sound sure.

Jamie shook his head. “A raccoon would knock the bottle over. Leave a mess. There’s no mess.”

That afternoon, Jamie found a clue. A milk bottle cap, the silver kind with the little tab, lying in the grass near the fence. It was clean and dry, as if someone had popped it off neatly and set it down.

“Somebody’s been taking it,” Jamie told his friend Priya on the phone. “Somebody who doesn’t want to make a mess.”

“A ghost?” Priya said, and laughed.

“Maybe,” Jamie said, but he didn’t laugh. He had a plan.

***

That night, Jamie set up his stakeout. He dragged a kitchen chair onto the porch, wrapped himself in a blanket, and clicked on his flashlight. The beam cut a bright circle through the dark.

“You’re really doing this?” Mom asked from the doorway.

“I’m solving a mystery,” Jamie said. “Detectives stay up all night.”

“Detectives also need sleep,” Mom said, but she kissed his forehead and went inside.

The porch was quiet. Crickets sang in the grass. A car passed, its headlights sweeping across the yard, then everything went dark again. Jamie kept the flashlight on his lap, ready.

At first, he was alert. He listened for footsteps, for the clink of glass. But the night was warm, and the blanket was soft, and the crickets kept up their steady song. His eyelids grew heavy. He shook his head. “Stay awake,” he whispered.

He thought about the milk bottle. Who would take it? Not a neighbor: they all had their own. Not a dog: the bottle would be chewed. Someone quiet. Someone careful.

Jamie yawned. The flashlight slipped from his fingers and rolled onto the boards with a soft thud. He told himself he’d close his eyes for just a minute. Just one minute.

The crickets sang on.

When Jamie opened his eyes, gray light was seeping over the rooftops. The blanket had slipped to the floor. The flashlight lay beside it. And the milk bottle, the one he’d set out as bait, was gone.

Jamie sat up fast. The blanket slid off his knees and the cold morning air hit his arms. He stared at the empty step where the bottle had stood.

“No,” he said out loud. “No, no, no.”

He had fallen asleep. He had actually fallen asleep on the job. Jamie thumped his heel against the porch boards in frustration. Then he stopped thumping and looked closer. There, on the step, was a pale ring where the bottle had sat. Beside it, a single white drop.

Jamie crouched down. Another drop, small and white, clung to the edge of the step. Another one further along, on the path. He touched one with his finger. It was milk. Cold, thin, real milk.

“A trail,” he whispered. His heart began to thump, but it was a good thump now.

He fetched his flashlight and crept down the steps. The drops led across the wet grass, past the rose bush, past the garden gate. Jamie followed them slowly, bent nearly double, the way a detective in a book might. His slippers got soaked. He did not care.

At the garden gate, the trail turned. The drops wobbled along the fence line toward Mr. Alvarez’s house next door. Jamie stopped. Mr. Alvarez was old and grumpy and had once told Jamie to keep off his lawn.

“Huh,” Jamie said. “Not him. His porch.”

The drops went under the fence, through a gap, and on toward the sagging wooden steps of the neighbor’s porch. Jamie looked back at his own house. The kitchen light was on. Mom would be making coffee.

He had to know. He ducked through the gap and followed.

***

The trail of milk drops ended at the darkest corner of Mr. Alvarez’s porch. Jamie knelt in the dirt and switched on the flashlight. A low wooden lattice ran along the bottom of the porch, and one slat had come loose and leaned away from the others. Behind it was a hollow, a little cave of dirt and shadow.

Jamie’s hand shook a little. He leaned down and shone the light inside. Two green eyes flashed back at him. He jumped. Then he heard it, a tiny sound, thin and high.

“Mew,” said the sound.

“Oh,” Jamie breathed.

The eyes belonged to a cat, a thin gray cat with a torn ear. She was curled in the dirt with her body curved around something. Jamie moved the light gently, and there, pressed against her belly, were three kittens. Their eyes were barely open. Their fur was patchy and damp. They squirmed and squeaked and pushed their small faces into their mother.

The gray cat watched Jamie. She did not run. She just stared, her body curled tight around her babies.

Beside her, on the dirt, lay the milk bottle. It was tipped over and empty, licked clean. Jamie sat back on his heels.

“You took it,” he whispered. “You took the milk.”

The cat blinked at him slowly. One kitten made a small, hungry noise, and the mother nuzzled it with her nose.

“You weren’t stealing it for you,” Jamie said softly. “You were stealing it for them.”

He looked at the three kittens. They were so small. Smaller than his hand. The mother cat was thin, and her ribs showed through her fur. She had been carrying milk home every night, drop by drop, to feed her family.

Jamie sat very still in the dirt. He did not feel like a detective anymore. He felt something else entirely.

Jamie stayed crouched in the dirt for a long moment. The mother cat’s eyes never left him, but she didn’t hiss or bare her teeth. She just watched, her thin flank rising and falling. One kitten crawled over her leg and mewed, a tiny, rusty sound. Jamie thought about the cold nights. He thought about the milk bottle, sitting on the porch, waiting to be brought inside. The cat had been taking it because her babies were hungry. She wasn’t a thief. She was a mother. “You must have been so scared,” Jamie whispered. “Every night, sneaking up to our porch.” The cat blinked again, slow and steady. Jamie looked at the empty bottle. He thought about how angry Dad had been about the missing milk. He thought about his own stakeout, his flashlight, his notebook full of clues. None of it mattered now. What mattered was the three little kittens and their hungry mother. “I’m sorry,” he said. “I didn’t know.” He backed out of the hollow slowly, on his hands and knees. The cat watched him go, her tail curled around her kittens. When Jamie stood up in the gray morning light, his knees were dirty and his hands were cold. But he knew what he had to do.

***

Back in the kitchen, Jamie moved quietly. Dad was still asleep upstairs. The house was still and dim. Jamie opened the fridge and took out the new bottle of milk. He found a saucer in the cupboard, a small white one with a chip on the rim. He poured the milk carefully, filling the saucer almost to the top. Then he carried it outside, across the yard, to the neighbor’s porch. He set it down near the hollow, where the cat could reach it without being seen from the street. “Here,” he said softly. “For you and the kittens.” He waited, but the cat didn’t come out. That was all right. She would find it when she was ready. Jamie went back inside and washed the saucer in the sink. He put the milk bottle back in the fridge, not empty this time. Dad wouldn’t notice. And if he did, Jamie would say he didn’t know what happened. It wasn’t a lie, exactly. It was a secret. The good kind. The kind you keep to help someone else. That night, Jamie fell asleep thinking about the gray cat and her three small kittens. He didn’t feel like a detective anymore. He felt like something better. He felt like a friend.
