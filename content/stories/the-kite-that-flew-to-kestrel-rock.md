The boathouse smelled of tar and cold rope. Jemma pushed the door open and stood in the rectangle of grey light, letting her eyes adjust. Her brother's boat was gone (had been gone for three months), but his things were still here. They hung from nails and sat stacked on shelves, as if he might walk back in and need them.

She found the kite on the workbench under a coil of fishing line. It was a half-finished thing of paper and thin wood, the spine not yet glued, the tail a ragged strip of red cloth. Cal had been making it before the storm. Before Kestrel Rock took the boat and everything in it.

Jemma picked up the spine. The wood was smooth where his thumb had sanded it.

"You never finished it," she said, to the empty room, to the shape of him that lingered in the smell of varnish.

She finished it herself. It took an hour. The glue went tacky on her fingers, and the paper tore once at the corner, and she patched it with a scrap from the same red cloth. When she held the kite up, it was crooked. It would fly anyway. Cal always said a kite with a flaw flew truer, because it had something to argue with.

The cliff path started behind the boathouse. Jemma knew it the way she knew the back of her own hand: every loose stone, every place the gorse grabbed at your sleeve. At the top, the wind came off the sea hard enough to lean into. And out past the headland, black against the evening water, Kestrel Rock.

That was where his boat had gone. That was where she would fly it.

She tucked the kite under her arm and started up.

***

Dusk came down fast on the cliff. The sea turned from grey to slate, and the path narrowed to a thread between the gorse and the drop. Jemma climbed with the kite held tight, her breath loud in her own ears.

Halfway up, a light bobbed toward her. Old Marn, the lighthouse keeper, came down the path with a lantern in one hand and a coil of wire in the other. His coat was stiff with salt, and his face was all weather.

"You're late to be up here," he said. "And carrying a kite."

"I'm flying it from the point." Jemma kept walking.

Marn stepped into her way. Not rough, but firm, like a door closing. "Not tonight. The wind's wrong. It comes off Kestrel Rock and it takes things."

"It's just wind."

"It's just wind the way the sea's just water." He lifted the lantern, and the light caught the kite's red tail. Something in his face changed. "That's Cal's work. I'd know the stitching anywhere."

Jemma said nothing.

"Your brother was a good sailor," Marn said quietly. "Good sailors still go. The rock doesn't care. But it takes kites, too, girl. It takes them and it keeps them, and I've seen it take worse than paper."

"I'm not afraid of a rock."

"No," Marn said. "That's what worries me." He looked at the kite a long moment, then stepped aside. "If you go, keep to the path. And when the wind changes, come back. Don't chase it."

Jemma walked past him into the dark.

Behind her, the lantern did not move. "Don't chase it, Jemma," he called again, and his voice was smaller now, swallowed by the wind.

The wind came at her sideways, shoving the kite before she had it fully unfolded. Jemma stumbled back from the cliff edge, her boots skidding on loose shale, and the kite yanked at her hands like something alive. The red tail whipped past her face. For a moment the whole thing was a mess of paper and sticks, and then the wind found its shape and lifted.

"Come on," she said, though she wasn't sure she was talking to the kite or to herself. She let out more line, and the kite rose. It wobbled, then steadied, climbing above the cliff path until it was a red cross against the grey evening sky. The sea below was dark and moving, and the foam showed white in the last of the light. Jemma planted her feet and leaned back against the pull. For a few breaths it was good. It was like holding a piece of the sky on a string.

The wind shifted. It came off Kestrel Rock now, cold and steady, and the kite jerked hard to the right. Jemma felt the line bite into her palm. "Easy," she said. "Easy." But the kite didn't want to be easy. It wanted to go where the wind went. The wind went out over the water, toward the black shape of the rock and the broken teeth of stone around it. The line hummed. She hauled back, hand over hand, and the kite dipped, then surged. The line went slack for a heartbeat before snapping tight again.

Then the wind gusted, and the line broke.

It didn't snap like string. It parted with a sound like a small crack, and the kite was free. It tumbled sideways, then caught, then sailed away toward the rock with the red tail streaming behind it. Jemma stood with the useless end of the line in her hand. "No," she said. "No, no." The kite was already small, already past the path, already over the water where the rocks began.

She ran.

The path hugged the cliff, narrow and stony, and she ran along it with her lantern swinging and her breath burning. Behind her Marn's voice came thin through the wind, calling something she didn't stop to hear. The kite drifted and dipped, teasing, always a little further ahead. The path curved, and the cliff fell away, and the sea kept up its noise below. Jemma didn't look down. She looked only at the red tail, and she ran.

***

The path ended at a gap.

Jemma stopped so hard she nearly went over the edge. Where the path should have gone on, the cliff had crumbled away. The gap was too wide to jump and too deep to see the bottom. The sea moved somewhere below, black and patient. On the far side the path picked up again. Between them ran the ledge that connected them, and it was barely a ledge at all. It was a strip of stone no wider than her two feet. A crack ran down its middle, and on one side the drop went all the way to the water.

She stood there with her heart going and the lantern shaking in her hand. The kite was still moving, further off now, a red speck against the dark. If she went back, it was gone. If she went on, she had to cross.

"Jemma!" Marn's voice, closer now. "Stop where you are!"

She turned. He was coming along the path behind her, his lantern bobbing, his breath loud. "You can't cross that," he said. "Not in this light. Not in any light. It's crumbling. It'll go under you."

"I have to get the kite."

"It's a kite, girl."

"It's Cal's kite." Her voice came out harder than she meant. "He never finished it. It's the last thing he made."

Marn was quiet a moment. The wind pulled at his coat. "I know what it is," he said. "I know. But Cal wouldn't want you to die for it."

Jemma looked at the ledge. Then she set her lantern down on the path, took a breath, and stepped onto the stone.

The ledge held. She went sideways, one hand flat against the cliff, the other holding the lantern out for light. The crack ran under her boots. Halfway across, the stone shifted, and she froze. For a long moment there was only the wind and the sea and her own heart. Then she moved again, slow, careful, and her foot found the far side.

She was almost across when the lantern slipped.

It wasn't a big thing. Her fingers were cold and the handle was wet, and it went out of her hand and over the edge. She watched it fall, turning, its light shrinking, until it hit the water below and went out. The dark came down like a lid. Jemma pressed herself against the cliff and did not move.

"Jemma!" Marn's voice, from the other side of the gap. "Are you across?"

"I'm across," she said. Her voice shook. "I dropped the lantern."

"Never mind the lantern. Can you see the path?"

She looked. The dark had thickened, but the moon was up now, thin and white, and the path showed faint ahead of her, climbing toward the rock. "I can see it."

"Then go," Marn said. "Go on. I'll wait here."

Jemma pushed off from the cliff and went on into the dark.

***

The path climbed, and the wind climbed with it. Jemma kept her hand on the cliff face where she could, feeling the cold stone breathe under her palm. The moon gave her just enough to see the ruts worn by boots and sheep, and she followed them because there was nothing else to follow. Below, the sea worked at the rocks with a slow, patient sound. Above, the sky had gone a deep, clean blue, and the stars were coming out one by one, as if someone were lighting them.

She smelled the cairn before she saw it. Salt and old wood and something sweeter underneath, like the inside of the boathouse. Then the path bent around a shoulder of rock, and there it was. A heap of driftwood stacked waist-high, grey and silver in the moonlight. Beside it stood a dead hawthorn tree with its branches spread like fingers. The kite was caught in the topmost branches, red cloth flapping, tail wrapped twice around the wood.

"Found you," Jemma said. Her voice came out small in the open dark, but it steadied her.

She climbed the last few feet and stood in the lee of the cairn, where the wind dropped to a mutter. The kite pulled and snapped above her, wanting to be free. She reached up, but the branch was too high, and the tail was knotted tight. So she circled the cairn, looking for a way up, and that was when she saw the knife.

It lay on the flat stone at the top of the cairn, placed there like an offering. A clasp knife with a bone handle, worn smooth at the edges. Jemma knew it before she touched it. Tom had carried it since he was twelve, and he had lost it on the boat, or so he said. Their father had laughed and said the sea keeps what it takes. She picked it up. The bone was cold, then warm from her hand, and she opened the blade with her thumbnail. It was clean and sharp, as if someone had looked after it.

"Tom," she said, to the wind and the cairn and the dead tree. "You daft thing. You left it here."

She climbed the hawthorn then, using the knife to saw through the knotted tail, and the kite came loose in her hands like a bird she had caught. She held it against her chest, feeling the paper crackle and the frame press into her ribs. Below, the sea went on with its slow work, and the moon rode high, and the cairn stood quiet at her back.

***

Jemma sat down beside the cairn and let the kite rest across her knees. The wind had dropped a little, or she had stopped noticing it. She could hear Marn's voice calling from the far side of the gap, thin and far away. She called back that she was all right, that she had found it, and the old man's laugh came across the dark like a handful of stones.

She turned the knife over in her hands. Tom had taught her to whittle with it, sitting on the harbour wall, and he had told her that a knife was only as good as the hand that kept it. She thought about the kite, half-finished in the boathouse, and the way he had planned to fly it from the cliff on the first good wind. He had never got the chance. The boat went out and the boat did not come back, and the kite stayed on its bench with the glue gone hard and the paper waiting.

"You never finished it," she said. "So I finished it for you."

The kite tugged at her hands, wanting the sky. Jemma stood up and walked to the edge of the rock, where the wind came clean and strong off the sea. She held the kite up and let it fill, and it pulled like a live thing. For a moment she thought about keeping it, taking it home, hanging it on the wall where Tom's coat used to hang. But that was not what kites were for.

"Go on, then," she said. "Go and find him."

She let go. The kite went up and out, red against the dark, and the wind took it and carried it away over the water. It turned and dipped and rose, until it was only a speck, and then not even that. Jemma watched until her eyes ached. Then she put the knife in her pocket, where it sat warm against her leg, and she turned and walked back along the path.

***

The gap was easier going back. Marn was waiting on the far side with a lantern, and he took her arm without a word. He walked her down the cliff path to the harbour, where the boats knocked against the wall and the lighthouse beam swung out over the water. At the door of her house she stopped and looked back at the dark shape of Kestrel Rock, and the sea beyond it, and the sky where the kite had gone.

"You all right?" Marn asked.

"I'm all right," Jemma said. She put her hand in her pocket and felt the bone handle of the knife, and it was like holding a piece of her brother, small and solid and hers to keep.
