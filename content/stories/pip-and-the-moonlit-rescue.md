Pip the field mouse sat at the mouth of her burrow, counting stars.

She did this most nights. It was a game. She would pick a star, then find another one, then another, until the sky felt like a map she could almost read. The wheat above her whispered in the wind. Somewhere far off, a stream talked to the stones.

Then the wind changed.

It came hard and sudden, not the soft meadow wind Pip knew. Grass bent flat. A shutter banged on the farm far away. Pip's whiskers streamed sideways.

Something came out of the dark sky. It tumbled, a small black shape, turning over and over like a leaf in a gutter. It missed the fence post. It missed the oak.

It fell into the tall grass ten tail-lengths from Pip's door.

Pip did not run. She stood very still, ears up. The grass rustled where the shape landed. Then it went quiet.

"Hello?" Pip called. Her voice sounded small.

No answer.

Pip crept forward, one paw at a time. The wheat was cold and wet from the storm. She parted the stalks with her nose.

A bird lay there. A swift, she thought, though she had only seen them high up, small dark arrows against the clouds. This one had a white belly and a forked tail. One wing stuck out at a wrong angle. The bird's eye rolled, blinking.

"You fell," Pip said.

"I know." The swift's voice was thin and fast, like her heartbeat. "The storm pushed me. I couldn't hold my line."

Pip looked up. The clouds were breaking. A few stars showed through.

"You're on the ground," Pip said. "Swifts don't do that."

"I noticed." The bird tried to stand. Her feet were tiny and weak. She wobbled and sat down again. "My wing hurts. And it's dark. I can't fly in the dark. I've never been in the dark before."

Pip had been in the dark every night of her life. She thought about saying so, but the bird looked so frightened that she didn't.

"What's your name?" Pip asked.

"Sky." The swift blinked. "My mother named me for where we live."

"I'm Pip." She touched a paw to the bird's wing, gently. "You landed near my burrow. That's lucky."

"Is it?"

"It is," said Pip. "I know every path in this meadow. I'll help you find your flock."

Sky's eye went wide. "You will?"

"By dawn," Pip said. "Before the sun comes up. You can fly then, can't you?"

"I can fly in daylight," Sky said. "I've never tried anything else."

"Then we have until dawn." Pip stood up tall, which was not very tall. "First we have to get you out of the grass."

***

Getting Sky out of the grass took longer than Pip expected.

Sky could not walk well. Her legs were made for clinging to cliff walls, not for meadow floors. Every step, she stumbled. Pip went ahead and pushed the stalks apart with her shoulders, making a tunnel.

"Left," Pip said. "Now right."

"I'm trying," Sky said.

"You're doing fine."

"I'm doing terribly."

They came out at last beside a flat stone. The meadow opened up around them. Above, the clouds had thinned to rags. The stars were bright and cold.

Sky looked up and made a small sound.

"What is it?" Pip asked.

"My flock is up there somewhere." Sky's voice cracked. "They're flying west. They always fly west at night. And I'm down here on a stone."

Pip sat beside her. "Then we go west too."

"You don't have to come." Sky tucked her head low. "I'm not your problem."

"You fell by my door," Pip said. "That makes you my problem."

Sky was quiet for a moment. "The other swifts say mice are scared of everything."

"We are," Pip said. "That's why we're so good at being careful. Being careful is what you need right now."

Sky almost laughed. It came out as a chirp.

Pip checked the sky. The wind had turned soft again. Far off, the stream rushed loud through the dark.

"West is that way," Pip said, pointing with her nose. "But the stream cuts across it, and there's an owl who hunts near the old fence."

"An owl." Sky's feathers went flat against her body.

"We'll go around her," Pip said. "I know the way."

Sky looked at the dark meadow, then at the small mouse beside her. "You promise?"

"I promise," said Pip. "Before dawn, you'll be up there with your flock."

She started walking west, and Sky hopped after her, one careful step at a time.

The grass was taller than Pip's ears, and every stem was wet with cold dew. She kept her nose low and her feet quiet. Sky hopped behind her, wings held tight, breathing hard.

"How much farther?" Sky whispered.

"Not far to the fence," Pip said. "Past the fence is the stream."

A shadow slid over the moon above them. Pip froze. Sky froze too, so still she looked like a stone.

"Don't move," Pip breathed.

The owl drifted down and landed on a fence post. Her head turned slowly, one way, then the other. Her eyes were two yellow lamps in the dark.

Pip pressed herself flat against the ground. Her heart beat so loud she was sure the owl could hear it.

"Mouse," the owl said softly. "I smell mouse."

"She's talking to us," Sky squeaked.

"She's talking to the night," Pip whispered back. "Owls do that. Stay down."

The owl lifted her wings and swept low over the grass. Wind from her feathers brushed Pip's back. Then she rose and glided away toward the far trees.

Pip let out a long breath. "She's gone. Come on."

They hurried to the fence and squeezed under the bottom rail. Beyond it, the stream ran fast and black, loud with rain from the storm.

Sky stopped at the bank. "I can't swim."

"You won't have to," Pip said. "There's a log across it, upstream. Follow me."

The log was slick with moss. Pip went first, step by careful step. Halfway across, Sky slipped. Her wing dragged in the rushing water, and she cried out.

Pip turned and grabbed a feather's worth of Sky's wing. "Up. Up!"

Sky scrambled onto the log, shaking. Her left wing was soaked through and heavy.

"It won't lift," Sky said. "It's too wet."

"It'll dry," Pip said. "But not here. Keep moving."

***

On the far bank, the meadow sloped up to a line of old trees. Pip led Sky to a fallen log with a hollow in its side, big enough for both of them.

"In here," Pip said. "Out of the wind."

Sky crawled inside and huddled in the dry leaves. Her wet wing hung loose and dripping.

Pip sat at the opening and watched the dark. The stream roared behind them. Somewhere a night bird called, once, twice.

"You're shivering," Pip said.

"So are you," Sky said.

Pip looked down at her own paws. They were trembling. "I know."

"You said you weren't scared."

"I never said that," Pip said. "I said I knew the way. That's different."

Sky tucked her head under her wing. "The others fly all night. They sleep in the air, over the sea. I've never done it. I've only ever slept in a nest."

"Tonight you sleep in a log," Pip said. "Tomorrow you sleep in the air."

"What if I can't?"

Pip was quiet. She thought about the owl, and the stream, and the long dark ahead. Then she thought about the old badger who lived under the oak, who had told her once that fear was just a visitor, and visitors leave.

"Then we'll walk," Pip said. "Or hop. Or crawl. But we won't stop."

Sky lifted her head. "Why? Why won't you stop?"

"Because you fell by my door," Pip said. "And because I want to see you fly."

Sky's wet wing stirred. Outside, the wind shifted, and the first pale gray crept into the east.

Pip poked her nose out of the log. The meadow was gray now, and the grass shone with dew. Somewhere far off, a blackbird was waking up.

"Which way?" Sky asked.

Pip closed her eyes. She remembered the old badger, sitting by his door with his chin on his paws. *If you're lost in the dark,* he had said, *don't look at the ground. Look up. Find the tallest oak. It stands above everything, and everything else stands below it.*

"The tallest oak," Pip said. "That's where we go."

"Which one is that?"

"The one the badger lives under. It's past the stream, on the far side of the hill."

Sky looked at her wet wing. "Can you see it from here?"

Pip stood on her back legs. Grass, grass, and more grass. Then, just over the rise, a dark shape against the pale sky. It was huge and old, and its branches spread out like arms.

"There," Pip said. "See it? That's it. That's the oak."

They set off. Sky hopped, and Pip walked beside her. The grass was taller than both of them, and every stem was heavy with water. It fell on their heads and backs.

"I'm getting wetter," Sky said.

"So am I," Pip said. "We're nearly there."

A crow called from a fence post. Pip's heart jumped. She kept walking.

The hill was steep. Sky slipped on the wet grass and slid back down. Pip went back for her and pushed with her shoulder.

"You're very small to be pushing me," Sky said.

"You're very light," Pip said.

At the top of the hill, the oak stood alone. Its trunk was wide as a door, and its roots made steps in the ground. Pip put her paw on the bark.

"We made it," she said.

Then she heard it. A sound like tiny scissors, high up, over and over. *Skree, skree, skree.*

Sky lifted her head. Her whole body went still.

"That's them," she whispered. "That's my flock."

Pip looked up. Against the gray morning, dark shapes were wheeling around the top of the oak. Round and round they went, screaming to each other, cutting the sky into pieces.

"Go on," Pip said.

Sky spread her wings. They were still damp, but the wind lifted them. She ran, flapped, and rose. Up she went, wobbling, then straight, then higher.

The flock turned and opened like a hand. Sky flew into the middle of it.

***

Pip sat under the oak and watched until her neck ached. The swifts were small and black against the brightening sky. She could not tell which one was Sky.

Then one bird broke away and dropped toward her. It landed on a root, clumsy and glad.

"You did it," Pip said.

"I did it," Sky said. "My wing dried on the way up."

"Good."

Sky hopped closer. "The others are going south today. Over the sea. Tonight I'll sleep in the air."

"You'll be fine."

"I was scared," Sky said. "The whole way. Even when I flew."

Pip thought about that. "So was I."

"But you kept going."

"So did you."

Sky touched Pip's ear with her beak. It was quick and soft, like a leaf falling.

"Thank you, Pip."

"Fly safe, Sky."

Sky jumped, beat her wings, and climbed. She joined the flock, and they turned together and swept away over the hill. In a moment they were specks. Then they were gone.

Pip sat a while longer. The sun came up over the meadow, and the dew turned to gold. She was tired, and her fur was wet, and her paws were muddy.

She walked home slowly. The grass was shorter in the daylight, and the path was easy to find. When she reached her burrow, she looked back at the hill. The oak was small from here, and the sky above it was wide and empty and blue.

That night, when the dark came, Pip did not stay inside. She sat at her door and watched the stars come out, one by one, over the meadow.

The dark was not empty. It was full of things going somewhere.

She listened to the wind in the grass and thought of Sky, somewhere over the sea, sleeping on the air. Then she went in, and she slept, and she was not afraid.
