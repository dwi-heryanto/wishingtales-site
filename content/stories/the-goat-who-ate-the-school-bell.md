The morning of the inspection, the school bell was gone.

Not broken. Not stolen by someone with a ladder and a grudge, though that was the head teacher's first theory. Just gone, leaving a clean brass-shaped hole in the bracket above the assembly hall door and a silence that made every step across the yard sound like a confession.

Mr. Pargeter gathered us all in the hall before first lesson. He stood on the stage with his hands behind his back, the way he does when he has decided to be calm about something enormous.

"The bell," he said, "was rung on Friday afternoon. It was not rung this morning. Therefore, between Friday afternoon and this morning, someone removed it."

"Or something," said Priya from the third row, and got a look.

"Someone," Mr. Pargeter repeated, with weight. "I am not accusing anyone. I am simply noting that the bell is a heavy object, and heavy objects do not walk."

Out in the yard, through the tall windows, Brenda the goat lifted her head from the fence and looked at us with her long, calm, criminal face. Brenda belongs to the school farm project. She has eaten a geography display, two traffic cones, and, last spring, the hat of a visiting inspector. She has never eaten anything as big as a bell, but the way she was chewing just then, slow and thoughtful, did not fill anyone with confidence.

"It was her," said Danny Okonjo, immediately.

"It is never her," said Mr. Pargeter, who has a soft spot for Brenda that he denies at every meeting. "Goats do not eat bells. Goats are discerning."

The assembly broke up into muttering. Mrs. Ash, the caretaker, said she had locked the hall herself and the key had not left her pocket. Mr. Fenn, the music teacher, said he had heard something metallic in the night and assumed it was the pipes. Everyone had a theory and no one had a bell.

I stood at the back with my hands in my pockets and watched Brenda through the window. She had stopped chewing. She was looking at me now, and there was something about the set of her jaw that I did not trust.

I am Kit, by the way. I am not the cleverest person in this school, but I am the one who notices things, which is a different and occasionally more useful talent.

***

The first thing I found was brass filings.

They were scattered under the bracket above the hall door, tiny bright curls like the shavings from a pencil sharpener, but yellow and hard. I crouched and picked one up. It had a tooth-mark on it. Not a clean cut. A dent, the way a coin looks when someone has bitten it to test it.

Goats test things with their teeth. This is not a secret.

The second thing I found was the sound.

I went out to the fence at break, casual, hands in my blazer, and stood near Brenda like a person who had simply come to enjoy the weather. She regarded me with one yellow eye and went back to chewing grass. I leaned on the rail. She leaned on the rail. We were two professionals, sizing each other up.

Then a gust of wind came across the field, and Brenda shifted her weight, and I heard it.

Clang.

A soft, muffled, brassy clang, somewhere deep inside her, like a spoon in a saucepan a long way off. I froze. She froze. We looked at each other.

"Brenda," I said, quietly. "Did you eat the bell?"

She blinked. She did not deny it. She did not confirm it either, to be fair, but she also did not look innocent, and I have seen Brenda look innocent. It is a whole performance. This was not that.

I went and found Priya, because Priya is the only person in this school who takes me seriously before lunch.

"A goat," she said slowly, when I had explained, "swallowed the school bell."

"It's in there. I heard it clang."

"Kit. It's a bell. It's the size of a melon."

"She's a big goat."

Priya looked at Brenda, who was now chewing with tremendous dignity, and then at me. "You want me to go to Mr. Pargeter and tell him his favourite goat ate his favourite bell."

"Yes."

"And you think he'll believe us."

I considered this. Mr. Pargeter had spent the whole assembly insisting goats are discerning. Mr. Pargeter would sooner believe the caretaker had flown away.

"No," I admitted. "But I'm going to prove it anyway."

The nurse's office smelled of antiseptic and lollipops, and Nurse Adeyemi was out on a yard duty she did not know she was on. I borrowed the stethoscope the way honest people borrow things: quickly, with an apology already prepared. Priya kept watch in the doorway.

"You have three minutes," she said. "I'm timing it. If you make me an accomplice to goat-related theft, I want it on record that I objected."

Brenda was exactly where I had left her, working her jaw gently against the fence post like a cow chewing its own thoughts. The stethoscope went over my ears. The cold disc settled against her left flank, and Brenda turned her head to look at me with the mild suspicion of a librarian who has spotted a folded page.

"Breathe," I told her.

For a moment there was nothing but the gurgle of a goat's long digestive afternoon, a sound like a stream running under a bridge. Then, faint and perfect, a tick. Then a tock. Then a tick.

"Priya," I said, very calmly. "Come here."

She crouched and took the other earpiece, and her face went through several expressions in order. "That's a bell."

"That is a bell."

Brenda chose this moment to hiccup.

I want to be clear that I do not blame her. A hiccup is not a decision. But the bell inside her gave a full, ringing, assembly-hall clang, the kind that means stand up, sit down, stop talking and pretend to sing. Priya fell backwards into the grass. Brenda's eyes went wide and white-rimmed, and I watched the exact thought arrive behind them: something is ringing, and it is me, and I would like to be somewhere else.

She went through the fence like wet paper.

"Brenda!" I shouted. "No!"

Goat, I have learned, is a language with one word in it, and that word is gone. She banked left across the vegetable beds, took the corner of the shed at a speed that should not be legal on four legs, and headed for the open double doors of the assembly hall. Priya was already running.

"Don't yell!" she shouted, yelling. "Yelling makes it worse!"

"She's a goat, not a horse!"

"She's a goat with a bell in her stomach, and we are going to be on the news!"

The assembly hall was full of stacked chairs and one very startled caretaker. Brenda skidded on the polished floor, all four legs pointing at different compass directions, and rang. She rang the long ring, the impatient one, and the caretaker dropped an entire tower of chairs, which clattered and rang too, and Brenda, deciding the building was now full of bells and could not be trusted, bolted for the corridor.

***

We chased her past the science lab, where a model skeleton lost an arm, past the trophy case, where she stopped to admire a silver cup and then thought better of it, and up the stairs she was not supposed to climb. Priya kept shouting helpful instructions. I kept ignoring them.

"Left! No, your other left!"

"I know my left!"

"Kit, she's going into the music room!"

The music room door was open, and Brenda went through it at full tilt, and then, finding herself in a dead end full of chairs and stands and a grand piano, she did the only sensible thing available to a panicking goat. She turned around and stood still, breathing hard, ringing softly with every breath like a badly tuned radio.

Then she hiccuped again, and the bell went bong.

Brenda looked at me. I do not know how to explain the look to anyone who has not been judged by a goat. She was not sorry. She was not even embarrassed. She was simply a goat who had made a choice some time ago and was now living with the consequences, and she expected me to do the same.

"Priya," I said, very quietly, "shut the door."

"Why?"

"Because if Mr. Pargeter sees her like this, she's going to the farm. He said so at assembly. He said any goat that causes an incident goes to a farm and gets a nice paddock, and everyone knows what a nice paddock means."

Priya's hand stayed on the door. "You think he actually would."

"He's a man who loves one goat and one bell, and she's eaten the bell. He hasn't done the maths yet."

Brenda hiccuped. Bong.

And then, from the corridor, calm and cheerful and much too close, a voice I did not know yet but would come to know very well said: "Hello! Is this the school? I did knock, but I think a goat answered."

Priya and I looked at each other. Brenda looked at the door. The bell in her stomach gave one small, hopeful ring, as if it had heard the word goat and wanted to be helpful.

The inspector was a tall woman in a raincoat the colour of wet slate, and she had the calm of someone who had seen far stranger things than a school on a Tuesday. She stood in the music room doorway with her clipboard tucked under one arm.

"I'm Ms. Okafor," she said. "I'm here about the bell. I gather there's been a situation."

Mr. Pargeter arrived behind her at a speed that suggested he had run the whole way from assembly. His tie was askew. His face was the colour of the brickwork outside.

"There is no situation," he said. "There is a perfectly ordinary school. And a goat. And a bell that has gone wandering, which is not the same as missing."

"It's missing," I said.

Everyone looked at me. Brenda looked at me too, with her wet goat eyes, and hiccuped, and the sound that came out of her was unmistakably a bell. Bong. Ms. Okafor's eyebrows went up about a centimetre.

"That," she said, "is interesting."

"It's a stomach thing," I said quickly. "She swallows air. It's very common. I read about it."

"You read about it."

"In a book. About goats." I was making it worse with every word, and I knew it, and I kept going anyway, because the alternative was Brenda in a nice paddock and Mr. Pargeter's whole term ruined. "She's got a condition. It makes her sound like a bell. She's had it for years. It's very sad, actually."

Priya made a small noise beside me that might have been a cough and might have been a laugh.

Mrs. Ellery, the music teacher, folded her arms. "Kit," she said, "did you take the bell?"

There it was. The question I had been dodging since breakfast. I looked at Brenda, who was chewing a bit of the curtain with the air of a goat who had done nothing wrong in her life. I looked at Mr. Pargeter, who was already drafting the letter to my parents in his head. I looked at Ms. Okafor, who was watching me the way you watch a coin before you call it.

"Yes," I said. "I took it. I thought it would be funny. I'm sorry."

The silence was enormous. Mr. Pargeter opened his mouth. Priya grabbed my sleeve. And Brenda, who had never in her life understood anything at all, stopped chewing, turned her whole head toward me, and looked at me with an expression I had not seen on a goat before.

Then she burped.

It was not a small burp. It went on for some time. It started low and went up at the end, and out of her mouth, in a wet brass arc, came the bell. It hit the music room floor and rolled in a wobbly circle and stopped against Mr. Pargeter's shoe.

"Well," said Ms. Okafor, into the quiet. "That's that, then."

***

Ms. Okafor picked up the bell and turned it over in her hands. "I was a farm vet for eleven years," she said, "before I took this job. I've pulled a bicycle pump out of a cow. A bell out of a goat is, honestly, a good day for me."

Mr. Pargeter had gone a strange grey colour. "She ate it," he said. "The goat ate the bell. My bell. The bell."

"She did," said Ms. Okafor. "And then she gave it back, which is more than most of us manage."

"But the inspection. The inspection is ruined. The school is a laughing stock. A goat has eaten the bell, and the child has lied to me, and it is all a catastrophe." He rounded on me. "You said you took it."

"I did say that," I agreed.

"Why would you say that?"

I looked at Brenda. She was standing very close to me now, her chin resting on my shoulder, her breath warm and hay-smelling. "Because I thought she'd be sent away," I said. "And she didn't mean it. She never means it. She's just a goat, sir."

Mr. Pargeter stared at me for a long moment. Then he stared at the bell in Ms. Okafor's hands, the bell he had polished every morning for twenty years, which was now slightly the worse for wear and absolutely intact.

"I see," he said, and sounded, for the first time all day, less like a head teacher and more like a man who had misplaced something and found it again.

Ms. Okafor wrote something on her clipboard. "I'll be recommending the bell be rehung with a guard," she said. "And I'll be recommending the goat stays exactly where she is. She's got good instincts, that one. She knew to give it back."

"She did not know," said Mr. Pargeter.

"She burped it out the moment the child took the blame," said Ms. Okafor. "Call it what you like."

So that was that. The bell went back up the next morning, and the whole school stood in the yard to watch it ring, and Mr. Pargeter rang it himself with a face like a man at a funeral and a bit of a smile underneath. Brenda was moved to the paddock by the sports field, which had no bell in it and no fence low enough to matter, and she was given a plaque that says The Goat Who Rang In The Term. Priya says the plaque is the funniest thing that has ever happened. I think the funniest thing is Mr. Pargeter polishing the bell every morning before assembly, the way he always did, except now he checks it over first, very carefully, for teeth marks.

Brenda still hiccups sometimes. When she does, the whole playground stops to listen, and someone always says, oh, that's just the bell, and everyone laughs, and Brenda chews her fence and pretends she has no idea why.
