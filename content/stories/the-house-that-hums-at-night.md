Mila stood in the hallway of her grandmother's old house, hugging a cardboard box. The floorboards creaked under her sneakers. Outside, the moving van rumbled away, leaving a silence that felt too big. Then she heard it.

A faint hum drifted down from somewhere above. Not a fridge. Not a bee. A soft, steady note, like someone humming a tune with their mouth almost closed.

"Mom?" Mila called. No answer. Her mother was still in the kitchen, wrestling with a box of pots.

Mila climbed the stairs. The humming grew a little clearer. It seemed to come from the ceiling itself, from a place that wasn't any room at all.

At the top of the stairs, a narrow door sat in the wall. It had a brass knob and no keyhole. The hum pulsed behind it, slow and patient.

"Hello?" Mila whispered. The humming stopped. The house went still. Mila's heart thumped once, hard. She backed down the stairs, two steps at a time.

That night, she lay in her new bed under a quilt that smelled of lavender. The humming started again, soft and far away. It wasn't scary, exactly. It was lonely. Like a song waiting for someone to sing it back.

Mila pulled the quilt to her chin and listened until she fell asleep.

***

The next morning, Mila was in the front yard, poking at a stubborn weed, when a boy her age leaned over the fence.

"You're the new kid," he said. "I'm Sam. I live two doors down."

"Mila." She wiped dirt on her jeans. "Do you know anything about this house?"

Sam's eyebrows went up. "Everyone knows about that house. My dad says it's been quiet for years. But my grandpa told me stories."

"What kind of stories?"

"About a song," Sam said. "He said your grandma used to hum something, and the house hummed back."

Mila's skin prickled. "I heard it last night. A humming sound."

Sam stepped closer. "You heard it? Really?"

"From upstairs. Behind a little door with no keyhole."

Sam whistled. "Show me."

Inside, the hallway felt dim after the bright yard. Sam followed Mila past the kitchen, where her mom was on the phone. They checked the living room. No hum. The dining room. Nothing. The pantry, the laundry room, the closet under the stairs.

"Wait," Sam said, holding up a hand. "Listen."

There it was. A low, steady hum, coming from the ceiling above the hallway.

"It's upstairs," Mila said. "I told you."

They hurried up the steps. At the top, the narrow door waited. The hum was close now, right on the other side, like a bee in a jar. Mila reached for the knob. It wouldn't turn.

"Locked," she said.

"There's no keyhole," Sam said. "So how do you unlock it?"

They stared at the door. The humming went on, patient and soft, waiting.

Mila pressed her ear to the door. The hum was louder now, a low thrum that tickled her skin. "It's like a song," she whispered. Sam put his hand flat on the wood. "My dad says your grandma used to sing to the house. He said the house sang back." Mila pulled her ear away. "That's just a story." "Maybe." Sam shrugged. "But the door's locked and there's no keyhole. So how do we get in?" They searched the hallway walls for a hidden latch. Nothing. Mila ran her fingers along the frame. The wood was old and smooth, except for a small patch near the bottom where the paint had chipped. She knelt. A tiny hole, no bigger than a pea, was drilled into the wood. "Look," she said. Sam crouched beside her. "A keyhole?" "Too small." Mila sat back on her heels. The humming swelled, then faded, like a breath. "It's waiting," she said. "For what?" Sam asked. Mila didn't answer. She didn't know.

***

Downstairs, Mila's mom was still on the phone. They slipped out the back door. The garden shed leaned against the fence, its door hanging open. Inside smelled of rust and dry leaves. "Your grandma kept everything," Sam said, lifting a tin of nails. "My grandpa called her a magpie." Mila smiled. She remembered Grandma's pockets full of buttons and string. A wooden shelf held jars of screws, a broken clock, and a coffee can of old keys. Mila tipped the can onto a workbench. Keys clattered out, big and small, silver and brass. One was long and rusty, with a twisted head. "This looks old," she said. Sam took it and turned it over. "Try it." Back upstairs, Mila knelt at the door. The little hole waited. She slid the key in. It fit. She turned it. The key stuck, then snapped. The broken end stayed in the hole. "No!" Mila stared at the jagged half in her hand. Sam let out a breath. "We're stuck outside." The humming stopped. The hallway went quiet. Mila pressed her palm to the door. "I'm sorry," she whispered. Nothing hummed back.

Mila sat back on her heels. Her eyes stung, but she didn't want to cry in front of Sam. She stared at the broken key in her hand and thought about Grandma. Grandma used to hum while she dusted. One song especially, soft and slow, with a tune like a sleepy bee.

"What is it?" Sam asked.

"My grandma's lullaby," Mila said. "She hummed it every night when I stayed over."

Mila closed her eyes and hummed the first notes. Her voice was small and shaky. The hallway stayed quiet. She hummed a little louder, and the tune filled the dusty air.

Then something clicked inside the wall.

"Did you hear that?" Sam whispered.

Mila kept humming. The attic door shuddered, and the latch popped open. The door swung inward with a long creak, like a yawn after a nap.

"Whoa," Sam said. "How did you do that?"

"I didn't," Mila said. "The house did."

They stepped into the attic. It was dim and warm, smelling of cedar and old paper. A round window let in the moon. In the middle of the floor sat a small wooden box with a crank on the side. Its lid was carved with tiny stars.

"A music box," Mila said. "I've never seen this before."

Sam nudged it with his toe. "Think it still works?"

Mila lifted it gently. Something inside shifted with a soft chime. "Only one way to find out."

***

Mila set the music box on the attic floor. The wood was smooth and cool under her fingers. A tiny brass crank stuck out from the side, green with age.

"Go on," Sam said. "Turn it."

Mila gripped the crank and wound it slowly. It resisted, then gave. Click, click, click. She let go.

At first, nothing happened. Then a thin, clear note rose from the box. It was the lullaby. Not a hum this time, but a real song, small and bright, like a music box should be. The notes drifted through the attic and down the stairs.

"That's your grandma's song," Sam said.

"She must have wound it every night," Mila said. "That's why the house hummed."

They sat on the floor and listened. The song played on and on, gentle and steady. Mila felt something loosen in her chest. The house wasn't spooky. It was lonely. It had been waiting for someone to hum back.

"You know," Sam said, "my mom said your grandma used to leave this on the windowsill so the whole street could hear."

Mila smiled. She carried the box to the round window and set it on the sill. Moonlight fell on the little stars carved into the lid.

The song played until it wound down. Then the attic was quiet, but it was a good quiet.

"Goodnight, Grandma," Mila whispered.

She and Sam went downstairs. That night, Mila slept in her new room without the light on. Every evening after that, she wound the music box and left it on the windowsill. The whole street could hear it, just like before. And Mila began to think of the old house as home.
