The town flame lived in a stone bowl at the top of the square, and every morning the lamplighter unlocked the iron grate around it so people could take a little home. Mara queued with her family’s lamp, a squat brass thing with a handle worn smooth by three generations of hands. The flame burned tall and orange, and when she touched her wick to it, the fire caught with a sound like a breath. She carried it carefully across the cobbles, one hand cupped around the glass. But halfway home she noticed the flame was smaller than it should be. It leaned to one side, thin and pale, as if it had been running. She stopped and held the lamp up. The flame flickered, then steadied, but it stayed low.

At the corner, the lamplighter was trimming the wicks of the street lamps with a long brass pole. His name was Corin, and he had been doing the job since before Mara was born. “Something wrong with your lamp?” he asked, without turning around.

“It’s burning low,” she said. “It was fine at the square.”

Corin glanced at it, then shrugged. “Damp wick. Happens this time of year. It’ll pick up.”

“It’s not the wick,” Mara said. “It caught properly. I saw it.”

He turned then, and his face was tired in a way she hadn’t noticed before. “The flame does what it does,” he said. “Don’t worry at it.” He went back to his trimming, and the click of his shears sounded very final.

Mara walked on, but she kept looking down at the lamp. The flame had shrunk to the size of a thumbnail, and its colour had gone from orange to a thin, worried yellow. At home she set it on the kitchen table, and her father lit the stove from it without comment. The flame caught, but it caught slowly, as if it had to think about it. Mara watched until her father told her to stop hovering and eat her supper. All evening, the lamp sat in the corner, and the little flame never grew.

***

After dark, Mara went back. The square was empty, and the town flame was not the tall orange thing she had left that morning. It had pulled in on itself, a blue bead no bigger than a marble, and it gave off almost no light. The iron grate stood open, and Corin was sitting on the steps of the fountain with his head in his hands.

“You knew,” Mara said.

He looked up. In the blue glow he looked older than he had at noon. “I knew it was low. I didn’t know it was this low.”

“You said it was fine.”

“I lied.” He rubbed his face. “It’s been fading for months. I kept thinking it would come back. The flame has always come back.”

Mara crouched beside the bowl. Up close, the blue bead was beautiful in a cold way, like a star seen through water. It did not warm her face. “What happens if it goes out?”

Corin was quiet for a moment. “The lamps go out. All of them. And they don’t light again from a cold bowl.”

“So what do we do?”

“I don’t know,” he said. “I’ve never seen it this bad.” He stood up and brushed ash from his knees. “There’s a ledger. In the keeper’s room under the square. My grandmother kept it, and her mother before that. It might say something.”

Mara looked at the bead of flame, then at the dark windows of the houses around the square. Somewhere a dog barked, and a shutter banged in the wind. “Then we’d better read it,” she said.

Corin nodded slowly. “Come on, then.” He picked up his pole and led her toward the low door at the base of the fountain, and the blue light followed them like a curious eye.

The keeper's room was smaller than Mara expected, and colder. Shelves of ledgers climbed the walls like a second set of stairs, and dust hung in the blue light that followed them in. Corin set his pole against the door and pulled down a book bound in cracked leather. "Sit," he said. "This will take a while."

Mara sat on a stool and watched him turn pages. The writing was small and slanting, the ink brown with age. Some pages had drawings: a bowl, a flame, a hand. "What does it say?" she asked.

"It says the flame is not a fire like other fires." Corin ran his finger under a line. "It doesn't eat wood or oil. It eats what people give up."

"Give up?"

"Things they let go of. Things that cost them something." He turned another page. "When the town was young, people brought offerings. A loaf, a coin, a ribbon from a child's hair. The flame took them and burned brighter."

Mara looked at the blue bead in the bowl on the table between them. It pulsed faintly, like a slow heartbeat. "So we just bring offerings?"

"It's not that simple." Corin's voice was flat. "The ledger says the flame can tell the difference between a gift and a show. If you give something you don't care about, it does nothing. It has to hurt." He closed the book. "And lately, nobody's been giving anything that hurts. They bring old bread and broken buttons. The flame's been fading for months."

Mara thought of the square on market day, the stalls and the laughter, the way people hurried past the fountain without looking. "What did your grandmother give?"

Corin was quiet. "Her wedding ring. She told me once. She said it was the hardest thing she ever did."

The blue light flickered, and for a moment the room seemed darker. Mara stood up. "Then I'll give something."

"It has to be real," Corin said. "Not something you can replace."

"I know." She was already walking toward the door.

***

Her room was cold and tidy, the way it always was when her father worked late. Mara knelt by the bed and pulled out the wooden box from underneath. The music box was small enough to fit in two hands, its lid painted with a faded rose. Her mother had left it on the kitchen table the morning she went away, and Mara had never played it in front of anyone.

She carried it back through the dark streets. The blue bead had followed her home and now it followed her back, a cold star bobbing at her shoulder. Corin was waiting by the fountain, his face pale in the strange light.

"That's it?" he asked.

"That's it." She held the box out over the bowl. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. "What do I do?"

"Just give it. Mean it."

Mara opened the lid. The little tune began to play, thin and sweet, and she remembered her mother humming it while she washed the dishes. Then she tipped the box and let it fall into the bowl.

The flame guttered. For a moment it flared up, blue and white, and Mara's heart lifted. Then it sank back down, smaller than before, and the music stopped.

Corin let out a breath. "It wasn't enough."

Mara stared at the box, half-sunk in the bowl, its painted rose turning black at the edges. "Why not?" Her voice cracked. "It was the only thing I had left of her."

"Maybe that's the problem," Corin said quietly. "Maybe it's not about what you lose. Maybe it's about what you keep."

The blue bead flickered, and in its light Mara saw the square around her: the dark windows, the closed doors, the whole town waiting for a light that was dying. She had given the box. She had meant it. But something in her chest was still locked tight, and the flame knew it.

The blue bead of flame threw just enough light for Mara to see the faces around her. Corin's, worried and old. The baker's, half-hidden behind his door. The schoolteacher's, watching from her window with her shawl pulled tight. Everyone who had come to the square to see the lamp lit, and stayed to watch it die.

"What do you keep?" Corin asked, not unkindly.

Mara wanted to say something brave. Instead she heard herself say, "My mother didn't leave because of the money. That's what everyone says. That she went to the city for work." Her hands were shaking. "She went because of me. Because I told her I hated her. I was nine and she wouldn't let me go to the fair, and I screamed it at her in the kitchen, and she packed her bag that night."

The baker's door creaked open a little wider. Mara didn't look up.

"She wrote letters for a year," she went on. "I never answered them. I burned them in the stove one at a time. And then she stopped writing, and I told myself it was her fault, that she was the one who left." Her voice was steady now, which surprised her. "But it wasn't. It was mine. I've kept that in my chest for four years and I thought if I never said it out loud it wouldn't be true."

Corin was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "That's it. That's what the flame wants."

"What?"

"Not a thing. A truth. The fire in this town was never fed by what people gave away. It was fed by what they carried and never spoke." He nodded at the bowl. "The box was a gift. A good one. But it wasn't the thing you were hiding."

Mara looked at the flame. It had stopped sinking. The blue bead was still small, but it had steadied, as if it were listening.

"Say the rest," Corin said. "Whatever's left."

She took a breath. "I'm sorry," she said, and her voice carried across the square. "I'm sorry I burned your letters. I'm sorry I made you leave. I don't know if you're ever coming back, and I don't know if you'd want to see me if you did. But I'm not going to pretend anymore that it wasn't my fault."

Someone in the crowd shifted their feet. The schoolteacher had her hand over her mouth. Mara felt her face go hot, and for a second she wanted to run. But she stayed.

The flame did not move.

"Was that not enough either?" she asked Corin, and her voice broke.

"Give it a moment," he said. "Truth takes a while to catch."

***

For three heartbeats nothing happened. Mara could hear the wind in the gutters and her own breathing, loud in her ears. Then the blue bead quivered, like a drop of water about to fall.

It did not fall. It rose.

The flame climbed the wick in a thin gold thread, then thickened, then caught, and the glass chimney of the town lamp filled with light so bright that Mara had to shield her eyes. Heat rolled off it. The square jumped into color: red brick, green paint on the bakery door, the yellow of the schoolteacher's shawl.

And then, one by one, the other lamps began to light.

Mara turned in a slow circle. Down the lane, the lamp outside the smithy bloomed gold. Across the square, the lamp by the well caught and steadied. She heard a shout from the harbor road, then another, and another, as the light ran outward through the town like water finding its way downhill. Windows glowed. Doors opened. People stepped out into the cold with their coats half-buttoned, staring up at the lamps as if they had never seen them before.

The baker came down his steps and stood beside Mara. "Well," he said. "I'll be." He looked at her, then at the bowl on the ground. "That was a brave thing you said."

"It wasn't brave," Mara said. "It was just true."

"Same thing, most days." He went back inside and came out with a broom, and began sweeping the square as if it were an ordinary evening.

Corin lifted the town lamp and hung it back on its post, where it belonged. The light settled into the glass and held steady. "It'll burn now," he said. "For a while yet. But it'll need tending. Same as always."

Mara picked up her music box from the bowl. The rose on the lid was ruined, and the tune would not play again, she could tell by the weight of it. She held it for a moment, then put it in her pocket.

"Walk you home?" Corin asked.

"No," she said. "I know the way."

She went down the lane with the lamps lighting ahead of her, one after another, all the way to her own door. Her hands were empty. Her chest was not locked anymore. It felt strange and light, like a room with the windows opened after a long winter, and she walked slowly so she could feel it.

The light stayed on behind her all the way home.
