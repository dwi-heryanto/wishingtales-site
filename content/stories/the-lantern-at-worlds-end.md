Mara was on the roof when the star fell.

She had climbed out her window the way she did most clear nights, with a blanket over her shoulders and her back against the warm chimney. From up there the island was a dark hand in the water, and the sea beyond it was black and flat and endless. Her mother had called that water the Long Sleeve, because it reached out past everything and never let go. No boat from the island had ever gone out into it and come back.

Something slid across the sky, quick and thin, and then brighter, and then it dropped.

It fell beyond the last reef, far out where the water turned to ink. For a moment the sea lit up silver, like a coin catching a lamp, and then it was dark again, and the wind kept on doing what the wind does.

Mara sat very still. Her heart was going hard, but it wasn't fear, not exactly. It was the feeling she got when she found a word she'd been hunting for. There was a thing out there now. A real thing, burning in the dark at the bottom of the world.

She was down the ladder and inside before she knew she'd decided anything. Her father was asleep in the chair by the stove, a mending needle still hooked in his thumb. Mara put the blanket over him and stood looking at the hook by the door, where her mother's lantern hung.

It was old brass, dented on one side, with a wick that had been trimmed so many times it barely had a head left. Her mother had carried it on the water for twenty years. When she died, the whole island had come to the funeral, and afterward nobody had said a word about the lantern, so Mara had taken it down and kept it.

"You're not going out there," her father said. He hadn't opened his eyes.

"I just want to see it."

"See what?"

He sat up, slow, and looked at her with the tired patience of a man who had already lost too much to be surprised by anything.

"There's a light in the Long Sleeve," Mara said. "It fell tonight. I saw where."

"Then leave it where it fell."

"Somebody ought to know what it is."

Her father rubbed his face. For a long while he didn't answer, and the stove ticked as it cooled.

"Your mother used to say that," he said at last. "Word for word. And she was right more often than she was safe."

Mara waited.

"Go see Iven in the morning," he said, and lay back down. "If anyone's fool enough to take you, it'll be him."

***

Iven's boat sat on the shingle below the fish sheds, and it looked less like a boat than a boat's opinion of one. Every plank was a different colour. The sail was stitched from at least four dead sails. He was kneeling in the sand beside it, greasing a pulley, when Mara came down the path with the lantern in her hand.

"You're early," he said, without looking up.

"I didn't say I was coming."

"You're here, aren't you?" He wiped his fingers on his trousers and squinted at the sea. "And you've brought your mother's lamp, which means you want to go somewhere dark. Sit down. You're making the path nervous."

She sat on an upturned crate. Iven was older than old, with a white beard cut short and hands like knotted rope, and everyone on the island said he'd been a salvager before the reef took his hearing on one side.

"A star fell last night," Mara said. "Out past the reef. I want to go and find it."

Iven looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed, one short breath of it, not unkind.

"You and half the island, when we were young," he said. "There's a reason nobody goes into the Sleeve, girl. That water doesn't give things back."

"Then why is your boat patched for deep water?"

His laugh stopped. He looked at the boat, then at her, and something in his face closed like a door.

"Because I'm a stubborn old man who doesn't know when to stop mending things," he said. "Give me the lamp."

She handed it over. He turned it in his hands, thumbed the dents, and his mouth went crooked.

"Your mother carried this out past the reef more than once," he said quietly. "Did she ever tell you that?"

"No."

"No. She wouldn't have." He set the lantern down in the boat, in the bow, where it would throw light ahead of them. "We leave on the tide. Bring a coat, and bring nothing you'd grieve to lose."

Mara ran home and was back before the sun had properly cleared the hill. Iven had the sail up and the oars shipped, and he nodded at the bow without a word.

The tide took them out, slow and sure, past the last of the moorings and the reef buoys clanging their thin bells. The island shrank behind them until it was only a smudge of grey with the chimney smoke standing straight up off it. Mara looked back once. Then the Long Sleeve opened its arms, and the water under the hull went from green to grey to a black that gave nothing back.

The storm came out of the north with no warning at all. One moment the sky was a hard, bright blue; the next it was the colour of a bruise, and the wind hit the sail like a fist. Iven shouted something Mara couldn't hear and pointed at the halyard. She scrambled to loose it, but the rope was wet and stiff and the wind had already made its own plans. The mast gave a crack like a knuckle, and then it went over the side, taking the sail with it, and the boat rolled so far that Mara saw the black water where the sky should have been.

"Cut it loose!" Iven roared, and she sawed at the tangled rigging with the knife he threw her. The mast went, and the sail went, and the boat came upright with a lurch that knocked her to her knees. Rain came sideways, stinging like gravel. Iven had the oars out and was pulling against the swell, his face set, his breath a rasp. The lantern in the bow swung on its hook, and its little light held steady, which somehow made the rest of the dark seem worse.

They rode it out for hours. Mara bailed with a tin cup until her arms shook. The sea kept trying to come aboard, and Iven kept pushing it back with the oars and with a kind of stubborn silence. When the wind finally dropped, the rain went with it, and the boat sat in a flat, greasy calm under a sky that had forgotten how to show a single star.

Iven shipped the oars and sat looking at his hands. "I've been this way before," he said. "You asked me that first night if I'd ever crossed. I said no. That was a lie."

Mara said nothing. The cup was still in her hand, and she set it down very carefully.

"It was a long time ago. There was another boat, and two of us in it, and a reason to go that seemed good enough at the time." He rubbed his thumb across the dent in the gunwale. "The valley took the other one. I came back alone, and I told myself I'd never come again. Then a girl turned up on my step with a lantern, and I thought—" He stopped. "It doesn't matter what I thought."

"Who was it?" Mara asked.

"My brother," Iven said. "His name was Tobin. He was the one who could read the water. I was only the muscle." He picked up the oars again. "We're near the drop. I can feel it in the hull. Whatever you came for, it's down there, and I won't pretend I know how to bring you back."

The water changed as he spoke. It went from black to a deep, cold green, and then the green thinned, and Mara saw the light coming up from below.

***

The drowned valley opened under them like a held breath. The boat drifted over roofs and chimneys, over a bell tower with its bell still hanging, over streets where fish moved in slow shoals between the houses. Everything was green and quiet and impossibly still. Mara leaned over the side and saw a doorway with a door still on its hinges, and beyond it a table set for a meal that would never be eaten.

And in the middle of it all, in what must once have been a square, the star lay burning. It was not like a fire. It was a white, cold light, the colour of the moon on frost, and it lit the whole valley with a glow that made shadows in the wrong places. It was the size of a sheep, and it was beautiful, and it was wrong. Mara felt it in her teeth.

"There," she said. "That's it. That's what I saw fall."

Iven didn't answer at once. He was staring at the bell tower, at something caught in the doorway of a house. "I see it," he said at last, and his voice was flat.

Mara took the lantern from the bow and held it up. Its small yellow light made a puddle around the boat, and the star's white glare seemed to lean away from it, as if the two lights were not friends. "I'm going down," she said.

"No," Iven said. "We'll rig a line. You hold the lantern, and I'll—"

But the lantern went out. There was no gust, no drop, no reason. One moment it was burning, and the next the wick was cold and the glass was dark in her hand. And the valley's light went out with it, all at once, as if the star had been waiting for exactly that.

The dark that came was not the dark of a moonless night. It was thick and close and it pressed against her face. Mara heard Iven take a breath to speak, and then heard him not speak, and then heard a sound she had never heard from him before: a wet, dragging sound, and a splash.

"Iven?"

Nothing. Then his voice, from lower down and further away than it should have been. "Mara. The boat. Get back to the—" The water closed over the words.

She couldn't see him. She couldn't see the star. She could see nothing at all, and the boat was tilting, and somewhere below her in the black water the old man who had lied to her and come anyway was sinking.

The dark pressed against her eyes like a wet cloth. Somewhere below, Iven was sinking, and the star was sinking too, and the lantern was dead in her hand. She could not see either of them.

Her fingers found the rope. It ran from the bow, past her feet, down into the black water where the star burned cold. She had tied it herself, with a knot her mother had taught her, and she knew exactly how to undo it.

"Mara!" Iven's voice came up in a bubble, thin and far away.

She could save the star. She could pull it up, and the light would come back, and she would see everything. That was the whole reason she had come. The star was the thing she had wanted since she was small enough to sit on the harbour wall and count the lights that fell.

But Iven was not a light. He was an old man with a patched boat and a lie in his mouth, and he was sinking.

Her hands moved before her mind did. She sawed at the rope with the knife from her belt, once, twice, and the knot gave. The star went free. Its cold glow drifted down and away, a pale coin spinning into a well, and the black water took it.

Then she dove.

The cold hit her like a wall. She kicked down, blind, her hands sweeping through nothing until her fingers caught cloth. Iven's coat. She got a fist in it and pulled, and he came up heavy and limp, and she pulled again, and her lungs began to burn.

The surface was further than she thought. When she broke it, she gasped so hard it hurt. Iven's head lolled against her shoulder. He was not moving.

"Hold on," she said, though he could not hear her. "Hold on."

The boat was a black shape against the black sky. She swam for it with one arm, dragging him, and the valley seemed to breathe around her, cold and patient. Her hand found the hull. She got Iven's arms over the side, and then his chest, and then she rolled him in like a sack of grain, and she climbed in after him and lay on the boards shaking.

For a long time there was only the sound of her own breathing.

***

Iven coughed up water at dawn. Mara had wrapped him in the sail and sat beside him all night, and when the sky went grey he opened his eyes and looked at her.

"You cut it loose," he said. His voice was a ruin.

"Yes."

"The star."

"Yes."

He closed his eyes. "I told you I'd made the crossing before. I didn't tell you I lost my brother to it. He went after the light and I couldn't hold him." He was quiet a moment. "I thought if I brought you, I might get it right this time."

Mara looked at the empty lantern in her lap. The glass was cold and dark, and she turned it over in her hands.

"Why did it go out?" she asked. "The lantern. It never goes out."

Iven shifted, and his hand found hers. "Your mother's lantern? It's a keeper's lantern. It burns for the one who carries it, not for the light it shows. When you stopped looking after yourself and started looking after me, it had done its work. It didn't need to burn anymore."

Mara stared at him. "That's not how lanterns work."

"No," he said. "That's how that one works."

She sat with it. The sea was flat and grey and the sky was going gold at the edge. Somewhere below them the star lay in the drowned valley, and she would never see it, and she found that she did not mind as much as she thought she would.

They rowed home slowly, with the sail gone and the mast broken, and the island grew out of the sea like a word she had been trying to remember. Her father was on the harbour wall. Mara saw him stand up when the boat came into view, and then she saw him run.

When they reached the stones, her father caught her and held her, and said nothing at all. Mara held the dark lantern between them, and for the first time she understood what it had been for.

"I didn't get it," she said into her father's shoulder.

He pulled back and looked at her. "You came home," he said. "That's what the light was for."

And Mara looked at the empty glass in her hand, and the sun came up over the water, and she nodded.
