The brass bell above the door rang twice, which meant someone had come in fast and then stopped dead. Juno looked up from the counter she was wiping. A girl stood on the mat, breathing like she had run the whole length of Corvid Lane.

"I've lost my kite," the girl said.

Juno put down her cloth. "What colour is it?"

"Red," said the girl. "Red with a black tail, and one strut is longer than the other because I fixed it myself. The string is wound on a wooden spool and the spool has a chip out of one side."

Juno waited for more, but there was no more. The girl just stood there, holding her own elbows.

"That's very exact," Juno said.

"I know what it looks like," said the girl. "I've looked at it a lot."

Oda came through from the back room with her reading glasses on her forehead. She took them off and looked at the girl for a long moment.

"Wren," Oda said. "Sit down. Juno, put the kettle on the little flame."

Wren sat on the stool by the window, where the rolled paper stood in a barrel like a bundle of spears. Her hands were dusty. There was a red thread caught in her sleeve.

"When did you last have it?" Juno asked, filling the kettle.

"Saturday," Wren said. "On the hill behind the bakery. The wind took it, and I watched it go. It went over the roofs and I couldn't follow."

"And you want a map to where it landed," Juno said.

Wren looked at the window, at the lane shining outside. "I want a map to where it went," she said, very quietly, "because I don't think it came down at all."

***

Oda laid a sheet of brown paper on the counter and set the pencils in their row. Black. Red. Blue, in its drawer, closed.

"The kite is not the loss," Oda said.

Juno's hand stopped over the paper. "She told us exactly what it looks like. Every strut."

"Yes," said Oda. "That is how you describe a thing you are holding on to."

"She wants it back."

"She wants to know where it went," Oda said. "Those are different maps. Draw the one she asked for and she will be sad in a new way."

Juno pressed her pencil point into the corner of the paper. "Then what do I draw?"

"You are the mapmaker," Oda said, and went to pour the tea.

Juno looked at Wren, who was watching the lane as if the kite might still be in the air. The red thread on her sleeve had come loose and hung down.

"What does the hill look like from up there?" Juno asked.

Wren turned. "The roofs go on forever," she said. "And there's a chimney with a broken pot, and beyond it the lane bends and you can't see the end."

Juno picked up the black pencil. "Show me the chimney," she said. "I'll start there."

Oda, at the kettle, said nothing at all, which Juno had learned was its own kind of answer.

Juno drew the hill first, a long black curve like a sleeping animal. Then the roofs, one after another, each a small square with its own chimney. The black pencil was for what is there, and the rooftops were certainly there. She drew the broken pot on the chimney, two lines for the crack, a curl of smoke that was not smoke but a loose tile.

"That's it," Wren said, leaning close. "That's exactly it."

Juno switched to red. Red was for what is not there. She drew the kite string first, a thin red thread from a rooftop to the sky, and then the kite itself, a diamond shape with a tail. Her hand hovered. The kite should have been in the sky. She drew it there anyway, red and empty, a shape that was missing.

"It doesn't look lost," Wren said. "It looks like it's still flying."

"That's the point," Juno said, though she was not sure it was.

Oda came back with the tea and looked over her shoulder. "Red for what is not there," she said. "Good. Now show me what is."

Juno looked at the paper. The black roof, the red kite. There was a gap between them, a place where the string should have touched the tiles. She reached for the blue pencil.

The drawer was stiff. Juno had never opened it before. The blue pencil lay in a groove of its own, shorter than the others, worn down like a stub of chalk. She picked it up and drew one line, just one, from the red kite down to the black roof.

The line came out blue and true. And beside it, where she had not drawn anything, a small square appeared on the paper, a rooftop she had never seen, with a broken pot and a window and a boy's shape sitting on the edge.

Wren went very still. "Who's that?" she asked.

"I don't know," Juno said. Her ears were hot. She put the blue pencil back and shut the drawer.

***

Corvid Lane was busy with afternoon. The bakery door stood open and the smell of bread walked out into the street. Juno held the map flat in both hands, and Wren walked beside her, reading the roofs.

"Left at the bakery," Wren said. "Then the alley with the blue door."

They found the alley. A fire escape climbed the wall like a black ladder, and the map showed it as a red line, which meant it was not on the map but should be. Juno looked up. The steps went to a rooftop with a broken pot on the chimney.

"Up?" Wren said.

"Up," Juno said, and went first.

The fire escape rang under their feet, a low metal sound. At the top, the wind was stronger, and the whole lane lay below them like a drawing, the bakery, the bus stop, the bend where you could not see the end.

A boy sat on the edge of the roof with his back to them. Beside him, tied to a chimney with a long red string, a kite leaned into the wind.

Wren made a small sound. "That's mine," she whispered. "That's my kite."

The boy turned. He was younger than Juno, maybe nine, with a smudge of dirt on his cheek and a look on his face like someone caught in the rain without a coat.

"I found it," he said quickly. "It came down on the roof two days ago. I've been keeping it safe."

"You've been flying it," Wren said.

The boy looked at the kite, then at his shoes. "Only a little," he said. "Only when the wind was right."

Juno stood between them with the map in her hands. The blue line she had drawn was still there, bright and true, pointing at the boy as much as at the kite. She had thought the map was about a lost thing. It was about two people on one roof, and only one kite.

The boy's name was Tam. He said it twice, as if he wanted them to know it. "I wasn't going to keep it," he told the roof tiles. "I was going to bring it down. Every morning I was going to bring it down."

"But you didn't," said Wren.

"No." Tam rubbed the dirt on his cheek. "It's the best kite I've ever held. It goes up straight and it stays up. I fly it and I feel like the roof is the ground."

Wren walked to the chimney and touched the red string. The kite swung and pulled, and she let it pull her. Juno watched the map go slack in her hands.

"I saved for two years," Wren said quietly. "I bought it the day before it went missing. My little brother held the string for one minute and then let go."

Tam went very still. "You have a brother?"

"He's five. He cried all evening."

Juno waited for Wren to take the string and walk away. That was what the map had promised, after all. A lost thing, found. But Wren did not take it.

"You can't fly it from inside a house," she said at last. "You need a roof." She looked at Juno. "Does this roof belong to anyone?"

"It belongs to Tam," Juno said. "He's here every day."

Wren nodded slowly. "Then I'll leave it up here. You keep it safe on weekdays, and I'll come on Saturdays, and we'll fly it together. My brother can watch from the fire escape."

Tam's mouth opened and shut. "You'd trust me?"

"You kept it safe," said Wren. "You just kept it a little too well."

Juno folded the map along its creases. The blue line was still glowing faintly, and looking at it made her chest feel tight, like a held breath. She had drawn a true thing without being asked. Nobody had given her the pencil.

***

The shop was dim when they got back. The brass bell rang once, and Oda was behind the counter with her glasses on, sorting a box of buttons.

"Kite found?" she asked, not looking up.

"Found," said Juno.

"Good." Oda tipped a handful of buttons onto the counter. "And the map?"

Juno set it down. The black lines were steady. The red lines showed the roof edge and the chimney and the place where the kite had landed. The blue line ran straight from the shop door to a boy's shoes.

Oda looked at it for a long moment. She did not ask about the blue pencil. She did not say the rule out loud. She only picked up the map and laid it flat in the drawer where finished maps went.

"You'll want to sharpen that blue one," she said. "It's drawing soft."

Juno stared at her. "You knew?"

"I know what a soft pencil looks like." Oda put her glasses away. "And I know a map that tells the truth is worth the trouble it causes."

Juno took the pencils from the counter one by one. Black, red, blue. She set them in the tin in their places, and her hand stayed on the blue one a second longer than it should have.

"Next time," she said, "I'll ask first."

"Next time," said Oda, "you'll know when to ask."

The bell rang as a customer came in from the lane, and Juno reached for a fresh sheet of brown paper. It was still warm from the drawer.
