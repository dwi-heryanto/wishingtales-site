Elara climbed the lighthouse stairs two at a time, her boots clumping on the iron steps. At the top, the great bell hung in its wooden frame, ready to warn ships away from the rocks. But when she reached the bell room, the rope dangled loose and the bell was gone.

"Dad!" she called. "The bell's missing!"

Her father came up, wiping his hands on a rag. He stared at the empty frame. "That bell's been here a hundred years," he said. "Who would take it?"

Outside, the sea looked strange. A grey wall of fog was rolling in fast, swallowing the horizon. Elara shivered. Without the bell, ships would sail straight into the rocks.

On the floor, something caught her eye. A long white feather, bigger than any gull's. It shimmered like it was made of moonlight. She picked it up, and it felt cool and light in her hand.

"Dad, look at this."

He frowned. "That's no bird I know."

The feather twitched, pointing toward the cliff path. Elara's heart thumped. "It's showing me the way," she said. "I have to follow it."

"Elara, the fog's coming in. It's not safe."

"But the bell…" She looked at the feather, then at her father. "I'll be quick. I promise."

He hesitated, then nodded. "Take the lantern. And be back before the fog reaches the point."

Elara grabbed the lantern and ran outside. The feather floated ahead of her, glowing faintly, leading her down the cliff steps toward the shore.

***

The path ended at a small cove where an old sailor sat mending a net. His name was Tomas, and he'd fished these waters since before Elara was born. He looked up as she scrambled down the last rocks.

"Well, if it isn't the keeper's girl," he said. "You're in a hurry."

"The bell's gone, Tomas. The fog bell from the lighthouse. Have you seen anything?"

Tomas set down his net. "Gone, you say?" He scratched his chin. "That'll be the wind spirits. They come on nights like this, when the fog rolls thick. They love shiny things that make noise."

Elara held out the feather. "I found this. It led me here."

Tomas took it and turned it over. "Wind spirit feather," he said. "They leave them behind sometimes. Means they want you to follow."

"Follow where?"

He pointed toward the cliffs. "There's a sea cave just around the point. At low tide you can walk right in. That's where they'd hide a prize like a bell."

Elara looked at the dark water swirling at the cave mouth. "It's getting dark."

"Aye. And the tide'll be turning soon. You'd have to be brave to go in there alone."

She gripped the lantern tighter. "I'm not alone. I've got the feather."

Tomas smiled. "That you do. But listen: the cave forks. Take the left passage. The right one floods first."

"Left passage," she repeated. "Got it."

"And girl…" He caught her arm. "If you find the bell, ring it. I'll hear it from here, and I'll come running."

Elara nodded and headed for the cave, the feather bobbing ahead of her like a tiny moon.

The cave mouth was a black yawn in the cliff. Elara stopped at the edge of the wet stones and held her lantern high. Beyond the first few steps, the light gave up and the dark began.

"You've been in dark before," she told herself. Her voice bounced off the walls and came back smaller.

Water dripped somewhere deep inside. The feather drifted forward, glowing faintly, and waited for her. She took one step. Then another. The ceiling pressed low and the walls were slick with green weed. Her heart banged like a fist on a door.

"Feather," she whispered, "don't go far."

It bobbed ahead, patient as a firefly. She followed it past a fork where the right passage gurgled with rising water. Left, she thought. Tomas said left.

The left passage opened into a wide round room. Salt crystals glittered on the roof like a sky turned upside down. And there, on a rock in the middle, hung the lighthouse bell.

Elara gasped. It was silver and small and shining, with a rope tied through its handle.

Curled around the rock beneath it was a seal. Grey and whiskered, it slept with its nose tucked under its flipper. One eye opened. Then the other. It blinked at her lantern and gave a low, grumbling bark.

"Oh," said Elara. "Hello."

The seal did not move.

***

Elara crouched low so she would not look big. The seal watched her with round dark eyes. Its whiskers twitched.

"I'm not here to bother you," she said softly. "I just need the bell. The fog's coming, and the boats can't see."

The seal huffed and settled its chin back on the rock. It clearly had no plans to move.

Elara thought of her father out on the water, squinting into grey. Then she remembered something. Seals liked music. Old Tomas hummed to them sometimes from his boat.

So she sang. It was a small song, one her mother used to sing while mending nets, about a boat coming home across a silver sea. Her voice shook at first, then grew steady.

The seal lifted its head. Its whiskers swayed. Slowly, it slid off the rock and glided into the water, turning in a slow circle as if dancing.

"Thank you," Elara breathed.

She reached up and worked the rope free. The bell was heavier than she expected, cold and smooth in her hands. She hugged it to her chest.

Then she heard it. A rush and a slap. Water was pouring into the cave mouth, climbing the walls fast. The way out was gone, swallowed by the tide.

"No," she whispered. "No, no."

The seal surfaced beside her and nudged her elbow, as if to say: this way.

The seal swam to the far wall of the cave, where a thin crack ran upward into darkness. Elara waded after it, holding the bell high. Cold water reached her waist, then her chest. The seal waited at a ledge of dry stone, and above it the crack widened into a chimney.

"Up there?" she asked.

The seal slapped its tail once against the rock. Elara looped the bell's rope over her shoulder and pulled herself onto the ledge. The stone was slick and gritty under her hands. She wedged a foot into the crack and pushed. Her knees scraped. Water rushed below her, louder now, filling the cave she had left behind.

Halfway up, the chimney pinched so tight she had to turn sideways. Panic fluttered in her chest like a trapped bird. *You have climbed the cliff path a hundred times,* she told herself. *Same rock. Same hands.* She breathed out slowly and kept going.

A grey coin of light appeared above her. Elara reached for it and hauled herself out onto the headland, gulping the cold, clean air. Fog pressed close around her, soft and blind. Nobody would see her here. Nobody would hear her shout.

But they might hear the bell.

She unwound the rope and gripped it tight. The bell swung, and she let it strike the stone. *Clang.* The sound rolled out over the water, deep and steady. She rang it again. And again. Each ring felt like a word sent into the fog.

"Papa!" she called between rings. "Papa, the bell! Follow the bell!"

***

Out on the water, Elara's father had been rowing in slow circles, calling her name into the grey. His arms ached. His throat was raw. Then a sound cut through the fog, low and familiar, a bell he had heard every evening of his life. He stopped rowing and listened.

"That's my bell," he said aloud.

He turned the boat toward the sound. The rings came again, closer, then closer still. A dark shape rose out of the fog: the headland, with his daughter standing on it, muddy to the elbows, ringing the lighthouse bell like a town crier.

"Elara!" he shouted.

"Papa!" She nearly dropped the rope. "I found it! It was in the sea cave, and there was a seal, and the tide came in…"

"Tell me on the way home," he said, his voice wobbling. "Get in. Careful now."

She climbed down and passed him the bell first, then tumbled into the boat. He wrapped his coat around her shoulders without a word. The bell sat between them, silent and heavy, and the fog began to thin as they rowed, as if the sound had pushed it back.

Back at the lighthouse, they carried the bell up the spiral stairs together. Her father looped the rope over the hook, and Elara set it swinging. *Clang.* Clear and bright, out across the quiet sea.

"You went into the dark alone," her father said.

"I was scared," said Elara. "But I sang, and the dark got smaller."

He put his hand on her wet hair. Below them, the fog lifted off the water, and the first stars came out.
