Tavi woke because the roof of the den was too loud. Rain had beaten on it all night, a steady drum she had fallen asleep to, and now the drum had stopped. The silence pulled her out of sleep the way a cold nose pulls a pup from a warm pile.

She padded out onto the ledge. The valley lay below her, and it was wrong. No water ran down the stone channel. The stream was a string of shrinking pools, and the pools were sinking even as she watched, mud rings widening around them. The sky above the far hills was a hard, clean blue, scrubbed of every cloud.

"Maren," Tavi called back into the den. "Maren, come and look."

The old badger came out slowly, one paw at a time, the way she did everything now. She stood beside Tavi and did not speak for a long while. Her grey muzzle lifted, testing the air.

"Gone," Maren said at last. "The whole storm. Not rained out. Taken."

"Taken where?"

"Up." Maren nodded toward the crag, the black tooth of rock that split the valley's north end. "There's a magpie lives up there. Collects things. Shiny things, mostly. But lately she's been collecting bigger."

Tavi looked at the stream, at the fish turning in the last deep pool with their backs showing. "A bird can't carry a storm."

"A bird can carry what a storm is made of," said Maren. "Piece by piece, if she's stubborn. And she is." The badger settled onto her haunches with a grunt. "I'm too old for that climb. You're not."

"What do I say to her?"

"Ask for it back. Politely, first." Maren's eyes were tired and steady. "Then not politely."

Tavi drank from the last pool, which tasted of mud, and started up the deer track before the sun cleared the ridge.

The climb took most of the morning. The crag was all loose shale and narrow ledges, and twice Tavi had to jump gaps where the path had fallen away. Wind combed the grass flat. Near the top she found the nest: a great tangled basket of sticks and wire and bottle caps, wedged into a cleft, and above it the sky was a bruise of black cloud that did not move.

The magpie was waiting. She was bigger than Tavi expected, black and white and hard-eyed, and she stood on the nest's rim with her head tilted.

"You're the fox from the south den," the magpie said. "You've come a long way to be disappointed."

"You took our storm," Tavi said. "The river's dying. The fish are dying. Give it back."

"Give it back," the magpie echoed, in a voice that was almost Tavi's own. She laughed, a dry clatter. "Everything in this valley belongs to somebody who wants it. I wanted this."

"It isn't yours."

"Finders keepers, little fox. I found it loose in the sky and I brought it home." The magpie walked along the nest rim. "Do you know what happens to a valley with no storm? It gets quiet. Quiet is good for me. Quiet means nobody comes up here to take what I've got."

Tavi's fur stood up along her spine. "You'd let the whole valley dry for quiet?"

"I'd let the whole valley dry for a great deal less," said the magpie. "Go home. Drink your mud. It's not my problem."

Tavi did not move. She looked past the magpie, into the dark of the nest, and saw something she had not expected: three small shapes pressed together in the sticks, their feathers dull, their eyes shut.

"Your chicks," she said.

The magpie's head snapped around. For the first time her voice lost its mockery. "Don't look at them."

The magpie hopped between Tavi and the nest, wings half-spread. "You've seen them now. Are you satisfied?"

Tavi took a slow step sideways, keeping her eyes on the three small shapes. One chick opened its beak, a thin yellow gap, and no sound came out. Its feathers looked wet, though the air was dry as dust.

"They're cold," Tavi said.

"They're always cold." The magpie's voice cracked. "Three nights ago the wind dropped and the frost came down and it hasn't lifted since. I stole a storm to keep them warm, and now I can't let it go." She jabbed her beak toward the valley. "Down there you've got sun on your back and soft moss to sleep on. Up here the nest is sticks and the sticks are ice. I wrapped the storm around them and they stopped shivering. That's the whole of it."

"The storm's in the nest?"

"Under them. Coiled like a snake. It won't stay put. Every hour it leaks a little more, and every hour they get colder." The magpie's head drooped. "I can't sit on them and hold the storm at the same time."

Tavi crept closer. The chicks were pressed into a hollow lined with shredded bark, and beneath the bark she could feel it: a faint warmth, like a stone that had sat in the sun. It was fading. Even as she watched, the smallest chick shuddered and went still.

"Is it dead?" Tavi whispered.

"Not yet." The magpie's voice was flat. "But it will be by dark if I don't find a way. And if I let the storm go back to the sky, the valley drinks, and my chicks freeze. So tell me, little fox. Which of us do you save?"

Tavi looked down the crag at the valley. The river was a thread. The orchard leaves were curling. She thought of Maren's badger sett, the damp earth going hard, the old sow panting in the heat. Then she looked at the three chicks again, and the smallest one twitched, a tiny spasm, and she felt something turn over in her chest.

"I don't know," she said honestly.

The magpie laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "There. Now you understand. There's no good answer. There's only what you can live with."

Tavi sat back on her haunches. The wind, what little there was, combed through her fur. She thought about the storm, coiled under the bark, warm and heavy and wanting to rise. She thought about the climb down and the climb up. Her legs were already tired from the first ascent.

"What if," she said slowly, "you don't have to choose?"

***

The magpie's eyes narrowed to black beads. "I'm listening."

"The storm wants to go up. It's a storm. But it can't get there on its own, not while it's tangled in your nest. So I carry it. Not all of it. A thread of it. I take the warmth down the crag and I lay it in the hollow where your chicks are, and you let the rest of the storm go."

"You?" The magpie tilted her head. "You're a fox kit. You'd fall."

"Maybe." Tavi's voice came out steadier than she felt. "But the warmth has to come from somewhere, and the only warm thing up here is the storm. I can carry a little of it in my mouth, in my fur, and I can run fast. Faster than it can leak out."

The magpie was quiet for a long moment. Then she stepped aside, just a little, and pointed with her beak at a place where the nest sticks met the rock. "There. That's where it's pooled. Take it, if you can."

Tavi pressed her nose into the hollow. The warmth hit her like a mouthful of summer. It was thick and golden and it made her fur stand on end. She gathered a little of it, the way you'd gather a pawful of water, and felt it settle against her chest, warm as a beating heart.

"Go," said the magpie. "Go now, before it fades. And fox?" Tavi paused at the crag's edge. "If you drop it, don't come back. I won't have you up here again."

Tavi looked down. The cliff face was cracked and loose, and the wind was already pulling at her. She took a breath, and she climbed down.

The cliff was worse than it had looked from above. Tavi went down tail first, feeling for holds with her back paws while her front paws gripped the warm hollow beneath her chin. Every few lengths the rock turned to something like dry biscuit, and a whole shelf of it would crumble away under her weight.

Above her, the magpie had not gone back to the nest. She perched on the lip of the crag with her head cocked, watching.

"Left," she called. "There's a crack to your left. No, your other left. Foxes."

Tavi found the crack. It was no wider than her nose, but it held. She wedged her paw in and eased herself sideways, and the warmth at her chest shifted, and for one sick moment she thought it would spill.

"Careful," said the magpie, and her voice had lost its mocking edge. "You're losing it. It's leaking out of your fur."

"I know," Tavi said through her teeth. A thread of gold was wisping away from her shoulder, thin as smoke, gone before she could catch it. She pressed her chin down harder and kept moving.

The wind came up the cliff like a hand pushing against her. Halfway down, the rock gave a sound she did not like, a low grinding, and then the whole shelf beneath her back paws slid away and she was hanging by three claws and her own stubbornness, swinging out over the drop.

The valley lay below her, small and pale. The river was a thread. She could see the dry stones where the water used to run, and the orchard with its leaves curling, and it was all so far down.

"Drop and roll!" the magpie shrieked. "If you fall, drop the warmth and roll! Save yourself!"

Tavi looked down at the long fall. Then she looked at the glow pressed to her chest, pulsing, patient, warm as a small life.

"No," she said.

She swung her hind legs up and kicked, hard, and her claws caught a ridge she could not see, and she hauled herself back against the rock, and she held. Her heart was going like a rabbit's. The warmth was still there. Thinner now, but there.

"You're a fool," said the magpie, but she sounded almost proud. "Go on. Down. Fast, before it's all gone."

Tavi went down fast.

***

The nest was in a split in the rock, low on the far side of the crag, and the two chicks lay in it like grey stones. Tavi came over the last ledge with nothing left of the warmth but a faint gold shimmer in her chest fur, and she crouched beside them and let it go.

It poured out of her, soft and slow, and sank into the nest sticks and the grey down and the two small bodies. The smaller chick stirred first. Its beak opened. Then the other one moved its head, slow and stupid and alive, and tucked itself against its sister.

"Oh," said the magpie from above her. Just that.

Tavi lay still for a while, too tired to move. The warmth was gone from her entirely. She was cold and her paws were shaking and she had never felt so emptied.

"You didn't drop it," the magpie said at last.

"No."

"You could have. You nearly fell. You could have dropped it and saved yourself."

"I know."

The magpie hopped down beside her, close, and looked at her chicks. Then she lifted her head to the sky, which was flat and grey and held no cloud at all, and she opened her beak and sang.

It came out of her in a black stream, a boiling, a churn, and it went up and up and spread across the valley like ink in water. Tavi felt the air change. It turned heavy. It smelled of rain.

The first drop hit her nose. Then the sky opened, and the storm came down, and the valley drank it.

Below them, the thread of river began to widen, and the dry stones went under, and the orchard leaves lifted and shook themselves green again.

Tavi got up. Her legs were unsteady but they held her.

"Will they live?" she asked, nodding at the nest.

"They might," said the magpie. "That's more than they had this morning." She looked at Tavi a long moment. "I'll not steal another storm. Not for them, not for anything."

"Good," said Tavi.

"Don't think this makes us friends."

"I don't."

But the magpie flew down with her, through the rain, to the place where the crag met the meadow, and she perched on a fence post and watched Tavi trot home to the den with the rain running off her fur. When Tavi looked back, the bird was still there, a small dark shape against the bright grey sky, keeping watch over the valley she had given back.
