Wren had mapped the same slice of sky for three years, and she knew it the way she knew her own hands. So when the bright one above the miller's roof went out, she noticed before anyone else did. Not a cloud, not a blink—just gone, like a candle pinched between two fingers. She stood on the workshop roof with her pencil poised, waiting for it to come back. It did not.

"You're late for supper," her master called from the yard below. "And you're going to fall off that roof."

"Something's wrong," Wren said, not turning. "The star over the mill—it's missing."

"Stars don't go missing. Come down before you catch a chill." But his voice had a thread of doubt in it, and Wren heard it.

She came down, ate her bread and cheese without tasting it, and went back up before the plates were cleared. This time she brought her oldest map, the one she'd made when she was nine and still drew the constellations as animals. The sky was thinner than it should be. Not empty—thinner, like a page scraped with a knife. She counted the stars she could see and compared them to her map. Eleven missing. All of them from the same corner of the sky, a loose curve that bent like a question mark.

That was the pattern. It wasn't random. Something was taking them in order.

She traced the curve with her finger, and her finger stopped at a gap between two faint stars—a gap that hadn't been there yesterday. As she watched, a star at the end of the curve shivered, dimmed, and fell.

It didn't fall like a meteor, fast and bright. It drifted, slow and heavy, like a lantern lowered on a rope. Wren watched it go down behind the hills to the north, and she was off the roof before she'd decided to move.

***

The hills were closer than they looked from the workshop, but the night made them longer. Wren followed the fall line with her map in one hand and a stub of candle in the other, though she didn't light it. The moon was enough. The star had come down in a hollow between two thorn trees, and it was still glowing when she found it—a soft, pulsing light like a coal that refused to go out.

But it wasn't a coal. It was a creature.

It lay on its side in the grass, no bigger than a cat, with wings folded awkwardly against its back. One wing was torn, the light leaking out of the tear in slow drips. It had a face like a fox's, but older, and its eyes were the deep blue of a winter sky just before dawn.

"You're a star," Wren said, and felt foolish the moment it was out of her mouth.

"I was," the creature said. Its voice was thin and dry, like wind through wheat. "Now I'm just fallen."

Wren knelt in the wet grass. "What happened to you?"

"The Collector." The star tried to lift its head and failed. "He takes us one by one. Puts us in jars. Says he needs the light."

"Needs it for what?"

"For a world that's dying. A dark place at the edge of everything." The star's light flickered. "He was a good man once, I think. Or he thought he was. He's been taking stars for a hundred years, and his world is still dark, and he keeps taking more."

Wren looked at the torn wing, at the light spilling out. "Can you fly?"

"Not now. Not for a while." The star closed its eyes. "You should go home. He'll come for me soon enough, and you don't want to be here when he does."

Wren stayed where she was. The grass was cold and the wind was picking up, and somewhere above her the sky was a little emptier than it had been that morning. She thought about her maps, all those careful lines, and how they were becoming wrong.

"Where does he live?" she asked. "The Collector?"

The star opened one eye. "You don't want to know that."

"I'm a mapmaker," Wren said. "It's my business to know where things are."

The star was quiet for a long moment. Then it lifted one trembling paw and pointed north, past the hills, past the last field, to a place where the dark was thicker than the rest of the night.

"There," it said. "In the between. But you can't walk there, girl. There's no ground."

Wren looked at the gap in the sky where the star had been. Then she looked at the star itself, small and hurt and still glowing.

"I'll find a way," she said. "But first, let me fix that wing."

Wren bound the star’s wing with a strip of her sleeve, using the last of her thread. The star winced but did not pull away. When it was done, it pressed its small nose to her hand and said, “You’ll need more than thread to cross the between.”

“I know,” Wren said. She took out her oldest map, the one she had drawn when she was eight, the one with the crooked mountains and the sea shaped like a boot. It was useless now, but it was still a map. She turned it over and drew a new line on the back. A straight line north, toward the thick dark. “The between is like the sky,” she said. “It has paths. You just have to find them.”

“There are no paths,” the star said. “There’s only the void.”

Wren folded the map and put it in her pocket. “Then I’ll make one.”

She walked north until the fields ran out. The last fence post stood alone in the grass, and beyond it the ground simply stopped. Not a cliff, not a shore. Just a place where the world ended and something else began. The something else was dark, and it moved like water, and it had no bottom. Wren stood at the edge and felt her stomach drop, as if she were already falling.

She thought about turning back. The star was safe now, healing in her satchel. She could go home, redraw her maps, pretend she had never seen the gap in the sky. But the gap would still be there. And another star would go out tomorrow night, and another after that.

“You’re afraid,” said the star, poking its head out of the satchel.

“Yes,” Wren said. She had never admitted that before. “But fear is just a place I haven’t mapped yet.” She stepped off the edge.

For a moment there was nothing under her feet. Then her boots found something solid, a thin black path that shimmered like oil on water. It stretched ahead into the dark, and the dark pressed close on either side, but the path held. Wren let out a breath she hadn’t known she was holding.

“How did you do that?” the star whispered.

“I didn’t,” Wren said. “The between wants to be crossed. It just needs someone to believe it can be.”

She walked for what felt like hours. The path twisted and branched, and each time she chose a fork, she marked it on her map. The void was not empty, she realized. It was full of faint, distant lights, like stars buried deep under water. Some of them flickered and went out as she watched. She did not look too long.

***

The tower rose out of the dark like a finger pointing at nothing. It was made of black stone, and it had no door, only a tall arch that opened onto a spiral stair. Wren climbed until her legs ached. At the top, a single room held a window as wide as the sky, and in front of the window stood a man with a long coat and a lantern that gave no light.

“You’re the Collector,” Wren said.

He turned. His face was tired, and his eyes were kind, which was worse than if they had been cruel. “And you’re a long way from home, mapmaker.”

“You’re stealing the stars.”

“I’m borrowing them.” He gestured to the window. Beyond it, in the distance, a small world hung in the dark, its surface cracked and cold. “That’s my home. It’s dying. The sun went out a hundred years ago, and the people there live in the dark. They’re cold, and they’re scared, and they’ve forgotten what light looks like. So I bring them stars. One at a time. Just enough to keep them warm.”

Wren stared at the dying world. She thought of the gap in her own sky, the empty place where a star had been. “You can’t just take them,” she said. “They’re not yours.”

“No,” the Collector agreed. “But I don’t know what else to do.”

Wren reached into her pocket and pulled out her map. It was crumpled and stained, covered in lines and notes and little drawings of constellations. It was her whole life’s work. “I have a map of every star in our sky,” she said. “Every one I’ve ever seen. If you let me take them home, I’ll give you this. You can use it to find your own way. To build something that doesn’t steal.”

The Collector looked at the map, and then at her. “You would give up your life’s work?”

“It’s not my life’s work if it’s wrong,” Wren said. “And it’s wrong if it doesn’t help anyone.”

He was silent for a long moment. Then he took the map from her hands, gently, as if it were made of glass. “Go home, mapmaker,” he said. “Take your stars with you.”

The Collector held the map as if it were a living thing. His thin fingers traced the careful lines, the tiny drawings of constellations, the notes in Wren's cramped hand. “You've seen all of these?” he asked.

“Every one,” Wren said. “Some of them only once. The ones near the horizon in winter are hardest.”

He nodded slowly. “I had a map once. A long time ago. It burned.”

Wren said nothing. The tower room was cold, and the jars around them hummed with stolen light. She could feel the stars pressing against the glass, restless.

“If I give you this,” she said, “you'll let them go. All of them. Back to where they belong.”

“I will.”

“And you won't take any more.”

He looked at her then, and his eyes were tired. “I don't want to take any more. I never did.”

Wren thought of the empty gap in her sky, the place where a star had been. She thought of her father, who had taught her to chart the stars before she could write. He would understand. He had always said a map was only worth the good it did.

“Then it's yours,” she said. “But you have to promise me something.”

“Name it.”

“When you find your way, when your world is lit again, you draw your own map. And you pass it on.”

The Collector closed his eyes. When he opened them, something had shifted in his face. “I promise.”

He set the map down on a table and turned to the jars. One by one, he lifted the lids. The stars rose like sparks, swirling around the room, and Wren felt their light on her face, warm as summer. They streamed past her, out the high window, into the dark.

“Go home, mapmaker,” the Collector said again. “Take your stars with you.”

Wren ran to the window. The sky beyond was filling with light, star after star finding its place. She climbed onto the sill, and the void below no longer frightened her. She stepped out into the night, and the stars carried her home.

***

Wren woke on the hill behind her village, her cloak damp with dew. The sky was pale with dawn, but she could still see them—the last few stars fading, exactly where they belonged. She lay there for a long moment, counting them.

Then she got up and walked home.

Her father was in the workshop, bent over a half-finished chart. He looked up when she came in, and his face went through several things at once. “You're back,” he said. “You're back, and you're—what happened to your map?”

“I gave it away,” Wren said.

He stared at her. “You gave it away?”

“To someone who needed it more.” She sat down at the table and pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward her. “I'm going to start a new one.”

Her father came to stand beside her. “A new map of the sky?”

“No,” Wren said. “A map of the dark.” She picked up her pen. “The places between the stars. The roads no one has charted. The paths that lead to other worlds.”

He was quiet for a moment. Then he pulled up a chair and sat down next to her. “Show me,” he said.

Wren dipped her pen in ink and drew a single line—a road leading out from their village, curving up into the empty spaces. It was a beginning. The rest would come.

Outside, the last star faded into morning, and the sky was full of light.
