The storm came down the gorge like a wall of water, and Sela heard the rope bridge give way before she saw it. A crack like a branch breaking underfoot, then a long, ugly groan as the old cables tore free of their posts. She stood at the edge of the village common with her hood plastered to her face, watching the planks swing out over the flooded gap and vanish into the white roar below.

On the far bank, a lantern moved. Small and yellow and bobbing, it staggered along the cliff path and stopped at the place where the bridge had been. Sela's chest went tight. That was her father's lantern, the one with the dent in the brass hood from the winter he fell off the mill roof.

"Da!" she shouted, but the rain took her voice and threw it away. The lantern didn't move.

The village lamps were all dark. They had been dark for three nights, and the whole valley knew why: the winter oil was on the far side, in the cart her father had taken across before the weather turned. He had gone to fetch it from the lowland merchant and the bridge had been fine then. Now it wasn't. Now the only way across the gorge was the little rowboat tied up behind the boathouse, the one her father called a floating coffin and refused to take out himself.

Old Cora came panting up the hill with a lamp of her own, shielded under her coat. "Sela! Get inside, girl, you'll be blown off your feet."

"He's stuck over there," Sela said. "The bridge is gone and he's got the oil."

Cora squinted into the rain. "Then he waits till morning. The river's too high to cross."

"He's hurt. Look at the lantern. It's not moving right." Sela was already backing away, toward the boathouse. "The lamps have to burn. You said it yourself, the dark brings the frost in."

"Sela, don't you dare. That boat's older than you are."

But Sela was running, boots slapping through puddles, and the rain swallowed whatever else Cora had to say.

***

The boathouse smelled of wet rope and tar, and the rowboat sat in its cradle like something asleep. Sela dragged it down the slip with her shoulder against the hull, and the water took it with a slap. The gorge below the village was black and shouting. She had never been afraid of the river until now, until she stood at the lip of it with the oars in her hands and the current tearing past like a herd of animals.

"You can do this," she said, and her voice came out thin. "You've rowed the millpond a hundred times."

The millpond was flat and warm and full of lily pads. This was not the millpond.

She pushed off. For three strokes the boat behaved, and then the current caught the bow and swung it downstream, and Sela had to dig in hard with the left oar to bring it back around. Rain stung her eyes. The far cliff loomed, closer than she'd thought, then farther, then closer again as the river played with her.

Halfway across, the boat dropped. Not tipped. Dropped, as if the water beneath had opened a hand. Sela's stomach came up into her throat and she gripped the oars until her knuckles ached, and then the boat rose again with a shove that nearly threw her over the side.

"Da!" she screamed into the rain, and this time the lantern on the far bank turned.

A voice came back, faint and cracked. "Sela? Sela, no! Go back!"

"I'm coming!" She hauled on the oars, one, two, and the boat crawled forward against the current. Her arms burned. The cliff wall slid past, and then she saw him, a dark shape crouched on the rocks with the lantern beside him and his leg twisted under him at an angle that made her look away.

The boat scraped stone. Sela shipped the oars and grabbed the rock with both hands, and the current pulled at her like a hand trying to drag her back into the dark.

"I told you to go back," her father said, but his voice shook, and he reached out and caught her wrist and held on.

The rock shelf was narrow and slick, no bigger than a kitchen table, and it smelled of wet stone and pine. Sela got one knee onto it, then the other, and for a moment she just breathed. Rain ran off her father's hood in a line. His left leg lay turned wrong beneath him, the boot pointing where no boot should point.

"Don't look at it," he said. "Look at me."

She looked at him. His face was grey under the lantern light, and there was a cut above his eyebrow that had stopped bleeding, which meant it had been bleeding a while. "Can you walk?"

"Not tonight." He shifted, and something in his jaw went tight. "The bridge went before I was halfway. I jumped for the shelf and my leg caught the rail post. The oil's behind me."

Behind him, wedged in a crack, sat the jar. It was the big one, glazed brown, tall as her arm, and Sela's heart dropped when she saw the seam running down its side like a vein of ice. A thin shine of oil leaked under it, black and slow.

"It broke," she said.

"It cracked." Her father's hand found her sleeve. "Most of it's still in there. Half a jar gets us to midwinter if we're careful. Sela, listen. You take the jar and go. Leave me the lantern."

"The boat won't take the jar and you both."

"I know what it takes."

She pulled her sleeve free. "Then you'll have to hold it on your lap." And before he could answer she crawled past him to the crack in the rock and put both hands on the jar.

***

The jar was heavier than she expected, and the crack wept oil over her fingers as she hauled it out. She set it down between her boots and wiped her hands on her thighs, thinking.

At home there was a tin of pine resin for mending the boat. Here there was pine everywhere, and a lantern, and rope. She went along the shelf on her hands and knees, scraping resin from the split bark of a wind-bent tree with her thumbnail and the blade of the boat's knife. It came away in amber beads. She warmed them in her palm until they went soft and sticky.

"What are you doing?" her father asked.

"Mending." She pressed the resin into the crack, thumb by thumb, smoothing it down the glaze. The oil stopped weeping. Then she wound the rope around and around the jar, six turns, cinching each one hard as she could, and knotted it off. The jar stood roped like a prisoner, and when she tipped it the seam held.

Her father watched her the whole time. "Your hands are steadier than mine ever were," he said quietly.

"My hands are cold." She crouched by him. "Now you. Can you get your arms around it if I lift you up?"

It took a long time. He swore once, low, when his leg moved, and Sela stopped and let him breathe and then went on. By the end he sat braced against the cliff with the jar on his lap and Sela's belt through the rope loops, holding it against him. The lantern hung from her own belt now.

"The current's worse," he said. "You'll have to go across it, not down it. Aim upstream of the landing."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Sela took up the oars and did not answer, because her mouth had gone dry. She pushed off, and the dark river took them.

***

The current hit the boat sideways and spun it like a leaf. Sela dug in with the left oar, fighting, and the far cliff slid the wrong way past them. Upstream of the landing, he had said. She fixed her eyes on the village lights, small and yellow through the rain, and rowed.

"Easy," her father said. "You're pulling too hard on one side."

"I'm pulling on the side that's winning."

The boat straightened. Water slapped the gunwale and came over, cold as a knife down her back, and the jar rocked against her father's chest and he gripped it and did not make a sound. Behind them the gorge narrowed and the current quickened, and Sela felt it through the oars, a pull that had a direction and a mind.

"There's a whirl on this side," her father said. His voice had gone flat and careful. "Off the point. You'll feel it take the bow."

"Then I'll steer off it."

"Sela." He looked at her across the roped jar. "If it takes us, you let go of the oars and you swim for the shelf. Not me. The jar goes where it goes."

She rowed. The point came up out of the rain, black rock and white water, and the boat's nose turned as if a hand had closed on it, and turned, and kept turning.

The whirl took them in a slow, patient circle. Sela felt it first in her hands: the oars no longer pushed against water but against something that gave and then pulled back, like a rope being paid out. The bow swung left, then wider, and the black rock at the point slid across her view and came round again.

"Hard on the right," her father said. "Both hands now."

She pulled. The right oar bit, the boat straightened for a breath, and then the circle took it back. Water climbed the gunwale and hung there, trembling, deciding.

"Sela." His voice was quiet. "Do what I said."

"No." She dug the left oar in and hauled, and the boat turned, and the turning was the wrong way, faster now, the rock slipping past a second time. The spare oars lay along the bottom boards, lashed to a cleat by a short anchor rope. She saw the rope. She saw the whole thing at once, the way you see a thrown ball in the air before you catch it.

"The anchor line," she said. "If I cut it, the spares go, and we lose the drag."

"That rope's holding us to the current," her father said. Then he understood, and his face changed. "Cut it, then."

Sela pulled her knife from her belt, and the blade was wet and her hands were wet, and she knelt low in the rocking boat and sawed at the rope. It was old and it parted in three strokes. The spare oars slid off the boards, rolled, and went over the side, and the drag went with them. The boat leaped.

"Now row," her father said. "Row out of the circle, not across it."

She rowed with everything she had, and the circle loosened, and the bow found the straight water beyond the point, and the rock fell behind them. Sela did not stop rowing. Rain ran into her mouth and she spat it out and kept rowing, and her father watched her with the cracked jar roped against his chest and said nothing at all.

***

The rain thinned as they came down the gorge, and the village lamps came up out of the grey like a line of small yellow arguments. Sela's arms had gone past hurting into a numb, steady rhythm. She pulled the boat in at the landing stones and sat for a moment, and the boat bumped the stone, and her father reached over and held the edge.

"You cut the rope," he said.

"You told me to."

"I know what I told you." He looked at the jar, then at her. "You did the sum before I did."

People were running down the bank now, the innkeeper and two of the fishers, and they took the jar and helped her father up the steps, and someone put a blanket around Sela's shoulders that she did not remember accepting. The lamplighter's ladder stood against the first post, waiting, as it did every evening.

Sela looked at it. Then she picked up the taper from the hook where it always hung and climbed.

The first lamp was stubborn. The wick was damp and the flame guttered and nearly went, and below her the village had gone quiet, watching. She cupped her hand around it, the way she had watched him do a hundred times, and the flame caught and stood up straight.

"Steady," her father called from the bottom of the ladder. "Don't rush the second one."

The second one took on the first try. So did the third. By the fifth her hands had stopped shaking, and the line of lamps ran down the street ahead of her, yellow and warm, marking the road home for anyone still out in the dark. When she reached the last post at the edge of the water, she lit it and stood there a moment, and the light fell on the gorge, and the gorge was only a river again.

Her father was waiting at the foot of the ladder with the jar in his good arm. "Come down," he said. "You've done enough for one night."

Sela came down.
