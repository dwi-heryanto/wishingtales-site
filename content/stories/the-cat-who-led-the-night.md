The wind came off the sea like a train with no brakes. It hit the harbor and shook the masts, and the rain followed it, hard and cold. Pip was curled under a coil of rope with his mother, listening to the water slap the dock. He was small and gray, with one white paw.

"Stay close," his mother said, her voice low against the roar. "The boats will hold. We just have to wait."

But the rope coil shifted. A gust caught the loose end, and the whole thing came apart like a dropped ball of yarn. Pip tumbled sideways, his claws scrabbling on the wet wood. He heard his mother call his name, but the wind tore the sound away.

***

By morning the storm had gone, but everything else had changed. Pip woke in a tangle of netting near the fish market, alone. The harbor was a mess of broken crates and torn sails. Cats he knew were crouched in doorways, looking up at the sky as if it might do it again.

"Have you seen my mother?" Pip asked a ginger tom.

The tom just stared past him at the water. "Nothing's safe now," he muttered. "The tide took the old shelter."

Pip's heart sank. He walked until his paws ached, calling for her, but the docks were full of strangers. Then a shadow moved under a bench, and an old cat stepped out. She was thin, with a torn ear and eyes like cloudy glass.

"You're lost," she said. It wasn't a question.

Pip nodded, his throat tight.

"We're all lost today," the old cat said. "But I know a safe place. Up past the fish sheds, where the rocks make a cave. The water can't reach it."

Pip's ears lifted. "Can you take us there?"

"No," she said. "My legs are too old. But you can. You're small and quick. Lead the others, and I'll follow."

Pip looked at the frightened cats huddled around them. "Me? I don't know the way."

The old cat touched her nose to his shoulder. "You know how to be scared and keep going. That's all it takes. I'll tell you the turns."

Pip swallowed. Then he stood up straight, his one white paw planted firm on the wet stone. "All right," he said. "Everyone, stay close to me. We're going to the rocks."

Rain hammered the roofs as Pip gathered the cats under the fish shed's dripping eave. A tabby kitten shivered against his flank. "Stay close to me," Pip said, his voice small but steady. "We go one at a time, along the wall." The old cat nodded from the back. "Left at the blue door, then up the slope," she rasped. Pip led them out, belly low, one white paw flashing in the wet dark. Behind him came the tabby, then two grey sisters, then a limping tom, and last the old cat, patient as a shadow. Water rushed in the gutters like a river. At the blue door, Pip stopped. A stack of crates had toppled across the alley, blocking it wall to wall. "It's shut," whispered the tabby. "We can't get through." Pip's heart thumped. He looked back at the line of frightened faces. "Nobody panic," he said. "We'll find another way." The old cat crept forward and sniffed the crates. "There's a gap," she said. "Low down. But it's small." Pip squeezed his eyes shut, then opened them. "Small is what I do best," he said, and he meant it.

***

Pip pressed himself flat and wriggled under the crates, splinters scraping his back. The gap was tight as a fist. For one terrible moment he stuck, and his breath came fast. "You're all right," called the old cat. "Push with your back legs." He did, and popped out the other side into a narrow, hidden run between two sheds. It smelled of salt and old rope. "It's clear!" he called back. "Come through, one by one." The tabby came first, trembling. "I'm scared," she whispered. "So am I," said Pip. "Hold my tail. We go together." The grey sisters followed, then the limping tom, who needed a nudge. Last came the old cat, slow and careful. "You found the way," she said when she stood beside him. "I only followed," said Pip. "No," she said. "You chose to go first." They crept along the run until it opened onto the slope. Above them, the black rocks rose against the sky. The rain was easing to a drizzle. "Nearly there," Pip said, though his legs shook. He looked at the cats behind him, and they looked at him. Then he lifted his one white paw and led them up."

The slope was slick with rain, and the cats climbed in a crooked line. Pip went first, testing each rock with his one white paw. Behind him, the tabby kept her eyes on his tail. The grey sisters pressed close together. The limping tom breathed hard but did not stop.

"Not far now," Pip called back. The old cat nodded but said nothing. Her eyes were on the sea below.

A sound grew behind them, a deep rush like wind through a tunnel. Pip turned. A wave was rising beyond the harbour wall, taller than any he had seen. It curled and broke, and white water charged up the slope.

"Climb!" Pip shouted. "Higher!"

The cats scrambled. The tabby slipped, and Pip grabbed her scruff. The wave hit the rocks below and burst into spray. Cold water soaked them all. The limping tom lost his footing and slid down two lengths.

"My leg!" he cried.

Pip looked up at the safe ledge, then down at the tom. The next wave was already building. "Hold on!" he called, and scrambled down. He pressed his shoulder against the tom's side. "Push with me. Now!"

Together they climbed, one step, then another. The old cat reached down and gripped the tom's neck. They hauled him onto the ledge just as the second wave crashed below.

For a moment, no one spoke. The rain stopped. The sea pulled back, muttering.

"You came down for me," said the tom.

"You would have done the same," said Pip.

***

The ledge was dry and out of the wind. A crack in the rock led into a warm, sandy hollow, and the cats crowded inside. The grey sisters curled together. The tabby licked her wet fur. The limping tom stretched his bad leg with a sigh.

Pip sat at the entrance, looking back at the harbour. Boats bobbed in the grey water. Somewhere down there was his mother.

"She'll find her way," said the old cat, settling beside him. "Cats always do."

"How do you know?" asked Pip.

"Because you found yours." The old cat blinked slowly. "You led them. You went back for the tom. That is courage, little one."

Pip looked at his white paw. It had stopped shaking. "I was scared the whole time," he said.

"So was I," she said. "Courage is not the absence of fear. It is what you do while afraid."

Below, a small shape appeared on the harbour wall. Pip's heart jumped. "Mother!" he called. The shape turned, and a familiar meow floated up.

Pip looked at the cats in the hollow, then at the old cat. "I'll go down and bring her up," he said.

"We'll wait," said the old cat.

Pip stepped out onto the ledge. The wind was gentle now. He lifted his one white paw and began the climb down, and for the first time, he did not feel small.
