Rain came at Highside sideways, the way it did when it meant to stay. Otis stood at the window with the ledger open on the sill and watched the drops hit the glass in long streaks. Seven houses, one chapel, one phone box, and a hill going grey in the last of the light. He uncapped the pen.

"Rain all evening," he wrote. "Wind from the west, strong. No stars."

His grandfather's room was quiet behind him. Bram slept more now than he used to, and when he was awake he asked the same questions twice and laughed at the second asking. Otis kept his worry in his pockets, where it belonged, and wrote the date at the top of the page.

Then he saw it.

Below his line, in a hand that was not his, someone had already written: *How is your grandfather tonight?*

The letters leaned a little, the way old letters do in old books. Otis looked at the pen in his hand. He looked at the door. Nobody had come in. Nobody ever came in.

He went to the next room. Bram was awake, propped on his pillows, watching the rain.

"Grandad," Otis said. "Did you write in the ledger?"

"I can't see well enough to write," Bram said. "What's the weather doing?"

"Rain. Wind from the west."

"Good. Put it down." Bram closed his eyes. "Put it down exactly."

Otis went back to the window. The question sat there, patient, like someone waiting to be polite. He thought about answering it. He thought about not answering it. Outside, the hill held the rain the way it held everything, without complaint.

He capped the pen and left the page open on the sill.

***

In the morning the post came late, which meant Mira had stopped twice on the hill to look at the sky. She leaned her bicycle against the wall and pulled a bundle of letters from her bag.

"Two for the farm, one for the chapel, one for you," she said. "It's a bill."

"Right," said Otis.

"You've got a face on," Mira said. "What's happened?"

He showed her the ledger. She read his line, then the line under it, then she read them both again with her finger under the words.

"That's your grandfather's writing," she said.

"It isn't."

"Then it's yours and you forgot."

"I didn't forget."

Mira crossed her arms. "It's somebody playing a joke. Who's been in the house?"

"Nobody."

"Somebody's been in the house, Otis. Or you wrote it and you're testing me."

"I'm not testing you." He put the ledger on the table between them. "I want to test it."

Mira looked at the book the way she looked at a bill she didn't believe. "Test what?"

"The ledger. Whatever's writing in it."

"There's nothing writing in it."

"Then it's a joke," Otis said. "And a joke can be caught."

Mira was quiet for a moment. Rain dripped off the gutter and hit the step in a slow rhythm. "Fine," she said at last. "But if it's you, I'll know. And I'll say so."

"Right," said Otis.

Mira pulled an envelope from her postbag and held it up. "This came this morning. It's a bill for the chapel. Nobody's opened it."

Otis took it. The flap was sealed tight, still damp from the ride. "So we put it in the ledger and see if it knows what's inside."

"It won't," said Mira.

"Then there's nothing writing in it."

She looked at him for a long moment. "Fine. But if it says something stupid, we stop."

Otis slid the envelope between the pages, near the back where the paper was blank. He closed the book and put his hand flat on the cover. The lamp hummed. Out on the hill, the wind moved through the grass like somebody walking.

"What do we ask?" said Mira.

"Something only the letter knows."

He thought. Then he took up the pen and wrote in the margin, small and neat: *What is the amount on the bill inside this envelope?*

Mira snorted. "You wrote it in the book. Now the book knows."

"No," said Otis. "I wrote the question. I didn't write the amount."

They waited. Rain tapped the window. The clock in the hall ticked twice. Nothing happened.

"Told you," said Mira. She reached for the envelope.

Otis caught her wrist. "Wait."

He opened the ledger to the page where the envelope lay. Under his question, in the same old-fashioned hand, a new line had appeared. It was still wet.

*Four pounds and sixpence. Paid last Tuesday by Mrs. Ellery, who does not wish it known.*

Mira stared. She took the envelope and tore it open. Inside was the bill. She read it, then read it again, and her face went very still.

"Four pounds six," she said. "And a note at the bottom. *With thanks from M. Ellery.*"

Otis sat down slowly on the edge of the chair. The rain kept on. Neither of them said anything for a while.

***

"So it's true," said Mira. Her voice had gone quiet, which was worse than when it was loud. "It knows things."

"Right."

"Then ask it something useful."

Otis turned the ledger to the front, where the entries went back a hundred years. He found a blank page and wrote: *Will my grandfather get better?*

He wrote it fast, before he could think about it. Mira read over his shoulder and did not stop him.

They waited. The lamp flickered once. Then the ink beneath his question shifted, as if the page were breathing, and a line appeared.

*He will sleep well tonight. He will know you in the morning.*

Otis read it three times. "That's not what I asked."

"It's what it said," said Mira.

"It didn't answer."

"It answered something."

He closed the book hard. "I asked if he'd get better. It said he'd sleep well. That's not the same."

Mira sat on the arm of the chair. "Maybe it can't say. Maybe it only knows what's happening, not what's going to."

"Then what's the good of it?"

She didn't have an answer. Outside, the rain had stopped and the hill stood black against a grey sky. Otis could hear his grandfather coughing softly in the next room, the way he did every night around this time.

"I wanted it to say yes," he said.

"I know."

"It didn't even say no."

Mira stood up and put the envelope back in her bag. "It said something true," she said. "I don't like it either. But it's true."

Otis looked at the closed ledger. The cover was worn smooth at the corners from a hundred years of hands. His grandfather's hands, and his grandfather's grandfather's, all writing the same small lines.

"I'll write the entry," he said.

"Now?"

"It's evening."

Mira pulled on her coat. At the door she paused. "Same time tomorrow?"

"Right," said Otis.

Grandfather's door was open a hand's width. A stripe of lamp light lay on the floorboards. Otis pushed it wider with two fingers.

"Bram?" he said. "Are you awake?"

A rustle of sheets. "Otis. Come in, come in. Mind the chair."

The chair was where it always was, and Otis knew it by touch as much as sight. He sat down. His grandfather's face on the pillow was thin, but his eyes were open and bright, the way they got some evenings.

"You've been quiet," said Grandfather. "Two nights now."

"I've been thinking."

"That's the trouble with ledgers," said Grandfather. "They make you think. You write down the weather and then you start wondering who reads it."

Otis looked at his hands. "Someone else is writing in it."

The old man didn't move. For a moment Otis thought he'd gone back to sleep. Then: "Is it any good?"

"It's better than mine."

"Well," said Grandfather, and his voice was almost a chuckle, "that's not hard." He shifted, and the bed creaked. "What does it want?"

"It doesn't want anything. I asked it if you'd get better. It said you'd sleep well."

Grandfather closed his eyes. The lamp made a small circle on the wall, and the dark pressed in around it. Otis waited.

"When I was your age," said Grandfather, "my father kept the ledger. Every night he'd write the line and every night he'd say the same thing. 'The sky doesn't owe us an answer.' I thought he meant it was none of our business. Now I think he meant the answer isn't the point."

"Then what is?"

"Writing it down," said Grandfather. "Being here to write it." His hand moved on the blanket, small and slow, like something surfacing. "The ledger isn't a fortune-teller, Otis. It's a witness. Someone has to look, and someone has to say what they saw. That's all."

"That's not much."

"No," said Grandfather. "It's not." He smiled, and for a moment his face was the face from the photographs on the stairs. "But it's what we've got, and it's more than most."

Otis sat with him until his breathing went even. Then he stood, and put the chair back where it belonged.

***

At the window, the lamp was lit and the ledger was open. Otis took the pen from the jar and uncapped it.

The page for today was blank. He looked at the hill through the glass, black against a sky that was clearing now, one or two stars showing where the cloud had torn. The wind had dropped to nothing. The grass would be wet with it by morning.

He wrote: *Evening. Calm. Rain earlier, stopped by six. Wind light from the west. Hill clear. Grandfather slept well.*

He read it back. It was true. All of it was true.

Underneath, in the old-fashioned hand, a single line appeared. It took a moment — the ink came up slow, like water seeping through paper.

*Good. That is the entry.*

Otis looked at it for a long time. Then he closed the ledger and put the pen back in the jar and turned the lamp down until it clicked off.

Outside, the hill was still there. The stars were out now, more of them, and the village below was dark except for one window, which was Mira's, and it went out while he watched.

He stood a while longer in the dark, not waiting for anything. Just looking.
