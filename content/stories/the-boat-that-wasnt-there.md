Nell finished winding the clockwork and set the brass key back on its hook. The lamp above them hissed, then steadied to a low white glow.

"That's the light on," she said. "Now we can have supper."

Tessa was already at the window with the last of the bread in her hand. "Come and look at this, though."

Out past the pier the sea was a flat grey, the colour of a wet slate. A boat sat on the line where water met sky, and it was not moving the way a boat should.

"Fishing boat coming in late," said Nell, without looking up from her notebook.

"It's not coming in. It's going sideways." Tessa pressed her nose to the glass. "Like a leaf on a puddle."

Nell joined her at the window. The boat was small and dark, and it slid sideways along the horizon. It was not heading for the harbour mouth. It was not heading anywhere.

"Wind's from the west," said Nell. "Everything drifts east tonight. Maybe it's just a slow one."

"Slow ones still have a shape," said Tessa. "Look at it. It's all wrong in the middle."

Below them a door banged. Their father crossed the yard with his coat half on, heading for the pier where Mr Caddy's voice was calling about a rope. He would be out for an hour, maybe more.

The lamp hummed. Nell wrote the time in her notebook and underlined it twice.

***

"We should wake him," said Nell.

"He's on the pier," said Tessa. "He's not asleep. But he's busy, and it's probably nothing."

"Probably isn't a word you can put in a light."

Tessa turned from the window. "Then we signal it. That's what the lamp is for. We flash it three times and it flashes back."

"The signal is for boats that ask," said Nell. "It's not for strange ones that drift."

"So we ask it." Tessa was already at the lamp, her hand on the shutter lever. "One, two, three. Then we count."

Nell stood very still. Her notebook was open at the tally page, and her pencil hovered over it. "If it's a real boat in trouble, they'll answer fast. If they don't answer, we go and get Father."

"Agreed," said Tessa, and pulled the lever.

The shutter clacked. Three short blinks went out across the water, bright and then gone, bright and then gone.

They waited. Nell counted under her breath. Tessa leaned so far forward her breath fogged the glass.

No light came back. The dark boat slid on, sideways and silent, as if the harbour were not there at all.

"Again," said Tessa.

"No," said Nell. "Not yet. Something's wrong with it."

Nell pulled her sister back from the glass. "Look at its shape. It's low, and it's turned wrong. A boat coming in shows its bow. That one's showing its side."

Tessa wiped the fog from the pane with her sleeve. The dark shape was still there, halfway to the harbour mouth, sliding sideways like a leaf on a pond. No mast light. No cabin glow. Nothing.

"It could be a coaster waiting for the tide," Tessa said, but her voice had lost its push.

"Coasters anchor." Nell pressed her pencil to the page and drew a small square where the boat sat. Then she drew another, a finger's width along. "In ten minutes it's moved. Not toward us. Across."

Below them the tide slapped at the rocks and dragged back. Tessa counted on her fingers, then gave up. "So it's drifting. Boats drift. That's a reason to go out, not a reason to stand here."

"We signal again," Nell said. "Slower. Long, long, short. That's what Father does for a boat that's lost its way."

Tessa worked the shutter. The beam reached out and touched the dark shape, held it a moment, let it go. They waited through a long count of twenty. The boat gave nothing back. It did not swing toward the light. It did not slow.

"It's not answering," Tessa said quietly.

"No." Nell closed her notebook. "And it's not trying to. A crew would try."

***

Tessa was at the door before Nell had her coat on. "The rowboat's on the slip. Five minutes out, five back. We look, we see it's an empty dinghy, we come home. Father won't even wake."

"Tessa." Nell stood in her way, small and solid. "If we go out and it's not an empty dinghy, then there's nobody up here at all. The lamp's on a timer and the wick runs dry by one. You know it does."

Tessa's hand stayed on the latch. Wind found the gap under the door and ran along the floor. "Then we're quick."

"There's a book," Nell said. "Father's log. Every boat that came in or went out for years. If something's been lost out there, it'll be written down."

"It's a wreck, Nell. Wrecks don't visit."

"Then the book will say so." Nell was already at the shelf, pulling down a fat canvas-bound book that smelled of tar and old rain. Its spine cracked when she opened it. She ran her finger down the columns: date, boat, master, in, out, remarks.

Tessa came and stood at her shoulder, arms folded, breathing hard.

Page after page of neat ink. Then Nell stopped. Her finger sat on a line from a winter eleven years back. The name was short. The remarks column said: parted her moorings, no crew aboard, lost off the head, believed broken up.

"There," Nell said. "Look at the date. And look at the tide note in the margin."

Tessa leaned in. Her folded arms loosened. "That's tonight's tide," she said. "That's exactly tonight's tide."

A gust shouldered the tower, and the lamp above them shivered, but it held.

Nell traced the line in the log again, as if the ink might rearrange itself into something kinder. "The *Mary Ann*. She broke loose in a westerly gale and was never seen again. Until now."

Tessa stared out at the dark shape on the water. "So it's not a boat. It's a ghost."

"It's a wreck," Nell said. "An old one. The tide's lifting her off the shallows where she's been sitting for eleven years."

"And drifting her straight at the harbour mouth." Tessa's voice had gone flat and serious. "If she hits the pier in this swell, she'll smash the timbers. She could take out the whole north arm."

Nell closed the log. "We have to warn them. Mr Caddy needs to know."

"Warn them how? It's the middle of the night. The foghorn's on the pier and the phone line's down in a westerly."

"The lamp," Nell said. "We can signal. Three short flashes, then a long one. That's what Father does when there's a hazard in the approach."

Tessa was already moving to the shutter lever. "Then we flash. But we can't just leave it at that. Someone has to go down and make sure they understand."

"I'll go," Nell said.

"You're not going alone." Tessa pulled her coat off the hook. "I'll take the rowboat and get to the pier. You stay here and keep signalling. If they don't answer in ten minutes, flash the harbour code for 'all hands'."

Nell nodded. Her hands were steady on the lever. "Be careful. The swell's running high."

"I'm always careful," Tessa said, and was gone down the stairs before Nell could argue.

The lamp flashed its warning into the dark, three short, one long, over and over. Nell counted the seconds between each set. Below, the rowboat pushed off from the slip, a small dark shape rising and falling on the black water.

***

Nell watched until the rowboat reached the pier and Tessa scrambled up the wet stone steps. Then she turned back to the lamp and kept flashing. The wreck was closer now, a black hulk against the grey sea, moving with the slow certainty of the tide.

A light blinked from the pier. Mr Caddy's lantern, swinging in the wind. Then another light, and another. Men were running along the north arm with ropes.

Nell let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding. She kept the signal going, steady as a heartbeat.

It took an hour. The wreck came in slow, and the men caught her with grapples and hawsers, easing her against the pier instead of letting her smash. Nell could see the dark shapes of them working, hear the faint shouts carried on the wind. Then the wreck was still, held fast.

The tower door opened and Tessa came in, dripping and grinning. "We got her. She's tied up tight. Mr Caddy says he's never seen anything like it. He's going to write it in the harbour record himself."

Nell stepped away from the lamp. "You went out in that swell."

"I did." Tessa pulled off her wet boots. "And you stayed and flashed the code like you'd done it a hundred times."

They looked at each other. Nell found she was smiling. "You were right. We couldn't just watch."

Tessa hung her coat by the stove. "You were right about the log. I'd have gone out there rowing at a ghost."

Nell laughed, and the sound was strange and good in the lamp room.

Below, the kettle began to sing. Tessa reached for two mugs. "Tea?"

"Tea," Nell said, and went to check the light. It burned steady and clear, and outside the first grey line of dawn was showing over the sea.
