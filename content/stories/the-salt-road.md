The causeway went under at a quarter past six. Ines knew the time because the church clock on the mainland had just struck. The sound came thin and clear over the water, and she had been counting the strokes when the first stone vanished. One moment the old road lay across the salt flats like a spine of grey knuckles. The next, the spring tide poured over the lower stretches and erased it.

She stood on the village slip with her boots in the mud and watched. Behind her, the boats were hauled up and chained. The gulls were arguing over something rotten near the fish shed.

"You'll wear a hole in the sea, staring at it," said Marta, coming down the slip with a bucket of bait. "It comes in every year, Ines. It goes out again."

"Not this far. Look at the third post. The water's up to the cross-mark."

Marta set the bucket down and looked. She did not say anything for a moment. "That's the moon's doing. Spring tide, neap tide. Your grandmother could have told you."

"My grandmother's dead." Ines said it flatly, because she was thirteen and tired of being told things by people who had known her when she was small. "And the supply boat's meant to come Thursday. It can't come Thursday if there's no road."

At the top of the slip, Old Rui was coiling rope with the slow care of a man who had all day. He had been harbourmaster for forty years, which meant he owned a whistle and a ledger and not much else.

"Boat's not coming Thursday," he said, without looking up. "Wire came through this morning. The mainland company says the channel's silted and the spring tides are too high to risk the run. Next supply boat's in a month. Maybe five weeks."

Ines turned from the water. "A month?"

"Five weeks. Maybe." Rui spat over the edge and watched it go. "We've got flour and salt fish and the goats. We'll manage. We always manage."

The tide crept higher, and the causeway disappeared entirely. The village of San Marco became what it was in every spring. Forty souls, a church, a slip, a schoolroom that doubled as a barn in winter, and no road to anywhere.

Ines stayed until the light went. The water was flat and grey and looked like a floor you could walk on, if you were foolish. Out on the flats, the tide had covered the marker stones. The old salt-gatherers' paths were gone under, and the whole world between her and the mainland was a shining nothing.

***

Her mother was sick by Tuesday. It came on fast. A fever climbed through the night, and a cough sounded like something tearing. By morning Teresa could not lift her head from the pillow. Ines sat with her through the first day and the second, wetting cloths and holding the cup to her lips. She watched the colour go out of her face like dye out of cloth.

The village elder was called Senhora Branca, and she came up the hill with her stick and her bag and her opinions. She smelled of lavender and camphor. She put a hand on Teresa's forehead and held it there a long time.

"It's in the lungs," she said at last, drawing Ines out onto the step. "I've seen it before. Sometimes it burns out in a week. Sometimes it settles, and then—" She shrugged, a small, terrible shrug.

"Then what?" Ines said.

"Then she needs the medicine from the town. The brown bottle. The apothecary on the harbour square keeps it." Branca looked at her sharply. "You're not thinking what I think you're thinking."

"Someone has to go."

"The road's under water."

"The road comes out at low tide. The salt flats are dry at the ebb. My father took the mule across them every autumn for years." Ines's voice was steady, and she was surprised by it. "I know the way. I know the markers."

"You know them in daylight, in good weather, with a man who's crossed a hundred times."

"Then come with me."

Branca laughed, one short breath. "Child, I'm seventy-one and my knees are two bags of gravel. I'd be a burden on the flats and you know it." She was quiet a moment, and the gulls called somewhere below. "There's the boy. Tavo. His father runs the mainland boat, the one that does the harbour runs. He could meet you at the far jetty."

"Tavo's twelve."

"Tavo's twelve and he's been on the water since he could walk. He's not the worst company on a hard road." Branca took her arm. "Ines. If you go, you go at the ebb and you come back before the flood. The tide on these flats doesn't creep. It runs. Do you understand me? It doesn't creep."

"I understand."

They loaded the mule before dawn. His name was Pepe and he was old and grey and patient. He stood in the yard while Ines strapped on the panniers. Dried fish to trade. Two bolts of cloth her mother had woven. A jar of honey. The last of the good rope. Trade goods, to buy the medicine and a little besides.

Tavo arrived with a lantern and a coil of line over his shoulder. His jaw was set in a way that was trying very hard to look older than twelve.

"You don't have to come," Ines told him.

"My father's the one meeting us. If I'm not there he'll wait half the day and then go back, and then where are you?" He scratched Pepe's ear. "Besides. You'll get lost without me. You always get lost."

"I do not."

"You got lost in the schoolroom once."

"That was a cupboard." Ines tightened the last strap and looked at the grey east, where the water was already pulling back off the flats. "Come on, then. Tide's turning."

The salt flats ran out from the village like a held breath. At low tide they were a world of grey mud and shallow channels. The causeway stones showed their backs above the water in a crooked line that only the old people could read. Ines walked ahead of Pepe, testing each stone with her staff before she trusted it. Behind her, Tavo kept up a steady stream of talk. Mostly it was about how he had once seen a fish the size of a door.

"It was in the rock pools by the point," he said. "My father said it was a grouper, but it had a face like a grumpy old man."

"Keep your voice down. Sound carries over the flats."

"Who's going to hear us? The seagulls?"

Pepe's ears flicked. The mule had been steady all morning, but now he slowed, his hooves sinking deeper into the mud. Ines felt the ground change under her staff, a softness that wasn't just wet clay. She stopped and held up her hand.

"Wait."

Tavo bumped into her back. "What?"

The mud ahead rippled, then sagged, as if something underneath had turned over in its sleep. Pepe snorted and tried to back up, but the panniers swung and threw him off balance. His front legs plunged to the knee. Then to the chest. The mule let out a scream that was almost human, and the sound cut across the flats like a blade.

"Hold him!" Ines grabbed the halter and dug her heels into the mud. "Tavo, the rope—get the rope!"

Tavo was already moving, but he was twelve and the rope was heavy and the mule was terrified. Pepe thrashed, and one of the panniers ripped free. The jar of honey spun out and shattered on a stone. The dried fish went sliding into the sinkhole in a slow, awful spill. Ines pulled until her arms burned, but the mule only sank deeper.

"Cut it!" Tavo shouted. "Cut the load!"

She didn't want to. The cloth, the rope, the trade goods—her mother's weaving, weeks of work. But Pepe's eyes were rolling white, and the mud was at his belly now. Ines drew her knife and sawed through the straps. The second pannier fell away, and the mule surged forward so suddenly that she fell flat on her face in the mud. When she pushed herself up, spitting salt, Pepe stood trembling on firmer ground, and half their supplies were gone.

Tavo stared at the wreckage. "The honey," he said quietly. "That was the good honey."

Ines wiped her mouth. "We still have the medicine money. That's what matters." She didn't sound convincing, even to herself. "Come on. We're losing the tide."

***

The fog came in from the sea without warning, the way it always did on the flats. One moment the world was grey and wide. The next, it was a white wall that pressed close enough to touch. Ines could see Pepe's hindquarters and nothing beyond. The marked stones—the old salt-gatherers' cairns—had been visible a minute ago. Now they were gone.

"Stay close," she said. "Hold the rope."

Tavo's hand found the line. "I can't see the next stone."

"Neither can I. We'll walk slow."

They walked. The mud sucked at their boots, and the fog swallowed sound until even Pepe's breathing seemed muffled. Ines counted her steps, trying to remember the rhyme her grandmother had taught her: When the fog eats the stones, trust the tide, not your bones. She had always thought it was just a saying.

After a while, Tavo's voice came out of the white. "Ines."

"What?"

"I don't know where we are."

She stopped. The truth was, neither did she. The last cairn she had seen was behind them, or maybe beside them—the fog made directions feel like a guess. She turned in a slow circle, and the flats looked the same in every direction. Grey mud, white air, no horizon.

"We should go back," Tavo said. His voice was higher than before. "We can find the causeway again. We can try tomorrow."

"There is no tomorrow. The medicine has to reach her today."

"But if we get lost out here—" He stopped, and she heard him swallow. "I'm scared, all right? I'm not pretending I'm not."

Ines looked at him. His lantern was useless in the fog, just a yellow smear, but his face was clear enough. He was twelve. He had come because his father would be waiting, and because he thought she needed him. Both things were true.

She thought about the rhyme. Trust the tide, not your bones. The tide was the one thing that never lied. If she could work out how much time had passed since the turn, she could guess where the water would come from first. The deep channels ran in a known direction. It was a gamble, but turning back was a gamble too.

"We keep going," she said. "But we go by the water, not the stones. Feel the mud. The channel runs east of here. If we walk against the current, we'll hit the last marker before the tide does."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we run." She tugged Pepe's halter. "Stay on the rope. Don't let go."

Tavo was quiet for a moment. Then his hand tightened on the line. "All right," he said. "But if we die, I'm telling my father it was your idea."

The mud changed. It had been pulling at their boots, greedy and soft, but now it pushed back—a slow, cold pressure against the soles. Ines stopped and held up her hand. Tavo bumped into her, then stood still. Behind them, Pepe snorted and stamped.

"Water's coming," Tavo whispered.

"Not here yet. But close." Ines turned her face to the east. The fog was thinning, or maybe her eyes had learned to read it. A darker line ran through the grey, and along that line, a shape. A post. The last marker, tilted but standing, its painted band almost worn away. Fifty paces, maybe sixty. Between them and it, a shallow channel was already filling with silver.

"There," she said. "Run."

They ran. The mule balked at first, then followed in a heavy, splashing gallop. Water sprayed up their legs, cold as needles. The channel was wider than it had looked, and deeper—up to Ines's knees by the time they reached the far bank. She hauled herself onto the marker's stone base and turned to grab Pepe's halter. Tavo scrambled up beside her, chest heaving.

"We made it," he said.

"We made the marker." Ines looked back. The way they had come was a sheet of water now, smooth and grey, swallowing the mud flats in silence. The tide was not a wave. It was a slow, patient rising, and it would not stop. "We have to keep moving. The mainland road is two hundred paces past the last stone."

Pepe tried to climb onto the base, and his load struck the post. The mule staggered, and one of the canvas sacks split open. Cabbages rolled into the water and floated away, pale green and ridiculous.

Tavo started to laugh, then stopped. "The trade goods. If we lose those, we have nothing to pay the boatman."

"We have the medicine money. That's what matters." Ines pulled her knife from her belt. The blade was blunt from cutting rope, but it would do. "Hold him still."

"What are you doing?"

"Making us faster." She sawed at the straps holding the heavier sacks. Barley, dried fish, a bundle of wool. The village had sent them to trade. They would have to manage without. The straps parted one by one, and the sacks slid into the water, where they bobbed and then sank. Pepe shuddered with relief, his back suddenly light.

Tavo stared at the floating wool. "My mother spun that."

"I know." Ines sheathed the knife. "Tell her I'm sorry. Now move."

***

The mainland road was a ridge of gravel that rose out of the flats like a spine. By the time they reached it, the water was at Pepe's belly and the fog had lifted entirely, showing a sky the colour of old pewter. The tide had won the flats, but they were off them.

Tavo walked with his hand on the mule's flank, his face grey with cold. "Your mother's going to be all right," he said. It was not a question.

"She'd better be." Ines pulled the medicine pouch from inside her coat and checked it was dry. The packet of white powder was still wrapped in oilcloth, untouched. "We'll be at the ferry by dark."

The ferry landing was a wooden pier with a bell on a post, and Tavo's father was there, stamping his feet against the cold. He saw them and let out a shout that echoed off the water. Tavo ran the last few paces and was caught up in a hug that lifted him off the ground.

"You're late," his father said, over Tavo's head. "I was about to ring the bell for the mainland search."

"We lost the trade goods," Ines said. "In the channel. I cut them loose."

The ferryman looked at the empty-backed mule, then at her face. "And you brought the medicine?"

"Yes."

"Then the goods don't matter." He set Tavo down. "Get aboard. The crossing takes an hour, and the doctor's house is the last one on the hill."

The ferry was a flat-bottomed boat with an outboard motor that coughed twice before it caught. Ines sat with her arm around the medicine pouch and watched the island shrink behind them. The village was a huddle of grey roofs and one white tower, and the causeway was gone under the tide, as if it had never been. She thought of her mother in the low bed by the window, and of the elder's words: the road home is never the one you walked out on.

Tavo sat beside her, wrapped in his father's coat. "You know," he said, "I was really scared back there."

"So was I."

"You didn't look it."

"I was too busy being scared of the tide." Ines smiled, just a little. "It's easier when you have someone to be scared with."

The ferry pushed into the dark water, and the mainland lights came up one by one. The medicine would work, or it would not. The village would pay its debts, or it would not. But the road would be there tomorrow, and the next day, and the people on both ends of it would keep crossing. That was enough to hold on to. Ines tightened her grip on the pouch and let the motor's drone carry her forward.
