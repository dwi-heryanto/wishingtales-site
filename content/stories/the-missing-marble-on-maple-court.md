Sam had exactly seven marbles, and the blue one with the white swirl was his favorite. He kept it on the windowsill so the morning sun could shine through it. On Tuesday morning, the blue marble was gone.

"It was right here," Sam said. He pressed his nose to the glass. "I put it there after supper."

Priya leaned over the sill beside him. She was the kind of friend who looked before she spoke. "There's a drop of water," she said at last. "Just one. And look, a scratch in the paint."

Sam looked. A thin pale line ran along the wood, like someone had dragged a pin across it. The single drop of water sat beside the scratch, round and clear.

"Marbles don't leak," Sam said.

"No," Priya agreed. "But something was here. Something that got wet and left a mark." She pulled her jacket tighter. "Let's go ask around. Somebody saw something."

They started with Mrs. Okafor next door, who was sweeping her step. "A blue marble?" She shook her head. "I've been up since five, dear. I saw a magpie on your fence, though. Bold as brass, it was."

"A magpie," Sam repeated.

"Big one," said Mrs. Okafor. "Black and white and shiny all over."

***

At the end of the fence, Priya crouched down and gave a little gasp. "Sam. Come here."

Caught on a splinter of wood was a tuft of thread, blue-green and bright. Below it, in the dust, lay a button. It was round and silver and winked in the sun.

"That's not mine," Sam said.

"It's not anybody's," said Priya. "Look at it. It's too shiny to be a real button." She turned it over. "There's a hole in it, and a bit of wire. It's a fake. Like something off a coat from a costume shop."

Sam took it and held it up. "So somebody dropped a fake button and snagged their coat on our fence."

"Somebody," Priya said slowly, "who likes shiny things." She pointed at the thread. "Blue-green. Shiny. Like a magpie's wing."

Sam looked back at his house, at the empty windowsill. "Mrs. Okafor said she saw a magpie."

"Then we know what we're looking for," said Priya. She pocketed the button and the thread. "Come on. You look up, I'll look down. If it was here, it left a trail."

Sam looked up at the fence line. Priya looked down at the ground. They walked slowly, side by side, the way detectives do.

"There," Priya said, and stopped. On the path was another drop of water, small and round, catching the light.

The trail led past the fence, past the leaning bin, past the gate with the squeaky hinge. Sam kept his eyes on the path. Priya kept her eyes on the ground, and her finger kept pointing.

"Here," she said. "Another drop. And here. And here."

The drops marched along the paving stones in a crooked line, like tiny footprints. Sam counted them under his breath. At the end of the path, the line turned under the porch of the yellow house on the corner. The porch sat low, and its boards were old and grey. Underneath was a dark space, cool and quiet, with a smell of dry leaves.

"Mr. Ellery's porch," Sam whispered. "We can't just crawl under there."

"We can look," said Priya. She knelt down and pressed her cheek to the stone. "Sam. Sam, come here."

He knelt beside her. In the hollow under the porch, something glinted. Then something else. Then a whole heap of somethings, catching the light that slipped between the boards. A silver spoon. A bottle cap. A ring of bright keys. A strip of foil, crumpled like a little star.

"It's a nest," Priya breathed. "A treasure nest."

Sam leaned closer. "Look at the top," he said. "That's my marble. That's my blue marble, right on the top."

It sat there, round and blue and perfect, as if it had been placed with care. Beside it, a shard of mirror threw a tiny sunbeam onto the porch beams.

"Somebody's been collecting," said Priya. "For a long time."

"Then somebody's coming back," said Sam. "We should wait."

***

They waited until the sky turned the color of a peach. Sam sat on the garden wall with his knees pulled up. Priya sat beside him, quiet as a cat, and they watched the porch.

The street grew soft and blue. A lamp came on across the road. Somewhere a door closed, and a dog barked once, and then the evening settled down to nothing at all.

"What if it's a person?" Sam said. "What if it's Mr. Ellery, and he's just been picking things up off the ground?"

"Mr. Ellery can't kneel," Priya said. "His knees are bad. He told my mum."

So they waited. Sam's feet went to sleep. He was just about to say they should go home when a shadow dropped out of the sky.

It landed on the porch rail without a sound. Black and white, with a long tail that flicked once and then held still. A magpie. It turned its head this way and that, checking the street, checking the garden wall, and Sam held his breath so hard his chest hurt.

"Don't move," Priya whispered. "Don't even blink."

The magpie hopped down. In its beak was something bright and new, a piece of crumpled sweet wrapper, gold on one side. It slipped under the porch, and for a moment there was only the sound of small claws on wood. Then it came back out, empty-beaked, and shook itself.

Sam let out his breath. "It took my marble," he said. "The bird took my marble."

Priya smiled. "Then it can give it back."

Sam crawled backwards out from under the hedge. His knees were green with moss. "We chase it, then," he said. "We climb up after it."

Priya shook her head slowly. "Magpies fly, Sam. You don't. And that nest is jammed under a porch. If we poke at it, the bird might fly off with everything."

"So what do we do? Just leave my marble down there forever?"

"No." Priya sat back on her heels and looked at the porch, thinking the way she did over a hard puzzle. "My gran says magpies love shiny things more than anything. More than they love keeping them, even."

Sam frowned. "What does that mean?"

"It means it's not really stealing. It's collecting. It saw your marble and thought, that's the shiniest thing I've ever seen."

"It was my best one," Sam said. "The blue one with the white swirl."

Priya pulled a crumpled wrapper from her pocket. It was left over from her lunch, silver foil with a bright red stripe. She smoothed it flat on her knee. "What if we give it something shinier?"

"A wrapper?"

"Look at it in the light." She tilted it, and the foil flashed like a little mirror. "That's shinier than a marble. I bet it can't resist."

Sam looked at the porch, then at the foil. "And if it doesn't trade?"

"Then we'll know magpies are cleverer than my gran thinks," Priya said. "But I don't think we will."

They crept to the porch rail. Sam set the foil down where the magpie had landed, right on the flat wood, and weighed one corner down with a pebble so the wind could not take it. Then they backed away and waited behind the hedge.

***

Next morning Sam was up before the sun had properly warmed the garden. He ran to the porch with his shoes half on.

On the rail sat the silver foil, gone. In its place was a small round thing, blue with a white swirl, sitting exactly in the middle of the wood like a present left on a doorstep.

"Priya!" he shouted. "Priya, it worked!"

She came running with toast in her hand. "Let me see." She turned the marble over in her fingers. "Not a scratch on it."

Sam held it up to the light. "It really did swap."

Above them, on the gutter, a magpie landed with a soft thump. It had the foil in its beak, and it turned its head to look down at them, first with one eye, then the other.

"Thank you," Sam said, feeling a bit silly.

The magpie flicked its tail and dropped the foil into the gutter, then hopped along the edge of the roof.

Priya laughed. "I think that means you're welcome."

Sam put the marble in his pocket and kept his hand around it. "You know what?" he said. "It wasn't stealing, really. It just wanted something bright."

"Everyone does," Priya said.

After that, Sam left a shiny thing on the rail every few days. A bottle cap. A scrap of ribbon. Once, a crumpled sweet wrapper in gold. And sometimes, in the morning, something small and bright would be waiting for him in return, as if the whole street had learned to trade with the sky.
