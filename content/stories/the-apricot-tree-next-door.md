Maya heard the hammer before she saw the fence. Three sharp taps, then a pause, then three more, coming from the empty yard next door.

She set down her bowl of cereal and went to the kitchen window. A man in a yellow cap was kneeling by the property line, driving posts into the grass. Beside him stood a stack of pale boards, still damp from the lumber yard.

"Mom," Maya said. "Someone's building a fence."

Her mother came and looked, coffee in hand. "Oh, that's right. The Hollands sold the place. New family's moving in this week."

Maya pushed open the back door and stepped onto the porch. Morning air smelled of cut grass and sawdust. The apricot tree stood at the corner of their yard, its trunk on Maya's side but its long branches reaching over the boundary like an arm offering something.

Most years the tree gave more apricots than her family could eat. Maya's grandmother had planted it, and every summer the fruit came in heavy and gold. Now a fence was going up right underneath it.

The man in the yellow cap measured a board against the posts and marked it with a pencil. Maya walked closer, staying on her own side of the line.

"Excuse me," she said. "Are you going to cut the tree?"

The man looked up. "Not me. I just build fences. You'd have to ask the folks moving in."

He tapped the board into place. Maya stared at the branches crossing the boundary, and her stomach felt like it did when she forgot her lunch.

The next morning, a moving truck sat in the driveway next door. Two men carried a couch up the steps, and a woman with a clipboard directed them. Then a boy came around the side of the house, carrying a box marked KITCHEN.

He was about Maya's age, with dark hair and one sneaker untied. He set the box on the porch and looked at the apricot tree.

Maya was on her side of the new fence, pretending to weed. She stood up. "Hi," she said.

"Hi." The boy wiped his hands on his shorts. "I'm Sam. We just moved in."

"I'm Maya. I live here. I mean, on that side."

Sam nodded at the fence. "My dad says it's so we know where our yard ends."

"It goes right under the tree," Maya said.

"I know." Sam looked at the branches. "Dad says we should trim the ones hanging over our side. He says they'll drop fruit on the patio."

Maya felt her face go hot. "That tree was here before the fence. My grandmother planted it."

"I didn't say we wanted to cut it down," Sam said quickly. "Just the branches. My dad hates messes."

Maya pictured the tree with one whole side shaved off, like a haircut gone wrong. "The apricots are almost ready," she said. "If he waits a couple weeks, there won't be any fruit left to drop."

Sam chewed his lip. "My dad isn't big on waiting."

"Then maybe," Maya said slowly, "we should pick them first."

Sam looked at the tree, then at her. "Both sides?"

"Both sides," she said.

The next morning, Maya found a plastic bucket on her side of the fence. A note was tucked under the handle. It said, "Meet at the gap after breakfast." At the gap, Sam stood with two more buckets and a long stick with a hook on the end. "Dad's at work till six," he said. "We've got all day." Maya grabbed a bucket. "The lower branches are easy. The high ones are the problem." Sam held up the stick. "I made this from a broom handle. We can hook the branches and shake them." They started on the side closest to Maya's house. Ripe apricots fell into the buckets with soft thumps. "This is faster than I thought," Sam said, wiping juice off his chin. "Don't eat the profits," Maya said, laughing. By lunch, they had filled three buckets. Maya's mother called from the kitchen window, "What are you two up to?" "Just picking apricots," Maya called back. Her mother raised an eyebrow but said nothing. Sam hooked a high branch and shook it hard. A shower of fruit rained down. Maya held her bucket up like a net. "Got most of them!" she shouted. Sam grinned. "We might actually save the tree." Maya looked at the fence between them. For the first time, it didn't seem so tall. "We need to hurry," she said. "Your dad might trim any day." Sam nodded. "Then let's pick everything we can today." They worked until their arms ached. The buckets grew heavy with yellow fruit. Maya felt a warm glow in her chest. This was their tree now, both sides of it.

***

That night, the wind woke Maya. It howled around the house and rattled her window. Rain hammered the roof. She pulled her blanket up and thought of the apricots still on the tree. In the morning, the yard was a mess. Branches lay on the grass, heavy with fruit. Some had fallen on Sam's side of the fence, too. Maya ran outside in her rain boots. Sam was already there, picking up apricots from the wet ground. "The storm got the high branches," he said. "We have to save what we can." Maya grabbed a bucket. "We'll pick them all before they rot." They worked side by side, reaching under the fence and over it. The fallen branches were tangled, but the fruit was mostly unharmed. "This one's still good," Sam said, holding up a bright orange apricot. "They're all good," Maya said. "We just have to be quick." Her mother came out with a basket. "I saw the mess," she said. "Let me help." Sam's father appeared at his back door. He looked at the fallen branches and the buckets of fruit. "I guess the storm did the trimming for us," he said. Maya held her breath. Then he picked up a bucket. "Let's get these inside before the birds find them." Together, they gathered every last apricot. Maya's arms were scratched and her boots were muddy, but she didn't care. The tree was still standing. And so was the fence, but now it had a gap where they passed buckets back and forth.

The kitchen table disappeared under apricots. Maya's mother spread them on newspaper, and Sam's father brought over two more buckets. “I was going to cut those branches back this weekend,” he said. “Now I'm glad I didn't.” Maya's mother wiped her hands on her apron. “The tree's older than both our houses,” she said. “It would be a shame to lose any of it.” Sam's father nodded slowly. “We could share the fruit,” he said. “Half for your family, half for mine.” Maya's mother smiled. “That sounds fair. And maybe we could leave the branches that hang over. They're not hurting anyone.” Sam looked at Maya and grinned. “So we get to keep the tree?” he asked. “We get to keep the tree,” his father said. “And the fence stays, but we'll put a little gate in it. That way you two can visit without climbing.” Maya's chest felt warm and full. She picked up an apricot and bit into it. It was the sweetest one she'd ever tasted.

***

A week later, the new gate stood open between the two yards. Maya and Sam sat on the top rail of the fence, a jar of apricot jam between them. The jam was dark orange and smelled like sunshine. “My dad says we should make jam every year,” Sam said, licking his spoon. “He's never made jam before. He burned the first batch.” Maya laughed. “My mom says the tree has been here for a hundred years. It's seen a lot of fences.” Sam dipped his spoon again. “I'm glad this one has a gate.” Maya looked at the tree, its branches still reaching over both yards. The storm had taken some, but new leaves were already budding. She thought about how scared she'd been when the fence went up. Now it felt like the best thing that could have happened. “You know what?” she said. “I used to think fences were walls. But this one's a door.” Sam nodded and held out the jar. “To the door,” he said. Maya took a spoonful of jam and smiled. “To the door.”
