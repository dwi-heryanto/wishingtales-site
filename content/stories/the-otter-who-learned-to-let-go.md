Pip the otter paddled through the cold, clear water of the river. Morning sun sparkled on the pebbles below. He loved the way the river hummed against his fur.

Then something caught his eye. A stone, smooth as a raindrop, lay wedged between two rocks. It was the color of honey, with a swirl of white through the middle.

"Look at this!" Pip shouted, diving down. He worked his paws around it and pulled. The stone came free with a soft pop.

His mother floated nearby, whiskers twitching. "That's a heavy one, Pip. Leave it be."

"But it's beautiful," Pip said. He hugged the stone to his chest. It was bigger than his head and cold against his belly.

"Beautiful things can weigh you down," she said. "Come along. Breakfast."

Pip didn't follow right away. He pushed the stone up onto the bank and rolled it under a fern. Then he swam after his mother, already planning to come back.

All morning the stone sat in his mind. He thought about its smooth curve and the way the swirl caught the light. By afternoon, he had decided. The stone was his.

He dragged it from the fern and held it tight. It was heavy, but he could manage. With a grunt, he waddled to the water's edge and slipped in.

The stone pulled him under at once. Pip kicked hard and broke the surface, gasping. Water streamed from his whiskers.

"Told you," his mother called from the bank. But she was smiling, just a little.

***

Pip hauled the stone up onto the flat rock where his friends liked to play. Tally the water vole was already there, and little Bim the frog sat on a reed.

"What's that?" Tally asked, eyes wide.

Pip set the stone down with a thump. "My treasure. I found it in the deep part."

Bim hopped closer. "It's so smooth! Can I touch it?"

Pip's paws curled around the stone. "No. It's mine."

"Just one touch?" Tally reached out.

"I said no!" Pip snapped. He pulled the stone against his chest and turned away.

The rock went quiet. Bim's throat stopped puffing. Tally crossed her small arms.

"You never let us share anything anymore," Tally said.

"That's not true," Pip said, but his voice came out small.

Bim slid off the reed and plopped into the water. "I'm going to find my own rock," he said, and swam away.

Tally stayed a moment longer. "It's just a stone, Pip." Then she followed Bim.

Pip sat alone on the flat rock. The stone was warm from the sun, and the white swirl seemed to glow. He told himself he didn't care.

But the river felt quieter without his friends. And when he tried to swim home for dinner, the stone dragged at him the whole way, so he had to walk along the bottom, slow as a beetle.

His mother watched from the den entrance but said nothing. Pip ate his fish in silence.

The stone sat beside his sleeping spot that night. It looked less golden in the dark, more like a plain gray lump. Pip turned his back on it and tried to sleep.

Pip woke to a different river. The air felt thick and heavy, and the sky above the water was a dark, bruised purple. Far off, thunder grumbled like a big animal waking up.

"Pip!" His mother's voice was sharp. "Storm's coming. We need to move to the high den, now."

Pip grabbed his stone. It felt heavier than ever, as if it had soaked up the night. He tucked it under one arm and slid into the water.

The current was already strong, pulling at his fur. His father and mother swam ahead, sleek and quick. His little sister, Lottie, paddled beside him.

"Hurry, Pip!" Lottie called.

Pip tried to kick. The stone dragged him down, down, until his belly scraped the pebbles. He pushed off the bottom, but each stroke was a struggle. The river was rising, brown and churning.

Rain began to fall in fat, cold drops. The water swirled around a fallen branch, and Pip had to fight to get past it. His arms ached. The stone felt like a boulder.

"Pip, let it go!" his mother shouted over the roar.

But Pip couldn't. Not yet. He clutched the stone and kicked harder.

A wave slapped his face. He went under, came up coughing. The far bank looked miles away. His family was already climbing out onto the high rocks.

"Pip!" His father's voice cut through the rain.

Pip's foot caught on a root. The current yanked him sideways. He spun, the stone pulling him under. Water filled his mouth. He kicked wildly, but the weight was too much.

Then a strong paw grabbed his scruff. His father hauled him up, coughing and spluttering, onto a half-submerged log.

"Hold on to me," his father said. "Let the stone go."

Pip looked at the stone in his arms. The white swirl caught the lightning flash. It was beautiful. But it was also heavy, and cold, and it had nearly cost him everything.

"I can't," Pip whispered.

"You can," his father said. "It's just a stone. We're your family."

Pip's paws trembled. The river roared. He looked at his father's steady eyes, then at the stone. Slowly, he opened his arms.

The stone slipped into the water and vanished with barely a splash.

***

Pip's father kept a firm grip on his scruff as they swam the last stretch. Without the stone, Pip's legs moved freely. The water was still wild, but he could kick now, could steer.

They reached the high bank, where his mother and Lottie waited under an overhang of roots.

"You made it," his mother said. Her voice was shaky, but she nuzzled him close.

Lottie pressed against his side. "I thought the river got you."

Pip shivered. "Almost."

His father shook water from his fur. "That stone was too heavy, Pip. You know that now."

Pip nodded. His arms still ached, and his chest felt hollow. He thought of the stone tumbling away into the dark water. It had been the most beautiful thing he'd ever found. But it had also made him slow, and stubborn, and lonely.

"I didn't want to let it go," Pip said quietly.

"I know," his father said. "But sometimes we have to choose what we carry."

The storm raged outside, but the den was warm and dry. Pip curled up between his sister and his mother. The thunder sounded farther away now.

He thought about Bim and Tally. He had been so sure the stone was worth more than sharing. But now, lying safe with his family, he wasn't sure at all.

"Will you sleep?" Lottie whispered.

"I think so," Pip said. And for the first time all night, he did.

Morning came grey and quiet. The rain had stopped, and the river ran high and brown past the den mouth. Pip crept to the edge and looked down. Twigs and leaves spun by in the current, and somewhere beneath that muddy water lay his stone.

"You're not going out there," said his mother, blocking the entrance with her shoulder. "Not yet."

"I just want to see," Pip said.

His father swam up beside him. "The river's still angry. But I'll go with you as far as the shallows, if you promise to stay close."

Pip promised. Together they slipped into the cold water, and the current tugged at his fur like a hundred small hands. Out in the middle, something glinted, pale and round, rolling slowly along the riverbed.

"There it is," Pip breathed.

"It's yours to keep or yours to leave," his father said. "But you can't carry it and swim against this."

Pip looked at the stone. It was still beautiful, still smooth as a moon. Then he looked at his father, steady in the water beside him, and at the den where his mother and Lottie waited.

"I'm leaving it," Pip said. And he let go of the branch he'd been clinging to, and did not reach for the stone. The current rolled it over once, twice, and carried it away downstream, until the water closed over it.

Something in Pip's chest went loose. He turned and swam, and his arms moved easily, and the river seemed to push him home instead of holding him back. His father laughed beside him. "Look at you go!"

When they reached the den, Lottie threw her paws around him. "You're all wet!" she squealed.

"So's the river," Pip said, and everyone laughed. That night he slept without dreaming of stones at all.

***

The next day the sun came out, and the river settled into its old, slow self. Pip walked along the bank with his father, sniffing at roots and pebbles. Near the old willow, something caught his eye: a plain grey stone, small enough to fit in one paw.

He picked it up. It was not beautiful. It was not smooth or pale or round. But it was light, and it was his.

At the bend in the river he found Bim and Tally turning over stones for beetles. They looked up, and Bim's whiskers drooped a little.

"Hello," Pip said. "I brought you something." He held out the grey stone in his open paw. "It's not much. But you can hold it. Both of you."

Tally stared. "You're letting us touch it?"

"It's only a stone," Pip said. "Stones are for sharing."

Bim turned it over in his paws and grinned. "It's a good one. Look, it's got a stripe."

"A stripe!" Tally said. "Let me see."

They passed it back and forth, and Pip watched, and his tail thumped the muddy bank. His father sat on a root above them, pretending not to smile.

"Race you to the willow," Pip said.

"You'll lose," said Bim.

"Maybe," Pip said. "But I'll be fast." And he was, his small stone tucked safe in a hollow, his friends splashing behind him. The river ran on beside them, wide and bright and free.
