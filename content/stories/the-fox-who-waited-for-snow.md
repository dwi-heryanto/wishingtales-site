Soot the fox lived under the roots of a crooked oak, at the edge of a field that ran all the way to the hills. All autumn he had watched the leaves turn from green to gold to brown. Now the branches were bare and the wind had a sharp tooth in it.

He trotted into the den one evening and sat down hard on his tail. "Mother," he said, "when will the snow come?"

His mother was curled in the warm dark, her nose tucked under her brush. "Soon," she said. "Watch the sky."

Soot went out and watched the sky. It was grey and wide and did nothing at all. "It isn't snowing," he reported.

"Not yet," said his mother. "Patience, little one. Snow keeps its own time."

Soot did not like the sound of that. Patience sounded like waiting. Waiting sounded like nothing happening for a very long while.

He tried it anyway. He lay on a flat stone by the field and stared up until his eyes watered. The clouds rolled slowly past. A crow flew over and laughed at him.

"What are you staring at?" asked the crow.

"The sky," said Soot. "I'm waiting for snow."

"Good luck," said the crow, and flapped away.

At dusk his mother came out and sat beside him. Her fur smelled of dry leaves and the den. "What did you learn?" she asked.

Soot thought about it. "That the sky is very big," he said, "and very slow."

His mother licked the top of his head. "That's a start," she said.

***

The next morning Soot decided that watching was too slow. If the snow would not come on its own, he would fetch it.

He ran to the field and gathered leaves into a great heap. He packed them tight with his paws, then climbed the heap and flung them up at the sky. Brown and gold, they tumbled back down on his head.

"Snow!" he shouted at the clouds. "Come down!"

The clouds drifted on, unbothered.

So Soot tried blowing. He stood on the highest stone in the field, filled his chest, and blew at the nearest cloud as hard as he could. His ears flattened. His tail stuck out straight. The cloud slid half a paw sideways and stopped.

"Harder," Soot told himself. He blew again until he was dizzy. The cloud did not turn white. It did not get heavy. It did not do anything.

A rabbit poked her head from a burrow. "What are you doing?" she asked.

"Making it snow," said Soot, out of breath.

"With your mouth?" The rabbit's whiskers twitched. "I've never heard of that."

"It's working," said Soot, though it plainly wasn't.

The rabbit watched a moment longer, then ducked back down. Soot sat in his leaf heap, damp and cross. Above him the sky stayed grey and empty, the way it had been all day, the way it had been all week.

He kicked the leaves apart. Somewhere behind him a twig snapped, and he turned.

"It won't come for blowing," said a voice from the dark of the hedge. "But you're welcome to try again tomorrow."

Soot spun around so fast his paws slid on the wet grass. Out of the hedge stepped an owl, grey as chimney smoke, with a soft white ring around each eye. She settled on a low stump and blinked at him slowly.

"You're the one huffing at the clouds," she said. "I'm Tallow. I've watched a great many winters come, and not one of them was blown in by a fox."

"But it's cold," said Soot. "It's been cold for days. Snow should be here by now."

Tallow tilted her head. "Snow comes on its own time, little one. Not when we stamp our feet, and not when we shout. It comes when the sky has gathered enough quiet."

"Quiet," Soot said, doubtful. "Is that all?"

"That's all." Tallow shuffled her feathers. "You can wait up here with me, if you like. My branch is wide, and I am not going anywhere."

Soot looked back at his leaf heap, then at the empty grey sky. Then he scrambled up the stump and sat close beside her, tail curled around his feet. From up here the field looked very wide and very still.

"How long?" he asked.

"As long as it takes," said Tallow. "Waiting is a thing you can be good at, if you practice."

***

Soot practiced for three nights. He sat on Tallow's stump and watched the sky until his eyes watered. He counted stars. He counted them again. The snow did not come.

On the fourth night he could not sit still. He jumped down and paced a circle in the frost. "It isn't coming," he said. "I've waited and waited. Maybe it forgot us this year."

"It did not forget," said Tallow from her stump. "Sit down, Soot. Let me tell you something."

Soot sat, but his tail kept twitching. "What?"

"The first frost," said Tallow. "I remember it. I was young and foolish, and I thought the cold would never reach my feathers. Then one morning the whole field was white and sparkling, like someone had spilled a bag of sugar in the dark."

Soot's ears came up. "Sugar?"

"Every blade of grass wore it." Tallow's voice went soft. "I hopped to the edge of it and did not dare step in. It looked too fine to touch. Then a sparrow landed right in the middle, and it crunched, and she laughed, and we all went crunching about until the sun came."

Soot pictured it. His twitching tail went still.

"The frost did not hurry for me," said Tallow. "And the snow will not hurry for you. But it will come. That is the promise in every cold night."

Soot looked up at the sky and waited.

The nights grew longer and colder. Soot curled tighter in his den, nose tucked under his tail. One night he woke with a start. The air had a new smell, sharp and clean, like the inside of a stone. He scrambled out and sniffed. Something was different. The wind had stopped muttering and held its breath. "Tallow!" he called, racing to the stump. "Tallow, wake up!" The owl's round face appeared. "What is it, little fox?" "The air," he panted. "It tastes like... like the sky is holding something." Tallow blinked slowly. "Ah," she said. "Yes. I feel it too." She spread her wings and glided down. "Look up, Soot. Watch." They stood together in the dark field. At first nothing happened. Then a single white speck drifted down, slow as a thought. Soot held his breath. Another speck came, then another. "Snow," whispered Tallow. "It's snowing." Soot spun in a circle, trying to catch the flakes on his tongue. One landed on his nose and melted. "It's cold!" he said, laughing. "And soft!" Tallow hooted softly. "The first snow always is." They watched the field turn white, flake by flake, until the whole world was quiet and new. Soot sat down beside Tallow, his tail wrapped around his paws. "You were right," he said. "It came on its own time." Tallow nodded. "Patience is its own kind of warm," she said.

***

Soot did not want to leave, but his mother would be wondering. "Go on," said Tallow. "Tell her." So Soot ran home through the falling snow, his paws leaving small prints. The flakes landed on his fur and stayed. He burst into the den. "Mama! Mama, it's here!" His mother lifted her head. "What's here, little one?" "Snow!" he said, dancing on his toes. "Real snow! And I waited, Mama. I waited and it came." She smiled and nuzzled him. "I knew it would." Soot pressed close, and a single snowflake clung to the tip of his nose. He did not shake it off. He wanted to keep it a moment longer, this small cold wonder that had come when it was ready. "You're different," said his mother. "Quieter." Soot thought about that. "I learned to wait," he said. "It's hard, but it's warm inside." She licked the snowflake away, and he laughed. Outside, the snow kept falling, soft and patient, covering the field where an owl and a fox had watched the sky together.
