Tilda was the first one in the square that morning, because she always was. She liked the quiet before the baker opened his shutters and the pigeons came down to argue over crumbs. But today the quiet felt wrong, like a held breath.

She looked up at the belfry out of habit. The bell should have been there, a dark mouth in the stone. Instead there was only a rope, frayed at the end, hanging down and swaying a little in the wind.

"Where's the bell?" she said, to nobody in particular.

Old Marta came out of the bakery with flour on her sleeves. "What's that, love?"

Tilda pointed. Marta looked up, and her floury hand went still.

"It's gone," Marta said. "The bell's gone up."

"Bells don't go up," said Tilda. "They fall down."

"Not this one," Marta said, and she sounded tired. "Not when it's been forgotten."

Tilda climbed the belfry stairs to see for herself. The rope was still tied to its wheel, but the wheel was empty. She touched the frayed end. It was cold, and it smelled of rain and something else, something like the sky before snow.

Below, people gathered. They looked up and muttered. Nobody rang anything. Nobody sang.

Tilda came back down. "It's really gone," she said.

"Then we'd best go see the rope-winder," said Marta. "She knows about ropes that go where they shouldn't."

***

The rope-winder lived at the end of a lane that smelled of tar and hemp. Her house was full of coils, hanging like sleeping snakes from every beam. She was old and small, and her hands never stopped moving.

"So," she said, without looking up. "The bell has fallen up."

"How did you know?" Tilda asked.

"Because it's the only thing that ever does." The rope-winder set down her work. "A bell rings to remind a village of its own sound. When the village forgets, the bell goes looking for it. It falls up, into the sky, to listen for what's missing."

Tilda frowned. "But we haven't forgotten. We sing at the well every evening."

"Do you?" The rope-winder tilted her head. "Who leads the song?"

Tilda said nothing. She sang, but only under her breath, only when she was sure nobody heard.

"Ah," said the rope-winder, gently. "There it is."

She went to a drawer and took out something small and bright: a brass tuning fork, no longer than a finger.

"Take this," she said. "It hums the note the bell remembers. When you find it, strike it. But the sky is full of things that want a hum of their own. You may have to trade."

Tilda closed her hand around the fork. It was warm, as if it had been waiting.

"How do I get up there?" she asked.

"By the rope, of course," said the rope-winder. "It's still tied to where the bell used to be. Now it's tied to where the bell is."

Tilda looked at the frayed end in her mind, and swallowed. "And if I can't bring it back?"

"Then the sky keeps it," said the rope-winder. "But you'll bring it back. You've got the right kind of quiet in you. Now go, before the cloud closes."

Tilda went.

The rope was rough and cold, and it swayed with every pull. Tilda climbed hand over hand, keeping her eyes up. Below her the roofs of the village grew small and neat, like toys set out on a shelf. Then the cloud swallowed them.

It was not wet, the way she expected. It was soft and grey and full of hush, and it smelled like clean laundry. Tilda kept climbing until her arms ached and her breath came short.

Something bumped her shoulder. Then something else bumped her other shoulder, and a warm, woolly weight pressed against her back.

"Baa," said a voice above her ear.

Tilda looked up into a face like a grey cushion with eyes. A sheep. A whole flock of them, drifting through the cloud on wide, slow hooves that walked on nothing at all. Their wool trailed in long curls and caught on the rope, on her sleeves, on her hair.

"Excuse me," said Tilda. "I'm rather stuck."

The nearest sheep chewed and considered her. "Stuck," it agreed. "You hum. We heard. Hum is ours now. Wool is yours. Fair trade."

More wool wrapped around her arms, soft as a blanket and twice as heavy. Tilda wriggled and got nowhere. The tuning fork sat warm in her pocket.

"If I give you the hum," she said slowly, "will you let me climb?"

The sheep blinked its long grey lashes. "Give hum. We drift to it. You go up. Yes."

Tilda pulled out the fork and struck it once against the rope. The note rang out, small and clear, and every sheep turned its head. The wool around her loosened like untying string. She let the hum go drifting off through the cloud, and the flock went with it, baaing softly, following the sound the way people follow a warm light.

The rope was bare again. Tilda climbed.

***

The cloud thinned and then was gone, and the air turned sharp and thin and cold. Tilda's breath came out in white puffs. Above her was nothing but blue, deep as a well, and in the middle of the blue hung the bell.

It was caught in a net. The net was made of starlight gone stiff with frost, all silver threads with little points of light frozen along them, and it held the bell tight as a fist. The bell hung still, and it looked smaller up here than it ever had down in the square. It looked lost.

Tilda climbed to the last knot of rope and stopped. The rope ended there, frayed and knotted, and the bell swayed just out of reach.

"Hello," she said. "I came to bring you home."

The bell said nothing. Its clapper hung silent inside it. She reached out as far as she could and her fingers brushed one thread of the net, and it was so cold it burned.

She tried to pull. The net held. She tried to pick the frost apart with her fingernails, and the threads hummed and tightened and stung her fingers.

"You can't do it alone," said a small voice.

Tilda nearly let go of the rope. A starling sat on the curve of the bell, tiny and black and speckled with frost. "I've been here since it came up," the bird said. "It won't ring. It won't fall. It just hangs, and the net gets colder."

Tilda looked at the bell, and at the net, and at her own two hands. "I gave away my hum," she said.

"Then you'll have to find another sound," said the starling. "The bell only listens to what's true."

Tilda hung there in the cold, thinking.

"What sound does the bell make?" Tilda asked. The starling tilted its head. "I don't know. I only came up after it stopped." Tilda closed her eyes and thought of home. She thought of the bell on winter evenings, when the whole village walked to the square and the rope swung and the note rolled out over the roofs. It had a rhythm, a slow one, like a heart that was in no hurry. Da dum. Da dum. Da dum. She hummed it first, quietly, and her throat felt strange and small. "Louder," said the starling. So Tilda sang. She sang the rhythm without words, the old three beats and the long rest, and her voice cracked and she kept going anyway. The net shivered. One thread of frozen starlight let go with a sound like a small bell of its own. "Again!" the starling shouted. Tilda sang louder. She sang the way the bell sounded when the snow came down and the whole square went quiet to listen. The bell moved. It swung in the net and the clapper stirred and knocked once against the rim, a low dull knock, and the net cracked all along its seams. Frost fell away in flakes of light. "It's getting heavy," the starling said. "Keep singing!" Tilda's voice was nearly gone. She sang the last of the rhythm, the three beats and the rest, and the bell rang out once, true and low, and the net burst into a thousand cold sparks. The bell dropped. Tilda held the rope with both arms and went down with it through the cloud, and the wool tore away, and the cold air rushed past her ears.

***

The square was full of people when the bell came down. It landed in the belfry with a boom that rattled the shutters, and the rope jerked in Tilda's hands and went still. She was standing on the cobbles, her hair full of frost, and the whole village had come out to stare. "Tilda?" said the rope-winder, pushing through the crowd. "You went up the rope." "I did," she said. Her voice came out scratchy and loud. "It fell up because we forgot our own sound. So I sang it back down." The rope-winder laughed, a surprised, happy laugh. "Then ring it," she said. "You brought it home. You ring it." Tilda took the rope. The evening song was starting, the slow one the village always sang when the light went. She pulled the rope and the bell answered, da dum, da dum, da dum, and the people sang. Usually Tilda mouthed the words and hid behind her mother's coat. This time she stood at the front with the rope in her hand and she sang. Her voice was not beautiful. It was true, and it carried, and the whole square sang with her. The starling landed on the belfry rail and stayed for the whole song. Afterward, Tilda walked home slowly, her hands still cold. She did not whisper goodnight. She said it out loud, and her mother heard her from the kitchen and said it back.
