Tomas the ferryman kept his coins in a leather pouch that hung from a nail by his door. Every night, after the last crossing, he emptied the pouch onto the table and counted them out loud. The coins were old and worn, some with the faces rubbed smooth, and they clicked against the wood like small stones.

He counted in stacks of ten. Six stacks, then four left over. Sixty-four coins. He wrote the number in his ledger with a stub of pencil, then frowned. Yesterday it had been sixty-five.

"One missing," he said to the empty room. The cat on the hearth did not lift its head.

He recounted. Sixty-four. He checked his pockets, the floor, the cracks between the boards. Nothing. He checked the ledger again. The last entry was for a girl named Lira, who had crossed three nights ago. She had given him a coin, and he had written it down. Now the coin was gone from the pouch.

Tomas sat back and stared at the wall. He remembered the girl. She had been young, maybe thirteen, with wet hair and a blue dress that clung to her. She had climbed into the boat without a word and sat at the far end, not looking at him. When they reached the other side, she had pressed a coin into his hand. It had been cold, colder than the river.

"Where are you going?" he had asked.

She had not answered. She had walked into the mist and disappeared.

The next morning, he heard the news. A girl had drowned in the river above the old village. They had pulled her out near the mill. Her name was Lira.

Tomas had crossed many souls in his time. Some paid, some didn't. But the river had its rules, and a toll was a toll. If the coin was missing, the river would want it back.

He went to bed, but he did not sleep well. The river lapped at the pilings all night. In his dreams he heard a girl's voice saying, *I paid. I paid.*

***

In the morning, the river was flat and grey. Tomas untied his boat and rowed upstream, toward the drowned village. The water was high and brown, swollen with rain from the hills. He passed the mill, its wheel still and mossy. Then came the first of the sunken houses. Only the roofs showed above the water, slate tiles glistening.

The old village had been flooded when the dam was built, forty years ago. The people had moved away, but their houses remained, drowned and empty. Sometimes, on quiet nights, Tomas heard bells ringing under the water.

He rowed to the church tower. It leaned slightly, and the cross at its top was green with slime. A window gaped just above the waterline. He shipped his oars and called out.

"Lira! I know you're here. Come out."

For a long moment, nothing. Then a ripple spread from the window, and a girl's head appeared. Her hair was dark and wet, and her face was pale as the underbelly of a fish. She did not blink.

"You're the ferryman," she said. Her voice was soft, with a bubbling edge, as if she spoke through water.

"I am. And you owe me a coin."

She tilted her head. "I paid you."

"You paid me, and then the coin vanished. The river took it. Now it wants another."

Lira climbed out of the window and stood on the water as if it were stone. Her dress dripped but did not spread ripples. "The river took it as payment for my passage," she said. "I gave it to you, and it took it back. That's how it works. A toll for the crossing, and a toll for the staying."

Tomas gripped the gunwale. "What do you mean, staying?"

"I can't leave," she said. "Not until someone carries my name to the living. The river keeps me here."

"That's not my concern. I need my coin."

Lira smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "You'll get your coin, ferryman. Or the river will get something else."

She sank back into the water, and the surface closed over her without a sound. Tomas sat in his boat, his heart knocking against his ribs. The river had never spoken to him before. But he had heard stories. The river always collected its debts.

***

The fog came down while he was still sitting there. The river began to rise. Tomas noticed it first at the mooring post, where the water climbed the rope an inch at a time. Then it lapped at the gunwale. Then it was inside the boat, cold around his boots. He grabbed the oars and pulled, but the current had turned against him. It ran upstream as if the river had somewhere to be.

"Ferryman," said the river.

The voice came from everywhere at once. It came from under the hull, out of the fog, and from the wet wood beneath his hands. Tomas stopped rowing. His arms would not obey him anyway.

"You crossed me without paying," the river said. "You brought the girl's coin to my bank and never gave me my due."

"I gave you your due," Tomas said, and hated how thin his voice sounded. "I've rowed this water thirty years. I've paid every toll."

"Not this one. The coin was mine, and you carried it, and then you came back to take it from a child. That is a debt, ferryman. A debt on the water, and the water remembers."

The boat tilted. Tomas felt the river press against the hull, not like water but like a hand, testing where it might grip. He put his palm flat on the seat and held on.

"What do you want?" he asked.

"A memory," the river said. "One memory, given freely. Something you would grieve to lose. Give it to me and I will let the boat go, and you will row home and count your coins and never think of me again."

Tomas thought of his mother's kitchen, of the smell of bread and the low fire. He thought of the first morning he took the ferry out alone, the way the mist had lifted like a curtain drawn back. He thought of all the small, warm things that made him who he was, and he understood exactly what the river was asking.

"No," he said.

"No?"

"I've watched you take from people all my life. You take and you take and you call it a toll. I won't give you a piece of myself."

A long silence, and then the river laughed. It was not a cruel laugh. That was the worst of it. It was the patient laugh of something that had outlasted every argument ever made against it.

"Then I will take the boat," it said. "And you may swim, ferryman. Or you may stay."

The water rose another foot in a single breath. Tomas felt the current take hold of the hull and begin to turn it toward the deep channel. He grabbed the oars and pulled anyway, hauling against the river with everything he had. The boat swung, slow and heavy, toward the far shore he could no longer see.

***

He rowed until his hands bled. The fog thickened, and the shore that should have been a hundred yards off never came. The current kept turning the boat back toward the middle of the channel no matter how he pulled. Once he heard the drowned church bell somewhere off to his left. Once he heard the girl Lira singing, soft and tuneless. Neither sound belonged to any shore he knew.

Then the water rose up around the boat and held it still.

It was not a wave. It was a coil, a rope of river wound around the hull and over the gunwales, cold and heavy and tight. Tomas dropped the oars and seized the seat with both hands. The coil tightened until the old wood groaned. Below him the river opened like a mouth and showed him the dark bottom of itself. He knew, with a calm that surprised him, that this was where the boat would end.

"You should have given it the memory," said Lira.

She stood on the coil itself, her bare feet resting on the moving water as if it were a doorstep. Her dress was dry now. Her face was not angry. It was tired, and tired was worse.

"I won't bargain with it," Tomas said through his teeth.

"Everyone says that. Then they drown." She crouched, and she looked at him the way a child looks at a broken toy, deciding whether it can be mended. "The river doesn't want your boat, you know. It wants a story. It wants to be owed something. It's been owed nothing for a hundred years."

"Then let it starve."

"It won't." Lira put her hand on the coil, and the coil loosened, just a little. "But I can pay it for you. I've been here so long I'm part of the water now. It would take my debt instead of yours."

Tomas stared at her. "Why would you do that?"

"Because you can carry my name to the living," she said. "My mother still stands at the landing every evening. She has stood there for eleven years. No one will say my name to her, because they think it will hurt her. But I'm not gone. I'm right here."

The coil loosened another inch. The boat rocked, free enough to swim for the far shore if the shore would only appear.

"If I do this," Tomas said slowly, "what does the river take from me?"

Lira looked at him for a long moment. "Whatever it decides is fair," she said. "It will want something. It always does."

Tomas looked at the dark water, and the fog, and the far shore he could not see. He thought of the landing where he had stood every night of his life, counting coins that were never quite enough. Then he nodded, once, and the river closed over his hands.

***

Cold climbed his arms like ivy. It was not the cold of the river — that had always been a wet, moving cold he knew the way he knew his own hands — but a cold that went looking for something. It found the place behind his eyes where his name lived and put its fingers there.

"Say it," Lira said. "Quickly. Say your name before it takes it."

Tomas opened his mouth. He knew the shape of it, the sound of it in his own throat, the way his mother had called it across the yard at suppertime. Then the cold pressed down, and the shape went smooth, like a stone turned over in a river until the lettering wore off.

"I—" He stopped. The water held him gently now, almost kindly. "I don't know it," he said, and the words came out strange and flat. "I knew it a moment ago."

Lira's face went very still. "Oh," she said. "Oh, that's cruel."

The coil slid away from his wrists. The fog thinned as if a hand had brushed it aside, and there, low and dark at the edge of the water, was the far shore. The boat bumped against a stone step. Tomas climbed out because his body remembered the motion, though he could not have said why his legs knew to step up when the world was all one color of dark.

He turned back. Lira stood in the shallows with her hands folded, small and patient, the way she must have stood waiting for a ferry that never came.

"I don't know who I am," he told her. It should have frightened him more than it did. There was a hollow in him now, clean as a scooped gourd, and the river had taken what it wanted and left the gourd.

"You know what you promised," she said. "That's still yours."

"What did I promise?"

"My name," she said. "Lira. Say it to my mother. She stands at the landing every evening, and everyone in the town walks past her and says nothing, and she goes home alone. Eleven years of evenings." The fog was pulling at her edges, softening her like breath on glass. "You have to say it before the water takes me back down."

***

The shore path rose from the landing in steps of worn gray stone. Tomas climbed it without knowing which way to turn at the top, so he followed the warmth. There was a light in a window. A woman stood at the end of the stone quay with a shawl pulled tight. Her hands were folded the way Lira's had been folded, as though waiting were a craft she had practiced until it fit her.

He stopped a few paces off. The river murmured at his back, patient, and he felt it listening.

"You're the ferryman," the woman said without turning. Her voice was dry from years of talking to herself. "You're not the old one. Or you are, and the night has been unkind."

"I don't know," Tomas said honestly. "I've lost something. It's gone smooth."

She turned then, and her face was lined like a riverbank. Something in it made the hollow in him ache — not a memory, only its shape. "You've been out on the water," she said. "Nobody comes off the water looking like that. What did it charge you?"

"My name," he said. "I think."

She was quiet a moment. The lamplight moved on the water. "What did you get for it?" she asked.

He looked at her folded hands, at the shawl, at the eleven years of evenings standing in the wet. "Lira," he said.

The woman made a sound. It was not a cry, quite. It was the noise a door makes when it has been shut a long time and is finally pulled open. She reached out and took hold of his sleeve as though the word itself might blow away. "Say it again."

"Lira," he said. "She's in the river. She isn't gone. She sent me to tell you."

Her grip tightened. "All this time," she whispered. "All this time they said to let her rest." She lifted her face to the water and said the name herself, once, out loud. The river answered with a low turn of sound, and the fog on the far bank lifted like a curtain.

"What do they call you now?" she asked at last, wiping her face with the heel of her hand.

Tomas looked down at his hands, which knew the oars and the wet rope, and at the boat waiting below. "I don't have a name," he said. "I gave it."

The woman studied him. "Then I'll give you one," she said. "You carried her to me. I'll call you Carrier, and you'll answer to it, and anyone who asks will hear how you earned it."

Carrier. It sat in the hollow of him like water finding a new bed and beginning, slowly, to make one. Below, the boat rocked at the landing. The river ran on with its own name, and he went down the stone steps toward it, already knowing how to row.
