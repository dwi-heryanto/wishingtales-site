The fog came up the harbour with the dark, and by suppertime it had swallowed the pier whole. Nell stood at the lamp-room window with the wick trimmed and the brass polished, waiting for the last of the light to drain out of the west.

"Now?" Tessa asked from the table, her mouth full of bread.

"Now," Nell said, and turned the wheel.

The great lens began its slow walk around the room, throwing a long white arm out over the water. Nell watched it go, then come back, then go again. Every night it was the same and every night she held her breath until the third turn, to be sure it had caught.

"It caught," Tessa said. "It always catches. Sit down and eat."

Their father came up the stair with his coat already on. "Net's fouled on the Black Stone," he said. "Caddy's shouting about it. I'll not be long."

"It's thick as soup out there," Tessa said.

"That's why I'll not be long." He looked at the two of them, and Nell knew he was deciding something. "Light's your job now. Both of you. Hourly wind, and mind the oil."

Then the door closed below, and the tower was theirs.

Supper was bread and cold potatoes and a scrape of butter. Tessa talked about a boat she meant to build in the spring, and Nell counted the ticks of the clockwork under her breath and wrote the number in her notebook. Outside, the fog pressed against the glass like a hand.

***

The first knock came just after the lamp had turned. Three slow taps, low down, from the stair.

Nell set her cup on the floor very carefully.

"Pipes," Tessa said. "Old tower like this. Pipes bang."

"Pipes don't knock three times and stop."

"Then it's the wind."

"There isn't any wind."

Tessa opened her mouth, and the knocking came again. Three taps, patient, like a knuckle on wood, and it was higher up the stair than before.

Nell went to the door. She put her hand flat against it and felt the cold of it, and the bolt was across, and the bolt was cold too. It had not been turned. She turned it anyway, hard, until it would not turn further.

"It's bolted," she said.

"It was already bolted."

"Now it's bolted more."

Tessa laughed, one short breath of it, and then stopped laughing. Below them the knocking began again, three taps, and then a pause, and Nell found she was counting the pause the way she counted the clockwork.

"That's four steps up," she said.

"You don't know that."

"I know the stair. It's four steps from the door to the turn."

Tessa looked at the door, then at the lamp turning its slow white arm out into the fog. "Da will be back any minute," she said, too loudly. "Any minute now."

Nell put her back against the door and slid down until she was sitting on the cold stone. "We don't answer it," she said. "We stay here and we keep the light going."

Tessa was already on her feet. "Stay here? Nell, it's coming up the stair. I'm not sitting still while something climbs toward us."

"Then what?" Nell's voice came out smaller than she wanted. "You going to shout at it?"

"Maybe." Tessa's chin went up. "Maybe it'll go away if it knows we're not scared."

"I am scared." Nell said it plainly, because it was true. "And the lamp needs winding. The weight's nearly down. If it stops turning, the light goes out."

Tessa looked at the lamp. Its slow white arm swept the fog, steady, patient, like it had all night.

"Fine," Tessa said at last. "I'll go to the top of the stair. Just the top. I'll call down and tell it to clear off. You stay and wind."

"Don't go past the turn," Nell said.

"I'm not an idiot." Tessa pulled her cardigan tight. "Wind the lamp. I'll be right back."

Nell got up and took the brass key from its hook. Her hands knew the winding without looking. Three turns, then let it settle, then three more. The chain ran up through the ceiling and the weight sank a finger's width with every turn.

Tessa crossed the room and opened the door to the stair. Cold air came in, and the smell of stone and old rope. "Oi!" she called down. "Whoever you are, that's far enough. This is a lighthouse, not a hotel."

The fog took her voice and gave nothing back. Nell kept winding. The clockwork ticked.

Then, from below, a knock. Three taps. A pause. Four taps. A pause. Five.

Tessa stepped back from the doorway. "Nell," she said, very quietly. "It's counting."

"Counting what?"

"The stairs. Three, four, five. It's coming up." Tessa pulled the door shut and put her back to it, just as Nell had done. Her face was pale. "I don't like it. I don't like it at all."

Nell wound the lamp another turn. The knocking had stopped. "You all right?"

"No." Tessa said it fast, like pulling off a plaster. "I'm scared. There. Said it. Happy?"

"Not happy," Nell said. "Just glad you told me."

Nell wound the lamp another turn. The clockwork ticked, steady as a heartbeat. Tessa stayed with her back to the door, arms folded tight.

"Listen," Nell said. "Not to the stair. To the lamp."

Tessa frowned, but she listened. The tick of the clockwork filled the room, patient and even.

"Now wait," Nell said. "Wait for the next knock. Then count."

Silence. Then three taps from below. Nell counted under her breath. "One, two, three." The lamp ticked on. "It came on the tick," she said. "The third tick. I'm sure of it."

"That's nothing," Tessa said. "Everything in this tower ticks."

"Then let's test it." Nell reached for the winding key. "When I turn the lamp, the tick changes. Watch."

She turned the key. The clockwork clicked faster, then slowed. From below came a knock. Three taps, then four, then five, then six. It stopped when the lamp settled.

Tessa's eyes went wide. "It's copying you."

"It's copying the light," Nell said. "Something down there is answering the lamp. Not us."

"So it's not coming up?"

"It's not coming anywhere. It's just . . . answering." Nell let out a breath. "It's been doing it all evening. We just didn't hear it because we were talking."

Tessa slid down the door until she was sitting on the floor. "All that for a knock." She laughed, a bit shakily. "I was ready to fight it."

"You were ready to shout at it," Nell said.

"Same thing."

They sat together, listening. The knocking came and went with the lamp, three taps, four, five, like a slow conversation. Nell found her notebook and wrote the pattern down, one line for each turn of the key. Tessa leaned over to watch.

***

The fog thinned near morning. Nell saw it first, the grey turning to silver in the window. The knocking had stopped an hour before, fading like a tide going out. Tessa had fallen asleep against the door, her head on her knees. Nell had kept the lamp wound and watched the stair.

Now she nudged her sister. "Tessa. It's dawn."

Tessa woke with a start. "Is it still there?"

"Gone. Listen." They listened. Only the tick of the clockwork, and far off, a gull.

Below, the tower door opened and shut. Boots on the stair, slow and heavy. Their father came up, his coat dark with damp, his face tired. He stopped when he saw them sitting together on the floor.

"You two all right?" he said.

"Fine," Tessa said. "We kept it lit."

He looked at the lamp, then at Nell's notebook. "Anything happen?"

Nell hesitated. "Something knocked," she said. "On the stair. It followed the lamp. We think it was the pipes."

Her father nodded slowly. "Old tower. It talks to itself sometimes." He hung his coat on the hook. "Kettle's on. Come down when you're ready."

He went down. Tessa stayed sitting a moment. Then she said, quietly, "Nell. Show me your notebook. The tally."

Nell looked at her. "You've never asked before."

"I'm asking now." Tessa held out her hand. "I want to see how you count things."

Nell opened the notebook and passed it over. Tessa read the neat columns, the small figures, the pattern of the knocking written out line by line. Outside, the sun came up over the harbour and the lamp went out.
