The attic smelled of old rain and mouse nests. Nell climbed the ladder because the roof had leaked since spring, and someone had to find where the water came in. Her brother Toby was supposed to help, but Toby was downstairs asleep, and had been sleeping most afternoons since the fever in March.

She moved boxes aside. Under a quilt stiff with dust, she found a book.

It was not a book anyone would leave in an attic. The cover was black leather, warm to the touch, and the pages were ruled in careful columns. Nell wiped a cobweb away and opened it. Names, dates, and beside each name a word she did not understand: owed, then a number, then a second name she half-recognized from the village.

The entries went back years. She turned pages until the ink grew fresh.

Her hand stopped moving.

TOBY HALE. OWED. ONE. And beside it, in a script finer than the rest: to be collected.

She read it again. Her brother's name was written in her father's handwriting. Or something very like it. The date beside it was the first night of Toby's fever.

"You're not supposed to be up here," said a voice from the ladder.

Nell closed the book on her thumb and turned. A woman stood on the top step, though Nell had not heard her climb. She wore a grey coat that hung too straight, as if there were no wind in it, and she carried a small lamp with a cold blue flame.

"This is our attic," Nell said.

"It is also a ledger room. You found the ledger. That means you can read it, which means you are already part of the account."

The woman stepped down onto the boards. The lamp did not flicker.

"Your brother made a trade," she said. "He asked for one more summer. He got it. The fever broke, didn't it?"

Nell's mouth went dry. Toby had been so ill the doctor had stopped coming. Then one morning he sat up and asked for bread and honey, and everyone had called it a miracle.

"He didn't know what he was signing," Nell said.

"He signed with his name. That is all a name is for." The woman set the lamp on a crate. "The debt comes due at midnight tomorrow. One name. His, or another's given freely."

***

Nell sat on the attic floor with the ledger open on her knees. The grey woman stood by the window, watching the yard below as if it were a page she was reading.

"Explain it plainly," Nell said.

"A name is a handle," the woman said. "People hold you by it. When a name is given, the handle passes to me. The person goes on breathing, but no one holds them anymore. No one remembers to."

"That's not a trade. That's a theft."

"It is a debt." The woman turned. Her eyes were the same blue as the lamp. "Your brother asked for time. Time costs. I do not make the prices, I only keep the book."

Nell thought of Toby downstairs, thin wrists sticking out of his sleeves, laughing at something the cat did. She thought of her mother saying his name in the kitchen, the way she said it now, softly, as if it were still fragile.

"If I give my name," Nell said, "what happens to me?"

"You keep your body. You keep your breath. You lose your place." The woman tilted her head. "You would not be his sister anymore. You would be a girl in the road that no one can quite recall."

"And Toby?"

"Toby keeps his summer. He keeps everything. He simply does not keep you."

Nell's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the ledger. Somewhere below, a door opened and closed. Her mother's voice carried up the stairwell, calling Toby for supper, and the sound of her saying his name was the most ordinary sound in the world.

"Can I pay part of it?" Nell asked. "Something smaller, to start?"

The woman considered her for a long moment. Then she smiled, and it was worse than her silence.

"There is a smaller coin," she said. "A memory. One memory of him, given up. It buys you until midnight. After that, the rest is due."

Nell looked at the page. Toby's name sat there in ink that seemed darker than the rest. She thought of a hundred afternoons, a hundred small things she could not bear to lose.

"All right," she said.

The woman picked up her pen.

The pen was a thin black reed, and when the woman touched it to the page, the ink ran like something alive. Nell felt a pull behind her eyes, gentle at first, then like a hook. She tried to hold on to what she was losing, but she did not know what it was until it was gone.

She blinked. The attic smelled of dust and cold lamp oil. The woman was watching her with mild interest, the way you might watch a kettle come to the boil.

"There," the woman said. "That was not so hard."

Nell looked down at the ledger. Toby's name was still there, but a line had been drawn through something beside it. She tried to remember what she had given up, and found a clean, smooth place where a memory should be, like a missing tooth she could not stop touching.

"What did you take?" she asked.

"Something small. A day at the river, I think. You were both very young. He fell in and you pulled him out by the collar. You were proud of that for a week."

Nell waited to feel it, and felt nothing. That was the worst part. "I don't remember it at all."

"No. You won't." The woman set the pen down and closed the ledger with a soft thump. "You have until midnight. Then the rest is due."

"The rest being what, exactly?"

"His name, or yours. One of you leaves this ledger, and one of you stays in it."

Downstairs, a plate clattered. Nell heard her mother laugh at something, and Toby's voice, high and pleased, saying something back. She stood up too fast and the room tilted.

"How do I pay the rest?" she said. "If I wanted to pay it."

"You come back before the clock strikes twelve and you say the words. I will write them down. It is not painful, after the first one."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the ledger takes what it is owed. It always does. It is very patient and very old, and it has never once been cheated." The woman smiled again, and this time Nell saw that her teeth were small and even and somehow wrong, like a child's drawn from memory. "Go and eat your supper. You are thin."

Nell went down the attic stairs with her heart going like a bird in a box. She did not look back, but she felt the woman watching her all the way to the bottom.

***

At supper, Toby passed her the bread and asked if she wanted to play cards after. Nell said yes. She watched him eat, the way his hair stuck up at the back, the way he chewed with his mouth a little open. She tried to find the place where the river memory had been, and found only a smooth blankness, like a page that had been pressed too hard.

"You're quiet," Toby said.

"Tired." She made herself smile. "I'll beat you at cards."

He grinned. "You never beat me at cards."

After the dishes, Nell went up to her room and sat on the bed with her back against the wall, and thought about the ledger. The woman had said it could not be cheated. But a ledger was only a book, and a book could burn.

The house was quiet by eleven. Nell took the candle from her windowsill and went up the attic stairs, one step at a time, keeping her weight on the edges where the wood did not creak. The attic door was shut, but not locked. She pushed it open.

The woman was not there. The lamp was out. The ledger lay on the table by the window, closed, its leather cover grey with dust. Nell crossed the room and picked it up. It was heavier than it looked, and warm, as if someone had been holding it.

She carried it down to the kitchen, where the stove still had a little heat in it, and she opened the door and pushed the ledger into the coals. For a moment nothing happened. Then the pages curled and blackened, and a thin line of smoke rose up, and Nell felt a fierce, bright relief.

"That was not kind," said the woman from the doorway.

Nell spun around. The woman stood with her hands folded, her grey dress perfectly still, and she did not look angry. She looked disappointed, which was worse.

"It's burning," Nell said. "You can't stop it now."

"Can't I?" The woman walked to the stove and reached in with her bare hand and took the ledger out. The flames went out around her fingers like water closing over a stone. She set the ledger on the table and smoothed the cover, and the burns faded and the pages grew white again. "It has been burned before," she said. "Drowned, too. Once a boy buried it in a field for a year. It always comes back. It is not a book that can be destroyed, only paid."

Nell's hands were shaking. "Then take me. Take my name and leave Toby alone."

The woman tilted her head. "Is that your choice?"

"Yes."

"Say it properly. Full name, and what you give."

Nell looked at the stove, at the ledger, at the dark window where her own face floated like a ghost. Upstairs, Toby was asleep. She could almost hear him breathing.

"Nell Marian Fletcher," she said. "I give my name for my brother's debt."

The woman picked up her pen. "Very well," she said, and opened the ledger to a fresh page. "Let us begin."

The woman wrote Nell's name at the top of the fresh page. The pen scratched, slow and deliberate, and where the ink met the paper it sank in like water into sand. “Nell Marian Fletcher,” the woman read aloud. “You give your name for your brother's debt. Do you understand what that means?”

“I won't be me anymore,” Nell said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. “I know.”

“You will be no one. Not a sister, not a daughter. Your mother will set three plates at supper and wonder why she set the third. Your father will find a ribbon in the yard and not know whose it was.” The woman's pen hovered. “You will remember them. They will not remember you. That is the shape of it.”

Nell thought of Toby's boots by the door, the left one always tipped over. She thought of her mother's hands in the bread dough, flour to the wrist. “Will Toby be all right?”

“He will be free of the debt. That is all I promise.”

“Then do it.”

The woman wrote the last letter of Nell's name with a flourish, and the ink ran backward up the page, unspooling like thread pulled from a hem. Nell felt something go out of her, not painful, not even strange. It was like standing in a doorway and stepping back so someone else could pass. The attic went very quiet. The stove ticked as it cooled. Her hands were still her hands, but they looked borrowed now, pale and unclaimed.

The woman closed the ledger. “It is done. Go down and see.”

Nell went down the attic stairs, and the third step did not creak under her foot, though it had creaked for as long as she could remember. In the kitchen her mother stood at the counter, kneading, and did not look up. Nell said, “Mum?” and her mother's hands paused, then went on with the dough.

“Toby's boots,” Nell said, louder. “The left one's tipped over again.”

Her mother turned her head toward the stove, frowning at nothing. “I could have sworn,” she said to the empty room, and then she shook her head and reached for the flour.

Nell climbed the stairs and stood in Toby's doorway. He was asleep on his back, one arm flung out, mouth open. She wanted to shake him awake. She wanted to say, It's me, it's Nell, remember me. But she knew the words would fall on him like rain on a roof and run off. So she only stood there, and after a while she went to the window and sat on the sill, where the dark was thickest, and watched him breathe.

***

Toby woke to sunlight and the smell of bread and a feeling he could not place, as if he had been dreaming of a room that was no longer there. He sat up and rubbed his face. His left boot was tipped over by the door. He righted it with his heel and went down to breakfast.

“Three plates?” he said, sliding into his chair.

His mother looked at the table. “I don't know why I did that. Habit, I suppose.” She carried one plate back to the shelf, and Toby watched her do it and felt the odd feeling again, like a word on the tip of his tongue.

School was school. He came home in the afternoon and did his sums at the kitchen table, and the house creaked around him in its ordinary way. But at dusk, when the light went blue and the lamp was not yet lit, he found himself humming. It was a tune he did not know he knew, low and simple, the kind of song you sing to a child who will not sleep. He hummed it through twice before he noticed, and then he stopped, because his throat felt tight.

“What's that song?” he asked his mother at supper.

She tilted her head. “I don't know it.”

“You must. I got it from somewhere.”

“Not from me.” She smiled, but she looked at him a moment too long. “Eat your bread.”

That night he could not sleep. He lay in the dark and hummed the tune under his breath, and the humming made the room feel less empty, though he had not known it was empty until then. He thought: if I sing it loud enough, someone will come. It made no sense. He sang it anyway, soft, three times through, and on the third time he heard something in the corner by the window, a small shift of weight, like a cat settling.

“Who's there?” he said.

Nothing answered. But the dark in that corner was not the same as the other dark. It was warmer, somehow, and it stayed until his eyes grew heavy and closed, and in the morning it was gone, and he had slept better than he had in weeks.

He did not tell anyone. He only kept the song, and sang it each night, and each night the corner was a little warmer, and he was a little less alone, and he never once knew why. Neither did Nell, sitting in the shadows with her borrowed hands folded, watching her brother sleep and humming along where he could not hear her.
