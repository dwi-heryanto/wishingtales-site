The boat was three days late. Enna had stopped pretending she wasn't counting. Every evening she walked down to the stone quay and stood where the mooring rope should have been. She watched the grey water fold and unfold itself against the harbor wall. The other fishing boats came in with their lamps lit and their decks slick with catch. Each one that wasn't her father's made her stomach tighten a little more.

On the fourth morning she went into his workshop. It smelled of tar and old rope and the sharp, clean bite of salt. His charts were rolled in the corner. She unrolled them one by one across the bench until she found the one she wanted. It was the northern coast, drawn in his careful hand. The salt road was inked in a thin red line. It ran from their village up past the cliffs to the harbor at Kesk.

"You're not thinking of going up there," said Mera from the doorway. Mera mended nets for half the village and had opinions about the other half.

"He's at Kesk," Enna said. "Or near it. That's where he was going."

"That's where the road goes. The road and the sea aren't the same thing."

Enna rolled the chart tight and tied it with a cord. "I'll be back before the week's out. Tell my mother I've gone to fetch him."

Mera opened her mouth, then closed it. Something in Enna's face must have told her that arguing would only waste the morning. "Take the high road and don't leave it," she said at last. "And take a lamp. The fog comes down fast up there."

Enna packed bread, a flask, a flint, and the lamp. She left before the sun had properly cleared the rooftops. The salt road opened ahead of her, pale and gritty underfoot, running north along the shoulder of the land.

The first hours were easy. Gulls wheeled overhead, and the sea kept her company on her right, blue and restless. By midday the road began to climb. The wind picked up, carrying a cold tang that made her pull her collar close. She passed a milestone worn smooth by weather and traced the letters with her thumb: Kesk, twelve miles.

Twelve miles was nothing, she told herself. Her father had sailed farther than that before breakfast.

***

The fire was a small orange eye in the gathering dark, and Enna smelled it before she saw it: woodsmoke and something savory. She had walked until her legs ached and the road had narrowed to a track between grey boulders. A man sat beside a cart with his back to a rock, turning a skewer over the flames.

He looked up as she came near. He was old and weathered, with a beard the color of wet ash and a coat that had been mended so many times it was more patch than cloth. "Evening," he said. "You're late to be walking."

"I'm going to Kesk," Enna said. "My father's boat is overdue. I'm going to find him."

The man considered this, turning his skewer. "Corr," he said. "I trade salt along this road. Sit down before you fall down. There's enough here for two."

Enna hesitated, then sat. The warmth of the fire reached her face and hands, and she realized how cold she had been. Corr tore a piece of bread from a loaf and handed it to her without ceremony.

"Kesk is another day's walk," he said. "Past the cliff pass. You know the pass?"

"I know it's on the map."

"The map." Corr smiled without much humor. "The map doesn't tell you the fog comes down in the pass like a lid on a pot. Doesn't tell you the path forks and one fork goes over the edge. I've lost two mules up there, and I'm not ashamed to say it."

Enna chewed her bread slowly. "I have a lamp."

"A lamp's good. A lamp and a cool head are better." He poked the fire. "What's your father's boat called?"

"The Grey Finch."

Corr stopped poking. "I saw her," he said. "Two days back, off the northern rocks. She was drifting, sail down. No one at the tiller that I could see."

Enna's bread tasted suddenly like nothing at all. "You're sure?"

"I'm sure of the name. I'm sure of the drifting." His voice was gentle. "I'm sorry to be the one to tell you."

She stared into the flames until her eyes watered. "Then I'm not stopping at Kesk. I'm going out to the rocks."

"In the morning," Corr said firmly. "You'll sleep by this fire, and I'll walk you to the pass at first light. Whatever's out there will still be there tomorrow, and you'll be no use to your father drowned in the dark."

Enna wanted to argue. Instead she pulled her coat around her and lay down on the hard ground, the chart pressed against her chest. The fire crackled, and Corr began to hum something low and tuneless, and the night closed in around them both.

Morning came grey and cold, and Corr was as good as his word. He walked her up the switchbacks toward the pass, carrying a lantern he did not light. "Fog's thick in the notch," he said. "Keep to the stones. There's a line of white ones marking the safe edge." Enna nodded, but her eyes were on the sky. "And if I lose the stones?" "Then you stop," he said. "You stop and you wait for it to lift. The cliff doesn't forgive a hurry." At the top he clasped her shoulder once, then turned back down the road. She watched him go until the mist swallowed him.

The fog began as a thin breath, then thickened until she could see no farther than her own boots. The white stones came slower now, half-buried in wet grass. Enna counted them under her breath to keep her mind off the drop she could feel but not see. Somewhere below, water hissed against rock. Then a light bobbed ahead, warm and yellow, swinging gently as if carried by someone walking. "Hello?" she called. No answer. The lantern moved on, and she followed, because following was easier than standing still.

The stones fell away beneath her feet. One moment she was on the path, the next her boot slid on wet moss and she was clawing at grass that came loose in her fist. The lantern ahead stopped. Enna threw herself flat, and the edge of the cliff bit into her palms. Her satchel strap snapped; the chart slid out and went over, a pale square tumbling into the white nothing. She heard herself cry out, but the fog took the sound and gave nothing back. For a long moment she lay there, breathing dirt, while the lantern light waited just out of reach.

When she pushed herself up, the light was gone. So was the path. She found a stone by touch, then another, and followed them backward until the ground grew solid and the fog began to thin. By the time she stumbled out of the pass, her knees were shaking and her hands were raw. The road dropped toward a harbor she could smell before she saw: salt, tar, and fish. Her father's chart was somewhere on the cliff, and she had nothing left to guide her but the name of a boat and the truth of what Corr had seen.

***

The harbor at Kesk was a crooked bowl of roofs and masts. Every face in it knew something she did not. Enna went from stall to stall asking after the Grey Finch until a woman with a mending net jerked her thumb toward the far pier. "Ask Old Renn," she said. "He brought word of her." Renn was a squat man with a face like weathered rope, coiling line beside a small fishing boat. He looked at her a long time before he spoke. "You're Toller's girl," he said. "You have his chin."

"Where is he?" Enna asked. "Where's the boat?" Renn set down his line. "Two nights back, the Grey Finch came drifting past the northern rocks. Empty. Sail down, tiller loose." He paused. "No sign of your father on her. We searched the near water. Found nothing." Enna's mouth was dry. "Then she's still out there. The boat." "She's out there," Renn agreed. "Wedged in the rocks, likely. No one's been fool enough to go look." "I'm not asking anyone to be a fool," Enna said. "I'm asking for a boat." Renn studied her, then nodded slowly. "I'll take you at first light. But you'll do as I say, girl, or you'll stay on this pier."

They left before dawn, Renn's boat nosing out past the breakwater into a sea like hammered pewter. The northern rocks rose black and jagged, and the swell broke white against them. Enna gripped the gunwale until her fingers ached. "There!" Renn shouted, pointing. In the shadow of the cliff, a dark hull was wedged between two stones, tilted hard to port. The Grey Finch. Renn brought them alongside, and Enna saw the sail furled wrong, the rail splintered. "Da!" she called. The wind took it. Then, faint, from inside the hull, a knock. Three slow beats. Enna's heart hammered back.

"He's alive," Enna breathed. The knock came again, weaker. Renn cut the engine and let the boat drift against the wreck. "Get a line on her," he said, tossing Enna a coil of rope. "I'll hold us steady." She looped the rope through a cleat and pulled it taut, but the gap between the boats was a black, breathing slit of water. Enna kicked off her boots and swung over the side before Renn could stop her. The cold hit like a fist. She surfaced inside the wreck's cockpit, gasping. The deck was slick with weed and the hatch was jammed half open. "Da!" she shouted. A groan answered. She hauled on the hatch until it screeched back. Her father lay wedged against the bulkhead, one leg bent wrong, his face grey. "Enna," he whispered. "You shouldn't—" "Save it," she said, and got her shoulder under his arm.

Renn's voice came over the rail. "The line's fraying! The rocks are grinding her!" Enna dragged her father up the companionway. His bad leg knocked against every step and he bit back a cry each time. At the top, the wreck lurched. The rope holding them to Renn's boat snapped with a sound like a whip. The Grey Finch swung wide, and for a moment Enna saw open water between the hulls. She grabbed the rail and swung her father's arm across to Renn, who caught him by the belt and hauled. "Now you," Renn grunted. Enna jumped as the wreck ground against the cliff. She hit Renn's deck hard, and the boat heeled. Renn gunned the engine. They pulled away as the Grey Finch slid sideways into the cave mouth and stuck fast.

Her father lay on the deck, breathing shallow. Enna knelt beside him. "We've got you," she said. "We've got you." His hand found hers and squeezed.

***

The harbor fire crackled in the iron brazier, and Enna sat close enough to feel it on her face. Her father was propped on a crate, his leg splinted and wrapped, a blanket around his shoulders. Renn had gone to fetch the doctor. The salt road map lay across Enna's knees, its edges soft from her pocket. She held it out to him. "You'll want this back," she said. He looked at it a long moment, then shook his head. "Keep it," he said. "I know that road. I don't need a map to tell me where the rocks are." "But it's yours." "It was," he said. "Now it's yours. You walked it. You earned it." Enna folded the map and put it away. The fire snapped, and somewhere out in the dark the tide went on turning. "I thought you were dead," she said quietly. Her father was silent. Then he said, "So did I. For a while." He shifted, wincing. "The knock—I heard you call. That's what kept me." Enna leaned her head against his shoulder. Above them the night was clear and cold and full of stars, and the harbor lights burned steady on the black water.
