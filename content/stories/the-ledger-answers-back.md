The lamp was on at seven, the way it always was. Otis set the ledger on the sill and opened it to tonight's page. Wind from the east, he wrote. Rain after dark, small and steady. Frost unlikely.

He read it back. The words sat still and honest. Then he turned the pen around and wrote underneath, in his smallest letters: Are you there?

He had thought about it all through supper. A question was not a demand. His grandfather had always said a good question was a door you knocked on politely.

Otis laid the pen down and set a saucer of milk beside the ledger. He put the ink pot on the other side, open, with the lid off. If something came to write, it would have to come close.

"Right," he said to the window. "That's the trap."

He waited until the lamp had warmed his face and the milk had stopped moving. Nothing happened. The hill outside was a black shape with stars above it.

At last he shut the ledger, left the saucer where it was, and went to bed. From his room he could hear his grandfather breathing slow and steady through the wall.

***

Morning came grey and wet. Otis was up before the kettle.

The saucer of milk was full to the rim. Not a ripple. The ink pot was dry, the lid still off, the surface flat and dark as a coin.

He opened the ledger with both hands. His own question was there. And under it, in the same old-fashioned hand from before, was a line.

What walks up the hill and never reaches the top?

Otis read it three times. "That's not an answer," he said. "That's a riddle."

He looked at the ink pot. Dry. He looked at the milk. Untouched. Whatever wrote in the ledger had not needed his pen and had not wanted his milk.

Outside, the rain had stopped and the hill stood up green and patient. Otis got the feeling, the way you do when someone is waiting for you to catch up.

He put the kettle on for his grandfather and cut bread. All the while the riddle sat in his head like a stone in a shoe.

"I'll work it out," he told the page. "Give me a minute."

Mira came up the lane at eight, her bicycle ticking like a clock that had lost its nerve. She had the post in a canvas bag and a face that already had opinions in it.

"Two letters," she said, "and one of them is for your grandfather from the council, and it has a window in it, which never means anything good."

She leaned the bike on the wall and saw the saucer on the sill and the open ledger on the table. Otis had not meant to leave it open. He had meant to be clever and had run out of clever by seven.

"You're writing in it again," she said. "I thought you were just keeping it. Like a list. Like a long list of weather."

"I am keeping it," said Otis. "It's keeping me back."

Mira picked up the ledger with two fingers, the way you lift a sock you do not trust. She read the evening entry, and the question, and the line beneath it.

"That's your handwriting," she said.

"It is not."

"It's your grandfather's, then. He woke up and did it and forgot."

Otis turned the pages back to last week, where his grandfather's hand still was: shaky and wide, the letters leaning on each other. Then he pointed at the new line. It was small and straight and certain, each letter standing on its own like a soldier.

"Look," he said. "Bram's letters hold hands. These ones stand apart."

Mira looked for a long time. Her jaw did the thing it did when she was checking a sum.

"It could be a trick," she said. "People do tricks."

"What people?"

"Clever ones."

Otis said nothing. He put the kettle on, because when someone argues, it helps to have a cup in your hand.

***

The experiment was Mira's idea, which she pretended was Otis's so that it would be allowed.

"Write the same question in two places," she said. "If it's a trick, the trick will answer the same way both times. If it's not a trick, it might not."

Otis thought about this. "That's the wrong way round," he said. "A trick would answer the same. A real thing might get bored and say something different."

"Then we learn something either way," said Mira. "That's what an experiment is."

So they did it properly. On the left page Otis wrote in pencil, small: What walks up the hill and never reaches the top? On a loose sheet, which he tucked under the kettle, he wrote the same words in ink. They put the saucer back on the sill, though Mira said it was a waste of good milk. Then they sat on the step and waited and did not say much.

The afternoon went long. Mira counted the post van. Otis counted the hens. The hill stayed green and the sky stayed grey and nothing happened for so long that Mira began to say "I told you" without finishing it.

Then Otis went in for the kettle sheet. The ink had dried.

Under his question, in that straight small hand, was a line: The one that keeps the ledger is not the hill.

He showed Mira. She read it twice.

"That's an answer," she said, slowly. "It's not a riddle. It's an answer."

"It's a riddle that's an answer," said Otis. "It's showing off."

Mira looked at the loose sheet, then at the open ledger, then at the window.

"Write it again," she said. "Somewhere else."

They wrote it again on the back of a feed sack, in pencil, and left it on the chapel step where the wind could not reach it. Then they hid behind the wall and watched. Nothing came. The sack flapped. Mira said it was a stupid place to put a question.

At dusk Otis fetched the sack. Under his pencil, in the straight small hand, was one line: The weather writes before I read. Otis read it aloud twice.

"So it's not a riddle," said Mira. "It's a note from the weather. Saying it already happened."

"It means the ledger doesn't make the weather," said Otis. "It only writes down what the weather did. I can't ask it to change anything."

Mira folded her arms. "That's a lot to get from one line."

"It's all it ever does," said Otis. "It answers exactly what I ask. Nothing more." He looked at the hill. "I asked it to stop the frost. It can't. It just writes the frost down."

"So it's a notebook," said Mira. "A very old notebook with good handwriting."

Otis nodded. "A notebook that answers." He put the sack in his pocket. "But it's still the weather talking. That's something."

***

That evening Otis wrote the entry by the window. Wind from the east, he wrote. Cloud all day, no rain. Frost tonight, hard. He stopped. The pen hovered.

Then he wrote: I asked for no frost. I understand now. You write what happens. I will write it too.

He set the pen down. Mira was on the step, her bicycle propped against the wall.

"You're not going to ask it anything else?" she said.

"Not tonight," said Otis. "Tomorrow I might ask it what it's like up there. On the hill."

Mira snorted. "You'll ask it the weather. You always ask it the weather."

"Right," said Otis. He blew out the lamp. The window went dark. Outside, the hill was a black shape against a grey sky, and the first frost was already shining on the wall.
