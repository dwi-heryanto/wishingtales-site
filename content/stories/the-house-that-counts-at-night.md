The moving truck rolled away, and Lila stood alone in the front yard. The house was tall and narrow, with a crooked porch and windows like sleepy eyes. Boxes waited inside. Her mom was already unpacking the kitchen, singing off-key. Lila dragged her own box up the stairs. Her room had a slanted ceiling and a window that looked out at a pine tree. She put her lamp on the nightstand and her books on the shelf.

That night, the floorboards creaked under her feet. The house settled with soft clicks and long sighs. Lila lay in bed, listening. Then she heard something else. A whisper, thin and steady. A voice counting.

"One... two... three..."

Lila sat up. "Mom?" she called.

No answer. The counting went on. "Four... five... six..." It came from everywhere and nowhere. Lila pulled the blanket to her chin. "Seven... eight... nine..."

The voice was not scary exactly. It sounded tired. It sounded like someone trying to remember something. Lila lay back down and listened until she fell asleep.

In the morning, sunlight filled the room. The counting was gone. Lila ate toast and asked her mom, "Did you hear anything last night?"

"Just the house settling," her mom said. "Old houses talk. You'll get used to it."

Lila wasn't sure she wanted to get used to it. But she also wasn't sure she didn't.

***

The next day, Lila explored the backyard. A boy was sitting on the fence, kicking his heels. He had a red cap and a pocket full of string.

"You're the new kid," he said. "I'm Sam. I live two doors down."

"Lila," she said. "Do you know anything about this house?"

Sam grinned. "Everyone knows about that house. It counts things."

"It counts things?"

"Every night," Sam said. "My grandpa told me. It counts the spoons, the stairs, the windows. Once it counted all the leaves on the oak tree out back. Took until morning."

Lila thought about the whisper. "Last night it counted to nine and stopped."

Sam hopped off the fence. "That's because it's missing something. It counts what it has, and then it tries to count what's gone."

"What's gone?"

"Nobody knows," Sam said. "But my grandpa said the house used to belong to a clockmaker. He made a clock for every room. When he left, he took one clock with him. The house has been counting ever since, trying to find it."

Lila looked up at the attic window. It was dark and dusty. "Have you ever been up there?"

"No," Sam said. "The stairs are locked. But I know where the key is. My grandpa showed me."

He pulled a small brass key from his pocket. "Want to find out what the house is counting for?"

Sam led the way up the back stairs. They creaked under his sneakers. Lila followed, one hand on the wall. The wall was papered with little green vines. In the dark, the vines looked like they were growing.

"Do you hear it?" Sam whispered.

Lila stopped. Far below, deep in the house, something was counting. Eleven. Twelve. A pause. Thirteen.

"It's counting the stairs," Sam said. "We're on the stairs. It's counting us."

"That's not creepy at all," Lila said.

Sam laughed, but he laughed quietly. They climbed past the second floor. The stairs turned narrow. At the top was a door. It was painted blue, but the paint was old and bubbly. A brass keyhole sat under the knob.

Sam tried the handle. It didn't move. "Locked," he said.

"Then use the key."

He pulled the brass key from his pocket. It was small and warm. He slid it into the keyhole. It fit perfectly. When he turned it, the lock clicked like a tongue.

The counting stopped.

Lila held her breath. The whole house went still. Even the floorboards under her feet seemed to wait.

"It knows," Sam whispered. "It knows we're here."

"Then let's not keep it waiting," Lila said. She pushed the door open. It swung without a squeak.

***

The attic was full of clocks. Clocks on shelves, clocks on tables, clocks hanging from the beams. Grandfather clocks stood in the corners like tall, patient guards. Every clock was different. One was shaped like a teacup. One had a face painted with stars. One was no bigger than a walnut.

Lila stepped inside. The floor was soft with dust. The air smelled like old wood and oil.

"Look at them all," Sam breathed. "The clockmaker made every one."

Most of the clocks were quiet. But a few ticked. Tick, tick, tick. It sounded like tiny footsteps.

"Why are only some of them going?" Lila asked.

Sam pointed. "Because some of them are wound. And some aren't."

Lila walked along a shelf. She touched a clock with a cracked face. It was cold. Beside it sat a round clock with a brass rim. Its hands were frozen at three minutes past midnight. The glass on its face was smudged with fingerprints.

"This one's broken," she said.

Sam came over. "That's the one," he said. "That's the clock the house is missing."

"How do you know?"

"Because it's the only one that matches the one in the picture." He pointed at a small painted portrait on the wall. An old man with white hair held a round clock with a brass rim. The same clock sat in Lila's hands.

"So the clockmaker left it behind," Lila said.

"No," Sam said. "He took it with him. But it came back. And now the house can't count it because it's broken."

Lila looked at the clock. A tiny keyhole sat at the bottom of its face. She reached into her pocket. Her fingers found something small and cold. She pulled it out. A little brass key. It looked exactly like the one Sam had used on the door.

"Where did you get that?" Sam asked.

"It was in my pocket," Lila said. "I found it in the garden this morning."

Lila turned the key over in her palm. The brass was warm, as if it had been waiting in her pocket for years. "It fits," she whispered. She knelt in the dust and slid the key into the tiny hole. It stuck. She wiggled it. Nothing. Sam crouched beside her, his breath loud in the quiet. "Try turning it the other way," he said. She did. The key clicked, and something inside the clock sighed. The ticking started, slow and steady. Tick. Tock. The counting in the walls changed. It wasn't counting the clocks anymore. It was counting along with this one. One. Two. Three. The numbers came from everywhere, soft as moth wings. Lila stood up. The other clocks ticked in time now. Each one matched the little clock in her hands. "You did it," Sam said. "The house found its missing piece." Lila looked at the painted portrait. The old clockmaker seemed to smile. "He left it for someone to find," she said. "For you," Sam said. "You found it. You fixed it." Downstairs, the creaking stopped. The counting faded into a hum, then into silence. Just the clocks. Just the ticking. Lila tucked the key back into her pocket. It felt lighter now, like a secret she could keep.

***

They went down the attic stairs, and the house felt different. The hallway was still dark, but it wasn't heavy. It was soft, like a blanket. Lila touched the wall. The wood was warm. "Listen," Sam said. She listened. No counting. No creaks. Just the clock in the kitchen, ticking like a heartbeat. "It's quiet," she said. "The house is quiet." Sam nodded. "It's been counting for a hundred years," he said. "Now it can rest." Lila walked to her room. Her bed was still there, and her box of books, and the window with the moonlight falling through. She sat on the edge of the mattress. The floor didn't groan. She lay down. The house didn't shift. For the first time since she moved in, she wasn't waiting for a sound. She wasn't counting. She just breathed. In the morning, her mother would ask how she slept. Lila would say fine. She would mean it. The little brass key sat on her nightstand, catching the moon. Somewhere below, the clock ticked on. The house had what it needed. And so did she.
