The dare came from Marla Finch, who was fifteen and had a laugh that made you want to be on the right side of it. She stood on the cracked sidewalk of Pell Street with her hands on her hips and nodded at the house behind her. "An hour," she said. "One hour inside forty-four. That's all. Then you come out and we believe you."

Dessa looked at the house. It was three stories of grey clapboard, windows boarded over with plywood that had gone soft and dark at the edges. The front door was still on its hinges, which surprised her. Someone had nailed a strip of yellow caution tape across it, but the tape had torn free on one side and hung like a dead vine.

"What happened to the last owner?" Dessa asked.

"Nobody knows," said Marla, and she said it the way you say a thing you've practiced. "Went in one night. Never came out. That's the story."

"There's no story," said a boy behind her, Tuck, who was small and nervous and always chewing his sleeve. "The guy just moved. My mom said he moved to his sister's."

Marla didn't turn around. "Then it's an easy hour, isn't it, Dessa? Go on. Unless you're scared."

The word sat there. Dessa was thirteen and scared of very little, or at least she had told herself that enough times that it had mostly stuck. She looked at the house and the house looked back with its blind windows, and she thought: an hour is nothing. It's just a house.

"Fine," she said.

Marla's grin widened. She reached into her bag and pulled out a flashlight, the cheap kind with a plastic case. "Take this. And don't come out early. That's cheating."

Dessa took the flashlight. The plastic was warm from Marla's hand. She walked up the two concrete steps and pushed the door. It gave with a groan, and the caution tape fell away completely, and the dark inside was cool and smelled like wet paper and old wood.

She stepped over the threshold. Behind her, the door swung shut with a sound that was not a slam. It was softer than that, more like a sigh, and then the latch clicked home.

The flashlight clicked on. Dessa turned it on the door and saw the boards, and the back of the knob, and nothing else. She did not try the knob yet. That would be admitting something.

"An hour," she said out loud, to hear her own voice. It didn't sound as steady as she wanted.

The hallway went on in front of her.

***

That was the first wrong thing. Dessa had seen the house from the street. It was narrow, and the hallway should have run twelve feet to a back wall with a kitchen door. Instead the beam of her flashlight went out and out, past a staircase on the left, past two doors on the right, and kept going into a dark that leaned away from the light.

"Hello?" she said.

The word didn't echo. It just stopped, swallowed up.

She walked forward because standing still felt worse. The floorboards were dry and gave a little under her sneakers, and the wallpaper was the kind with faded flowers, every blossom the same brown, every stem the same brown. Somewhere behind the walls, water dripped. Or something did.

She stopped and knocked on the wall. Three quick raps, knuckles on plaster. "Hello," she said again, louder.

The house knocked back.

Three raps, from inside the wall, in the same rhythm. Dessa's heart went cold and fast. She knocked again, twice this time. Two knocks came back. She put her ear to the wall, and through it she heard a sound like a child breathing, slow and even, and then nothing.

"That's pipes," she said. "That's just pipes."

She turned the flashlight on the nearest door. It was painted white and the paint had bubbled, and when she pushed it open it swung into a small room with a window boarded shut. In the middle of the floor, lying flat, was a piece of paper. A drawing. Crayon, by the look of it.

She picked it up. The paper was stiff and dry, and the picture was of a house. The house had a door, and the door was open. Inside the door, small, was a girl. She had a bob of dark hair and a wide mouth and round eyes. She was holding a flashlight, and the flashlight was drawn with a yellow star of light at its tip. The girl was Dessa.

Dessa stared at it. She turned the paper over. On the back, in careful, child's letters, someone had written a name. Not hers. A name she didn't know.

"Who drew this?" she said.

No answer. The paper trembled in her hand, though the air was still.

She put it down and backed out of the room. Her flashlight beam shook. She thought about the hour, and about Marla on the sidewalk, and about how long an hour could be.

The hallway took her back the way she came, or tried to. Dessa kept her hand on the wall and walked, and the wall kept going. She passed a door she had not opened, then the same door again. Then came a stretch of wallpaper printed with small grey birds that she was certain she had not seen on the way in. Her flashlight found the front door at last, and she almost laughed with the relief of it. The boards she had pushed aside were still there, stacked against the frame, and the gap of cold night showed through. She reached for the knob.

The knob turned. The door did not move.

She pulled harder, got her other hand around it, and braced her feet on the warped floor. Sweat ran down her back. The door did not so much as creak. Under her hands it felt like a door that had never opened in its life, like the door of a bank vault or a tomb.

"Hey," she said, to the door, to the house, to nobody. "Hey. I want out."

A long silence. Then, from the wall beside her, close to her ear, a voice said, "The door only opens for someone who lives here."

Dessa let go of the knob and stepped back. The voice had been quiet and level and not quite a child's and not quite a woman's, and it had come from inside the wallpaper, from the space between the lath, where no one could stand.

"I don't live here," she said. Her own voice sounded thin.

"Then the door is not for you," the voice said. "Not yet."

Something slid behind the wall, a long soft sound, like a coat being dragged along a floor. Dessa turned the flashlight on the wallpaper. The birds were still there. She counted them without meaning to, and there were more of them than before, and they had all turned their small grey heads to face her.

She backed away down the hall. The voice did not follow. It did not need to. She could feel the house holding its shape around her, patient as a hand around a coin, and she understood, with a cold drop in her stomach, that the hour was not a dare anymore. It was a stay.

***

The hallway let her go up the stairs this time. Dessa climbed because there was nothing behind her she wanted to see again, and because the flashlight beam had begun to fail, the light going brown and weak at the edges. Halfway up, a door stood open that had been shut when she passed before.

Inside was a bedroom. A bed was made up neat, with a quilt the exact blue of the one on her own bed at home, and a pillow with the dent of a head still in it. On the nightstand sat a glass of water and a small lamp, and the lamp was lit. It had not been lit a moment ago. She knew that the way she knew her own name.

"No," she said, out loud, to the room.

The room did not answer. It only waited, the way a chair waits. The quilt was turned down at the corner, and the sheet beneath was warm, she could see the faint steam of warmth coming off it in the cold air. Her legs ached. Her eyes ached. She had not slept well in a week, she remembered, and the bed looked like the kindest thing she had ever seen.

She stood in the doorway with her hands at her sides. She did not sit on it. After a while the warmth went out of the sheet and the lamp dimmed. The room looked less like a welcome and more like a trap that had given up.

Downstairs, the kitchen was lit. The flashlight was useless now, so she followed the light. A chore list was pinned to the cupboard door in a hand she knew better than her own, the looping y of her mother's signature at the bottom. Dessa Ann, it said, in her mother's writing. Sweep the hall. Wipe the sills. Do not answer the walls.

"That's not her," Dessa said. "She's at home. She's asleep."

A chair scraped back from the table by itself. It was her chair, the one with the wobble, the one from their kitchen, and it sat waiting with its seat turned up to her like an open palm.

"Sit down," the voice said, from everywhere now, from the cupboards and the ceiling and the drain. "You are tired. Sit down and be home."

Dessa looked at the chair. She thought about her mother asleep in a house three streets away, and about the name on the back of the drawing, and about how a house that wanted her would know exactly which kindness to offer. It was a test, she thought. Every room was a test, and the answer to all of them was the same.

"No," she said. "I don't live here. I'm not sitting down."

The light in the kitchen went out, all at once, and the chair fell over with a bang. Somewhere above her a door slammed, and then another, and another, working their way down the hall toward her like someone walking.

Dessa ran.

***

The hallway had changed while the lights were dying. Doors she had not seen, doors she had walked past without noticing, stood open on both sides, and behind each one a room was waiting: a bed with the quilt turned down, a lamp already lit, a window with the curtain breathing. As she passed, each door swung shut behind her. It did not slam. That was worse. Each one closed with a soft, certain click, like a book being shut on a page someone had finished reading.

"You could stop," the voice said. It came from the wall beside her ear, keeping pace, and it sounded almost kind. "There are seven rooms behind you now. There's a fire in the front one. There's a plate on the table. It isn't cold yet."

"I don't live here," Dessa said, and kept running.

Her breath tore in her chest. The floorboards gave under her feet like something soft, and the hallway stretched, and stretched. She understood that the house was not trying to catch her. It was trying to be patient. It was walking her the long way round to a door of its own choosing, and if she stopped, even once, she would find she had been standing in front of it for a very long time.

So she did the only thing she could think of. She stopped.

The hallway went silent. Doors hung half shut. Somewhere a clock she had never heard began to tick, slow and wet, like a drip.

Dessa turned around and walked back the way she had come. The house had not expected that. A door ahead of her tried to close, and she put her hand flat against it and pushed it open again. The wood was warm, and it shivered under her palm like a held breath. She kept walking. She counted the doors as she passed, saying the numbers out loud, because counting was a thing a person did when they were going somewhere and meant to arrive.

"This is my hallway," the voice said, and it had lost its kindness now.

"No," Dessa said. "It's a hallway I'm walking down. Then I'm leaving."

The front door was there. It had moved, or she had, but it was there, four panels and a brass knob, and behind it the night and the cold and her own street. She put both hands on the knob and turned, and it turned easily, and the door did not move. She pulled. She set her feet and hauled until her arms ached. The door was like a wall painted to look like a door. Behind her, very close, a door clicked shut, and then another, and the hallway was getting shorter in the way a throat gets shorter when it swallows.

Dessa let go of the knob and knocked. Three fast knocks, and she made herself say the words out loud, clearly, because the house had been listening all night and it could hear her now.

"I don't live here. I live at number nine, with my mother, and I'm not home yet, so I'm going home."

She knocked again. "I don't live here."

And the house, which had been so careful to be patient, did nothing at all.

***

The door opened.

It did not swing. It simply was not closed anymore, and the cold came in across the step and touched her face, and Dessa stepped out onto the path and pulled the door shut behind her with both hands. The latch caught. Wood knocked on wood, ordinary as a plate set down.

She stood on the path at 44 Pell Street and breathed until the shaking stopped. Her hands were white on the handle, and she let go of that too.

The house behind her was just a house. Two windows boarded, one with the board hanging loose. A gutter with weeds in it. The sagging porch that had been there her whole life, the one the older kids said was haunted, the one with a story about the man who went in and never came out. Someone had been in it all along, she thought. The house had been full of the man, the way a stomach is full of what it has eaten.

On the top step lay a piece of paper. She did not pick it up. She could see it well enough from where she stood: a drawing of a girl with her face, done in crayon, the name on the back in careful letters. She left it where it lay.

Her own street was empty, and the lamps were orange, and a dog was barking two roads over. Dessa walked home with her arms folded tight and her chin up, and she did not look back, not once, not even at the corner where the house would have been in the corner of her eye.

At her door she took out her key and let herself in quietly. Her mother was asleep on the couch with the lamp still on, and her book face down on her chest. Dessa turned the lamp off and pulled the blanket up over her mother's shoulder, and stood for a moment in the warm dark of her own kitchen, listening. The clock ticked. It was the right clock, in the right place, in the right house.

Her mother stirred. "You're back," she said, half asleep. "Was it cold out?"

"Yes," Dessa said. "It was cold."

She went up to her bed and lay in it, and it held her. In the morning she would tell the older kids she had stayed the whole hour, and they would not believe her, and she would not care. She had said what she needed to say, and the door had opened, and that was the last she ever told anyone about it.
