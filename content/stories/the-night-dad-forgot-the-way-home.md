The click was small, but Ellis heard it. He lay still in the dark with his heart going. He listened to the second click, the softer one the latch made when the door settled back into its frame.

He got up. The hallway was cold under his feet, and the clock on the landing said 3:41 in red. Mum's door was shut. He went down the stairs the way he'd learned to, on the edges where the boards didn't complain. The front door stood open a finger's width, breathing the night in.

Outside, the street was empty and blue. Then he saw the coat, moving under the streetlight two blocks down. He saw the pajama legs beneath it, and the slippers that made almost no sound on the pavement.

Ellis ran. He didn't shout, because shouting would wake the whole street and his mother. Some instinct told him that was the worst thing he could do. He caught up at the corner by the postbox, out of breath, his own pajama cuffs soaked with dew.

"Dad."

His father turned around slowly, as if he'd been called from a long way off. His eyes were open and his face was calm. He was completely, terribly somewhere else.

"Ellis," he said. "You're up."

"So are you."

Dad looked down at himself, at the coat over his pajamas, at the slippers. Something crossed his face then, quick as a bird, and was gone. "I must have been..." He stopped. "I don't know what I was doing."

"You were walking."

"Yes." He said it like a man agreeing to a diagnosis. "I was walking."

Ellis took his hand. It was cold, and the fingers curled around his without any strength in them. "Come on. Let's go home."

They walked back together. Dad didn't say anything else, and Ellis didn't ask. He had the feeling that whatever answer he got would be worse than the quiet. At the door, Dad stopped and looked up at the house as if he'd never seen it before.

"Don't tell your mother," he said. "Not yet. She worries."

"She'd want to know."

"I know." Dad's voice was thin. "That's why."

Ellis got him inside and up the stairs and into bed. Mum didn't stir. In the morning the coat hung on its hook by the door, dry and ordinary. Dad sat at the table with his toast, and nothing at all was said.

***

Mum was at the sink with her back to him, scrubbing a pan that had been clean for a while. Ellis could tell from the rhythm of it, the way she went after the same spot.

"Dad went out last night," he said.

The scrubbing stopped. Started again.

"Out where, love?"

"Down the street. Two blocks. In his pajamas."

She set the pan in the rack and dried her hands on the towel. When she turned around her face was doing the thing it did when she was deciding something: smoothing itself out for his benefit. "He's been sleeping badly," she said. "He walks in his sleep sometimes. It's not dangerous."

"Mum. He was at the corner. He didn't know he was there."

"I'll talk to him."

"You said that last month."

Her mouth tightened. "Ellis."

That was the end of it, the way her saying his name like that was always the end of it. She went back to the sink. He sat at the table with his cereal going soft and felt the morning close over the thing like water over a stone.

Later, in his room, he took the box of chalk off the shelf: the big one from the summer fair, fat sticks of it, red and yellow and white. He sat on the floor with a stick of yellow in his hand and thought about the dark street. He thought about his father's cold fingers, and the way Dad had looked at the house like a stranger.

If he couldn't be woken, Ellis decided, then he could be guided. A trail laid down ahead of him. Something to follow when the rest of him was somewhere else.

He went out the back door and crouched on the path, and drew the first arrow pointing at the gate. He pressed hard so it would show. Then he walked to the corner and drew another, and another, all the way along the route they had taken, back to the postbox and the streetlight and home again. It took an hour and a half. His knees were green with grass and his fingers yellow to the wrist.

When he was done, he stood at the front step and looked back up the road at his crooked line of arrows, bright in the daylight, waiting.

"Right," he said, to nobody. "Now there's a way back."

Dad came home at six that evening with a smear of paint on his sleeve and no memory of how it got there. Mum had made a shepherd's pie and the kitchen smelled of onions and the radio was on low. Ellis watched his father over the table, how he held his fork, how he chewed, waiting for something to give.

"There's chalk on the front path," Dad said at last, pointing with his fork. "Quite a lot of it. Yellow arrows all the way to the corner. Did you do that?"

"Yes," Ellis said.

Dad laughed. It was a real laugh, surprised out of him, and for a moment the kitchen felt like the kitchen again. "Why? Are you leaving breadcrumbs? Are we Hansel and Gretel now?"

"It's a way back," Ellis said. "For when you walk at night. I thought if you saw them you might turn round."

The fork stopped moving. Dad set it down very carefully, the way he did when something cost him effort, and the silence stretched until the radio adverts came on and Mum turned them down.

"Ellis," Dad said, quieter now. "Have you been following me?"

"Only once. The first time."

"And what did you see?"

"You went to the end of the road and stood at the postbox and looked at the sky. Then you came home. It was raining."

Dad was silent a long time. Mum put her hand over his on the table and he did not move it, which was worse somehow than if he had. "I don't remember the postbox," he said. "I don't remember the road. I remember going to bed. I always remember going to bed."

"Then the arrows might help," Ellis said. "You don't have to remember. You just have to look down."

Dad looked at the table, and then at Ellis, and his eyes were wet and he did not hide it. "I'm sorry," he said. "I'm sorry you had to do that in the dark on your own."

"It wasn't dark," Ellis said. "It was getting light."

Mum laughed then, a small cracked laugh, and the two of them laughed with her. For a minute the whole thing sat at the table like a guest, strange but manageable, and they ate their pie.

***

The rain came on a Thursday and took the arrows with it. Ellis watched from the window as the yellow ran into the gutter and the path went grey and clean. Something in his chest went tight and cold. He went out with the chalk but the path was too wet to hold it. The lines smeared into nothing under his hands.

He meant to draw them again in the morning. He did not get the chance.

That night Dad's bed was empty at two, and at three, and at four. Mum stood in the front room with her coat on and her phone in her hand and did not call anyone, because what would she say. "I don't know where he's gone," she said, and it was the first time Ellis had heard her voice come apart like that.

They searched. They took the car down to the roundabout and back, up the hill to the shops, past the pub where the lights were off and the car park where the bins stood in a row. Ellis ran ahead with a torch, sweeping it into gardens and alleyways. He called his father's name into the wet dark, and the dark gave nothing back. The rain stopped around four and the streets were black and shining.

"Where would he go?" Mum said at the corner by the postbox. "Where does he go?"

"He doesn't go anywhere," Ellis said. "He walks. He just walks until something stops him."

The dawn came up grey and thin. They sat in the car with the engine running and the heater on, and neither of them said what they were both thinking. Somewhere in the last week Ellis had begun to believe the arrows had been holding his father in place. Now the rain had taken them. The street was dark and open, and his father was out in it somewhere, walking, with no way back.

"We'll find him," Mum said, but she said it to the windscreen, not to Ellis, and her hands on the wheel were shaking.

Mum drove the main roads twice, then pulled into the lay-by by the canal and turned the engine off. "We should go home," she said. "He might have gone back. He might be at the door."

Ellis looked at the clock on the dashboard. Five past six. The sky had gone the colour of dishwater. "The bus depot," he said.

"Why there?"

"Because it's warm and nobody asks you anything. He used to sit there when I was little. He told me."

Mum reversed without a word. The depot was a long shed of corrugated iron with a bench outside and a timetable in a plastic case. Ellis saw the shape on the bench before the car had stopped: a man in a coat, shoulders drawn up, hands inside his sleeves. Dad's head came around slowly, like a sleeper woken by a door.

"Ellis?" he said. His voice was thin.

"You're cold," Ellis said. He sat down and took his father's hands. They were colder than the bench.

"I missed the last bus," Dad said.

"There isn't a last bus. Not for years."

"I know." Dad looked at the timetable as if it might explain something. "I've been sitting here since midnight. I thought I'd wait for the first one. I thought I'd go somewhere. I didn't know where."

Mum came round the car and stood with her arms folded. "Get in," she said. "Please."

Dad didn't move. "I'm frightened," he said. The words came out flat, without any weight, as if he'd been carrying them so long they had worn smooth.

"Of what?" Ellis asked.

"Of the person I'm becoming. He gets up in the night and he walks and he doesn't tell me where. I don't want to be him."

Ellis thought of the arrows on the pavement, chalk in the rain. "You weren't walking to nothing," he said. "You were walking along the arrows. You followed them home every time."

"I didn't know that," Dad said. "I didn't know anyone was doing it."

"Well, someone was."

Dad looked at him then, properly, for the first time in weeks. "You?"

"Me."

Mum's hand came down on Dad's shoulder. "Get in the car," she said, softer now. "We'll talk at home."

***

Ellis waited until the hardware shop opened at eight. He bought a tin of red paint and a brush with a wooden handle, and he took them to the bus depot in his rucksack. The bench was empty. The timetable flapped in the wind. He started at the bench leg, a thick red stripe, and worked his way along the kerb, across the road, past the bins and the postbox, up the hill to the corner of their street. It took him four hours. His knees ached and the paint got into the creases of his hands.

Mum came out at noon with a sandwich and stood watching him paint. "You're serious," she said.

"It's not chalk," Ellis said. "It won't wash off."

She looked down the red line, running away from them like a thread pulled through the grey street. "No," she said. "It won't."

That night Dad slept in the chair by the window, and Ellis slept on the floor beside him, and neither of them said anything about it. The next evening, just after dark, Mum touched Ellis's arm and pointed. A figure was coming up the hill, slow, coat undone, head down. Dad's feet found the red line without him looking. He followed it past the postbox, past the bins, round the corner, and stopped at the front gate.

Ellis opened it. "You found it," he said.

"I followed the red," Dad said. "It's a good colour."

He came inside. Mum had made soup. They ate at the kitchen table with the radio on low, and Dad told them about the depot, and the timetable, and the man who used to work there who let him sit when he was a boy. Ellis listened. The red line stayed on the pavement outside, bright against the wet stone, and in the morning it was still there. It would always be there, Ellis thought. He had made it himself, with a brush and a tin of paint, and that was enough for now.
