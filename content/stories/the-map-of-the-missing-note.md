The brass bell above the door gave a small, polite ring. A woman stepped inside, bringing with her the smell of wet leaves and a large brown dog on a red lead. The dog sat down immediately and yawned.

"Good morning," said Juno. She was behind the counter with the pencil tin open in front of her. "Can we help?"

The woman looked at the window full of rolled paper, then at the shelves, then at Juno. "I've lost a name," she said.

Oda came through from the back room, wiping her hands on her apron. "Whose?" she asked.

"That's the trouble," said the woman. "I don't remember. I've been walking Biscuit here every Tuesday and Saturday for years, and there's a person I used to say hello to. At the bandstand, mostly. I can see their face. I just can't get the name."

Biscuit thumped his tail once at the sound of his own name.

"I'm Mira," the woman added. "And this is Biscuit. He's the one who actually knows the way."

Juno pulled a sheet of brown paper from the drawer. "We'll need to know where you walk," she said. "Every turn."

Mira nodded. "The park. Same loop. Twice a day, rain or shine."

Oda was already reaching for the black pencil.

***

Juno spread the paper on the counter and smoothed it flat. "So I'll draw the person," she said. "The face you remember. Then we find them."

"No," said Oda. She didn't look up from sharpening the black pencil. "Draw the dog's route."

"But she lost a name, not a walk." Juno kept her voice level. "A map of the park won't tell us who it was."

Oda set the pencil down. "The name is attached to the person, and the person is attached to the place. Start with what's certain."

"The face is certain," Juno said. "I could draw it. I'm good at faces."

"You're good at faces," Oda agreed. "But a face on paper is a guess. A path is a fact."

Mira looked between them. "I don't mind which," she said. "I just want to say hello properly again."

Juno pressed her lips together. She wanted to argue. She wanted to prove she could draw the thing that mattered, not the thing that was easy. But Oda had already turned away to fill the kettle, which meant the conversation was over.

"Fine," Juno said. "The route."

She picked up the black pencil. It felt heavier than usual.

Biscuit sighed and put his chin on his paws, as if he had heard all this before.

Juno unrolled a fresh sheet of brown paper across the counter. It crackled like something waking up. She set the black pencil down first, then the red one, and lined them up parallel. The blue pencil stayed in its drawer, but she could feel it there, the way you feel a splinter you haven't pulled out yet.

"Start with the gate," Mira said. "Biscuit always pulls left at the gate. Every single time."

"Left at the gate," Juno repeated, and drew it. A small square for the gate, a line curving left. Black for what is there.

Biscuit lifted his head from his paws and watched the pencil move. His ears came up.

"He knows," Mira said softly. "He knows that walk."

Juno drew the path as Mira talked. Past the duck pond, where Biscuit once tried to swim. Around the rose garden, where he always stopped to sniff the same three bushes. Up the gentle hill to the bandstand, where Mira used to sit on the bench and eat her lunch. The black lines gathered on the paper like a trail of ants.

"Anything missing?" Juno asked.

Mira frowned. "There's a bit at the end. Near the bandstand. I keep thinking someone was there."

Juno picked up the red pencil. Red for what is not. She drew a small circle at the bandstand and filled it in. Then she drew a line from the circle to the edge of the map, stopping at nothing. A name should go there. She knew it should. Her hand hovered over the drawer where the blue pencil slept.

"What's that one for?" Mira asked, nodding at the drawer.

"Nothing yet," Juno said, and closed it with her knee.

***

The park was busy with Tuesday. A woman jogged past with a pram. Two boys kicked a ball against the fence. Biscuit trotted ahead on his lead, nose down, tail up, reading the ground like a letter.

"He's taking us the same way," Mira said. "Every turn."

Juno followed the black lines on her map, but she watched the red ones. They weren't on the paper anymore. They floated ahead of her, thin as thread, winding through the park. Only she could see them. She had learned that much from the other maps. The red lines were for what was missing, and they always led somewhere.

At the duck pond, Biscuit stopped and whined. Juno stopped too. The red line went straight across the water.

"He never liked the ducks," Mira said. "Not since one bit his nose."

"He's not looking at the ducks," Juno said. She pointed across the pond. "He's looking at the bench on the other side."

The bench was empty. The red line ended there, curled up like a sleeping cat.

"There was someone," Mira whispered. "I remember now. A woman. She had a notebook."

Juno looked at her map. The red circle at the bandstand pulled at her, a small knot of not-there. She rolled the map up and tucked it under her arm.

"Come on," she said. "The bandstand's next."

Biscuit pulled ahead, and Mira hurried to keep up. The red lines flickered between the trees, and Juno followed them, step by step, toward the small circle she had drawn and the name that wasn't written yet.

The bandstand stood at the top of a small hill, its paint peeling in long curls. A woman sat on the steps, a notebook open on her knees. She looked up when Biscuit barked.

"Biscuit?" She said it like a question she'd been asking for years.

Mira stopped walking. "You," she said. "You're the one from the bench."

The woman stood. Her coat was the color of wet leaves. "I'm Nell," she said. "I didn't think you'd come back."

"I didn't know I was supposed to," Mira said. "I lost a name. I couldn't remember whose."

Nell held out the notebook. Names filled every page, written in pencil, each one with a date beside it. "I've been keeping them," she said. "All the names of everyone I've ever walked with. When you stopped coming to the park, I wrote yours down so I wouldn't forget it. But then I forgot the sound of it anyway."

Juno looked at the notebook. There, halfway down a page, was Mira's name. And next to it, a smaller name: Biscuit.

"You used to walk together," Juno said. "Every Tuesday."

Mira sat down on the step. Biscuit put his head in her lap. "I remember the bench," she said. "And the ducks. And your thermos with the chipped lid."

"You remember the thermos," Nell said, and laughed. "I still have it."

Juno unrolled her map. The red line from the bench to the bandstand glowed faintly. She traced it with her finger. "You didn't lose a name," she said. "You lost the person who knew it."

Mira looked at Nell. "I'm sorry I stopped," she said.

Nell closed the notebook. "You didn't stop," she said. "You just took a different path. I kept walking the old one."

Biscuit wagged his tail. The red line on Juno's map faded to nothing, and the black path underneath it was just a path again.

***

Back at the shop, the brass bell rang once as Mira and Nell went out together. Biscuit trotted between them. Juno watched from the window until they turned the corner onto Corvid Lane.

Oda was at the counter, winding a length of string around her fingers. "You drew the dog's route," she said.

"I did," Juno said.

"And you wanted to draw the person."

"I did."

Oda nodded. "The map knew," she said. "You just had to walk it twice."

Juno picked up the black pencil. She put it back in the tin. The red pencil went next. Her fingers hovered over the blue one in its drawer, but she didn't open it. Not yet.

"Why does the blue pencil have to wait?" she asked.

Oda smiled. "Because you'll know when," she said. "And when you know, you won't need to ask."

Juno rolled up the map of the park. She tucked it into the drawer with the others. Then she sat on the stool by the window and watched the lane.

A man with a umbrella walked past. A girl on a bicycle. The bakery put out its sign. The lane was ordinary, and strange, and full of people who had lost things they hadn't named yet.

Juno took out a fresh sheet of brown paper. She didn't draw anything. She just held the pencil and waited for the bell to ring.
