The rope came up slack, and Wren knew before the bucket broke the rim what it would hold. Mud. Grey mud, thin as porridge, sliding off the bucket's lip and pattering back down the shaft. Below, the dark had no bottom she could see.

Her grandmother stood beside her with her hands wrapped in her apron. "Empty it anyway," she said. "We don't waste what the well gives."

Wren tipped the bucket. The mud slumped onto the flagstones and lay there, gleaming, useless. Above them the sky was the color of the mud, and the lamp on its iron hook by the wellhead burned low, its wick furred with soot.

"That's the last," Wren said. "It's been the last for six days."

"Then it's six days of lastness and we're still standing." Her grandmother turned toward the lane. "Come. The elder will want to hear it from the one who pulled the rope."

Hollowmere had nine lamps. Wren had counted them since she was small enough to be carried past them. She could tell you each one's name. There was the Gate, the Miller's, the Three Sisters by the bakery, the Hanging, the Long, the Widow's, the Bell, the Schoolroom, and the wellhead's own. That last one had no name, because everyone called it simply ours. All nine were fed from the spring beneath the well, and the spring had been failing since the frost broke.

The elder's house smelled of tallow and old paper. She was mending a wick with a needle when they came in, and she did not look up, which meant she already knew.

"Three days," the elder said. "The Miller's lamp went out at noon. I heard it from here, the way you hear a thing that has been burning a long time stop."

Wren's grandmother made a sound. "Three days to what?"

"To dark." The elder set down her needle. "Then the lamps go one by one, and the village goes with them. Not the houses. The village. You know what Hollowmere is without its lights."

Wren did know. Everyone knew. A place that could not light its own lanes was a place the road forgot, and the road was all that kept them fed.

"There has to be something," Wren said.

The elder looked at her then, and her eyes were the color of the mud in the bucket.

***

The elder lit one candle and set it between the three of them. By its light she took down a book so old the spine had gone to powder at the corners.

"You know the story of the drowned village," she said.

"Everyone knows it," Wren said. "It's a story for children."

"It is a story for anyone who lives on top of a drowned village." The elder opened the book. "Listen. When the spring first rose, it rose so hard it took the lower village with it. Nine houses, nine families, all of it gone under. The water came up through the square. It never went back down. That is the well. That is the spring. They are the same water."

Wren's grandmother folded her arms. "And the coins?"

"Nine coins," the elder said. "One from each family, thrown down as an offering so the water would remember them kindly. The spring has run sweet ever since. Until now."

"Someone took them," Wren said.

"No one took them. They went down with the village, and no one has gone after them, because no one has needed to." The elder turned the page. "The old words say the spring wakes when its coins come home. Not one coin. All nine. Put them back in the well and the water rises."

"And if it doesn't?"

The elder was quiet a moment. "Then Hollowmere learns to live in the dark. The road forgets us, and in a year there is no Hollowmere to speak of. That is what the spring is. It is not a luxury. It is the whole of us."

Wren looked at the candle. It was burning straight up, steady, as if nothing in the world were wrong.

"The well is deep," her grandmother said slowly. "Deeper than rope. No one has gone down in my lifetime."

"I'm not afraid of deep," Wren said.

The elder closed the book. "You should be," she said. "But you are also the only one here who can hold her breath and her nerve at the same time. I have watched you since you were small. Sit down. I will tell you the shape of the shaft, and the shape of what is under it, and then you may decide whether you are still not afraid."

Wren sat. Her heart was going like a bird in a sack, and she did not let it show.

***

The moon was a thin curl of light when Wren climbed over the well's stone lip. A rope went down into the dark, knotted every arm's length, and her grandmother held the other end wrapped twice around the oak post.

"Three tugs means pull you up," the elder said. "Four means trouble. Five means I come down myself, and I am too old for that, so do not make me."

Wren nodded and swung her legs into the shaft. The stone was cold and slick with moss. Down she went, hand under hand, the rope burning her palms. The walls narrowed. The air turned thick and mineral, then wet, then cold enough to make her teeth ache. Above her the moon shrank to a coin, then a pinprick, then nothing.

Her foot found water. It was black and flat and did not move. She let go of the rope and sank to her shoulders, and the cold went through her like a blade. Then she looked up and saw a roof.

It was a chimney, tilted, green with weed. Beyond it a gable, and beyond that a street sign still hanging from its bracket, the letters furred with silt. The drowned village lay under the water like a held breath. Walls stood where walls had always stood. Windows opened onto nothing. Somewhere a door swung slowly on its hinge, back and forth, though there was no current to move it.

Wren swam toward the street. The water was so clear in places that she could see cobbles under her, and so dark in others that she could see nothing but her own hands. A fish slipped past her cheek, then another, silver and unbothered. She paddled on.

In the square, something moved. A shape, tall and thin, crossed between two houses and was gone before she could fix her eyes on it. She told herself it was a curtain of weed. She told herself the water played tricks. Then it moved again, closer, and she stopped swimming and hung in the dark with her heart knocking.

"Who's there?" she said, and her voice came out in bubbles, thin and useless.

Nothing answered. The shape was gone. Wren swam on, because the alternative was to turn back, and turning back was not something she had come all this way to do.

***

The chapel stood at the end of the street with its steeple snapped off and its door hanging open. Wren went in. Pews floated at odd angles, bumping the ceiling like slow animals. The altar was still there, and on it a stone box, and in the box, coins.

She counted them by touch. Three. They were cold and heavy and stamped with a wheel. When she closed her fist around them, the water around her shivered, as if the whole drowned place had felt it.

Then the water began to rise.

It came from below, pushing up through the flagstones, and the chapel filled with a low groaning sound. Wren kicked for the door. Her lantern, hooked to her belt, swung and struck a pew and went out, and the dark closed over her like a hand. She could not see the door. She could not see her own arms. Somewhere above her the surface was a faint grey shimmer, and she swam for it, but the roof was there, and the beams, and she struck her head and saw stars that were not stars.

She floated. Her lungs burned. The coins were still in her fist, biting her palm, and that small pain was the only thing that told her which way was up. She followed it. Her shoulder hit stone, her knee hit stone, and then her hand found the doorframe and she pulled herself through and out into the street.

The street was gone. Water pressed her against the chapel wall, and the drowned houses were only shadows now. She had no light. She had no rope. Three coins and a burning chest were all she had, and the way back was a black maze she had swum once and could not see at all.

A door swung open to her left, slow and deliberate, and beyond it a stair went up into the dark. Wren did not ask who had opened it. She took the stair, and the water followed her, and above her, very far away, a rope hung down into the black like a thread of hope.

The stair climbed out of the water and into a wide room with a ceiling lost in shadow. Wren hauled herself onto dry stone and lay there, coughing, while the coins rolled loose from her fist and rang on the floor. When she could lift her head, she saw the drowned square. It was not underwater. It was a great hall of green-black stone, and above it the lake hung like a roof, pressing down on nothing. Houses stood upside down on the far wall. A pale figure sat on a throne of broken bells at the centre, and it was counting something in its lap.

"Three," it said, without looking up. Its voice was the sound of a bucket in a deep well. "You brought three. There are nine."

Wren got her feet under her. "Then give me the other six."

"I do not give." The keeper raised its head. Its face was smooth and white as a fish, and its eyes were two dark coins. "I trade. You have something I want." It held up a hand, and in its palm lay six coins, green with age, each one stamped with a fish. "The rest of the nine, for the memory of your home. All of it. The road, the door, the faces. You will climb out of the well and not know why you went down."

Wren's mouth went dry. "Why would you want that?"

"Because I have been down here a long time," said the keeper, and for a moment its voice was almost kind. "And I am tired of being the only one who remembers."

She thought of her mother's hands on the lamp wick, her father's boots by the door, the smell of the tallow and the wet wool. The keeper watched her face and smiled, slow and certain.

"Take the trade," it said. "Your village gets its water. You get to live. What is a memory worth, against that?"

***

Wren looked at the six coins in the keeper's hand. Then she looked past it, at the drowned square, and she saw the truth of the place. The coins were not in the keeper's keeping. They were scattered, caught in the cracks of the stones and the roots of the upside-down houses, and the keeper sat on its throne because it was afraid to leave it.

"No," she said.

The keeper's smile did not change, but the water above them shivered. "No?"

"I will not trade my home for a bucket of water." Wren bent and picked up her three coins. "But I will take the rest." She ran at the nearest house, and the keeper rose from its throne with a sound like a bell cracking. Wren did not look back. She saw a coin glinting in a doorway and snatched it. She saw another in the gutter and dug it out with her nails. The water above them began to fall in slow, heavy drops, and each drop struck the stone like a hammer.

"You cannot carry them all," the keeper called. "You cannot climb with them."

Wren shoved the coins into her mouth, one by one, and tasted the green metal. She found the last two in the bell throne itself, and the keeper lunged for her, and she twisted away and ran for the stair. The water was coming down now in sheets. She climbed with her teeth clenched and her lungs full of copper, and the rope was there, and she caught it, and she hauled herself up hand over hand while the dark roared below her.

At the top, dawn was grey and cold. Her mother was holding the rope. Wren spat the nine coins into her palm and said, "Throw them in."

They threw them in, one by one, and the well coughed, and then it sang. Water came up clean and cold, and it did not stop. Wren sat on the stones with her hands shaking and her mouth tasting of metal, and she remembered everything.

***

By noon the lamps were lit again, all nine of them, and the lanes of Hollowmere ran with water. Wren stood at the wellhead with her grandmother and watched the bucket come up brimming and clear.

"You went down," her grandmother said. "And you came back with your memory whole."

"I did." Wren looked at the water. "It wanted to keep it. I didn't let it."

Her grandmother was quiet. Then she reached out and touched Wren's hair, once, the way she had when Wren was small enough to be carried past the lamps.

That night Wren walked the lanes and counted. The Gate, the Miller's, the Three Sisters by the bakery, the Hanging, the Long, the Widow's, the Bell, the Schoolroom, and the wellhead's own. Nine lights, burning steady. She stopped at the last one and put her hand against the warm iron, and she thought of the keeper on its throne of broken bells, counting in the dark, and she did not feel sorry for it.

She felt the water moving under the stones, deep and patient, remembering. Somewhere far below, a door swung slowly on its hinge. Wren turned her back on the well and went home.
