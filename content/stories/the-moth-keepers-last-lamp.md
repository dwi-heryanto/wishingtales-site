Juna knew something was wrong the moment she stepped into the shed. Her grandfather stood at the workbench with his hands flat on the wood, staring at the big brass lamp. Usually the lamp hummed a little, the way a kettle does just before it sings. Today it was silent.

"It's gone out," Grandfather said, without turning around. "The wick drowned in its own oil. I've kept this light burning for forty years, and tonight of all nights it quits."

Juna set down her school bag. "Tonight? What happens tonight?"

"The moths come." He turned then, and his face was tired in a way she had never seen. "Once a year, the pale moths fly in from the cliffs. They follow the lamp home over the water. They have done it since before I was born."

"Then we light it," Juna said.

"Child, I've tried." He showed her the lamp's belly, dark and wet. "There's no flame left in it."

Through the shed window, the sea was turning the color of old tin. Juna thought of the moths out there somewhere, flying blind over the dark water. "When do they arrive?"

"When the sun goes down," said Grandfather quietly. "Not long now."

***

Tavi was already at the door when Juna ran up the path. He had a screwdriver in one hand and a rag in the other, because Tavi always brought tools to a problem.

"Your grandfather's lamp?" he said. "Everyone in the village knows. My mother says the moth keeper's light has never failed."

"It has now." Juna pulled him inside. "Help me fix it."

They unscrewed the brass cap and lifted the glass chimney free. Inside, the wick lay in a puddle of oil, black and soggy. Tavi poked it with the screwdriver.

"It's too short," he said. "Burned right down. There's nothing left to catch."

"Can't we stretch it?"

"You can't stretch a wick. It just falls apart." He held up the glass. A crack ran along the bottom like a hair. Then he turned it and found another. Then a third, thin as a thread.

Juna's stomach dropped. "Three cracks."

"Three cracks," Tavi agreed. "Even if we had a wick, the light would leak out sideways. The moths wouldn't see a straight beam. They'd see a fog."

Grandfather came and looked over their shoulders. He didn't scold. That was the worst part. He just sighed and put his hand on Juna's hair.

"We'll manage," he said, but his voice told a different story. Outside, the sky was going pink at the edges, and the sea was getting dark.

By the time the sun touched the water, the moths began to come.

Juna stood on the rocks with Tavi and watched them rise out of the dark fields behind the village. First a few, pale as ash. Then dozens. Then the air above the shore was full of soft, fluttering wings, turning and turning, looking for the light that should have been there.

"They're early," Tavi whispered.

"They're hungry," Juna said. "They've flown all day to get here."

A moth landed on her sleeve. It was grey and small, with two bright spots on its wings like tiny eyes. It rested there, tired, and then lifted off again, turning toward the sea.

Juna's heart gave a jump. "Tavi. They're going the wrong way."

"I know." He was already on his feet. "Without the lamp they'll follow the moon on the water. They'll fly out over the waves and never come back."

Grandfather stood at the door of the keeper's hut, leaning on his stick. "The light is the only thing that turns them," he said quietly. "No light, no turning."

Juna looked at the dead lamp in her hands. A wick too short. Glass cracked in three places. She turned it over and over, as if the answer might be written on the bottom.

"It's not just oil," she said slowly. "The lamp was never just oil, was it?"

Grandfather's eyebrows lifted a little.

***

"Tell me about the old keeper," Juna said. "The stories. All of them."

Grandfather lowered himself onto the bench. "Which one?"

"The trick. The one where the lamp had nothing in it."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he smiled, just slightly. "The old keeper's lamp went dry one night, years back. Same trouble. No wick, no oil. So she made a light that didn't need either."

"How?"

"Sea glass," he said. "Ground fine as salt and packed in tight. Dry grass for the flame to catch and hold. And one white flower, picked by moonlight, laid on top. She set it in the chimney and it burned blue and steady till morning."

Tavi frowned. "That's not a real lamp."

"It's a keeper's lamp," Grandfather said. "It only works if you believe it will."

Juna was already moving. "Sea glass is on the shingle below the point. Dry grass grows by the fence. And there's one white flower left in the garden, I saw it this morning."

"It's dark," Tavi said.

"It's night," she said. "That's exactly when we need it."

She grabbed his hand and pulled him down the path toward the shore, the dead lamp swinging at her side. Behind them the moths drifted, soft and confused, toward the shining water.

The shore path was all loose stones and cold wind, and Tavi slipped twice before they reached the point. “Juna, wait, the tide’s coming in,” he said, pointing at the white line of foam creeping up the shingle.

“Then we’re quick,” Juna said. She dropped to her knees and scraped at a patch of flat stones. The sea glass glittered faintly even in the dark, green and blue and one piece of amber. “Hold the bag open.”

Tavi held it. “How much?”

“Until it feels heavy.” She dug with both hands, and the stones rattled into the sack. Behind them, out over the water, a pale flicker, then another, like someone shaking out a sheet of light.

“They’re coming,” Tavi said.

Juna didn’t look up. “Grass next. By the fence.”

They ran along the edge of the field, and the dry grass hissed against their legs. Juna pulled up a handful and sniffed it: dusty and sweet. “This is right,” she said. “Now the flower.”

The garden gate hung crooked. Juna went straight to the one white flower, closed tight against the night. She picked it carefully, and it sat in her palm like a small cold star.

Back on the shore, the moths were everywhere. They drifted low over the shingle, pale wings catching the last grey light, and the sea shone beneath them, wide and hungry. Tavi’s voice went small. “They’re going to the water.”

“Not yet,” Juna said. She knelt by the flat rock where the lamp always stood.

She ground the sea glass with a stone until it was fine as salt. She packed it into the cracked chimney, pressed the dry grass in tight, and laid the white flower on top. Then she struck a match. The grass caught, and the flame ran blue and low and steady, and the glass dust sparkled like frost.

***

The first moth came to the lamp and stopped. Its wings opened and closed, slow and certain. Then another, and another, until the air around the rock was crowded with soft, pale shapes.

“Look,” Tavi whispered.

Out over the water, a few moths had already reached the foam. They turned, all together, as if a hand had brushed them back. The blue light reached across the shingle, and the moths followed it, drifting up the path toward the house and the garden and the dark trees beyond.

Juna stood with her hands at her sides. The lamp burned without oil, without a wick, and it did not flicker once.

Footsteps crunched behind them. Grandfather came slowly down the path, his coat pulled tight. He stopped when he saw the blue flame, and the moths circling it like a slow, bright snow.

He didn’t say anything for a long moment. Then he put his hand on Juna’s shoulder. “You remembered.”

“I remembered,” she said.

Tavi let out a breath. “Does this mean the lamp’s fixed?”

“No,” Grandfather said. “It means it’s a keeper’s lamp.” He smiled at Juna, and she smiled back, and the moths kept coming home.
