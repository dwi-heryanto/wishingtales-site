Liora climbed the last step of the sky stair with the evening candle held in both hands. The stair went up and up from the valley, past the roofs of her village, past the clouds, and ended here, on a small stone landing with nothing above it but dark. She set the candle down on its iron stand. The wick was old and black and bent from a thousand nights of use.

"All right," she said, and struck the flint.

The spark caught. The flame stood up straight and gold, and light spilled out across the whole sky. Below, in the valley, the windows of her village went dark one by one, as if the candle had told them it was time.

Then came the soft rushing sound she loved. The stars woke and drifted down into their places. The Swan spread its wings in the north. The Plough settled over the mill. A small red star, always late, hurried into the west and stopped, breathless, exactly where it belonged.

Liora sat on the edge of the landing with her chin on her knees.

"Good evening," she said to the Swan.

The Swan twinkled once, which meant good evening back.

Far below, her mother's window was the last one lit. Liora liked to see it. It was a small warm square, and it meant someone was still awake, waiting.

The wind came up the stair and ruffled the flame, and the flame leaned and straightened. Liora yawned and wrapped her arms around her knees. The night was settled. The sky was full. She had all night to sit here, and then the morning would come and she would climb down and sleep.

"Hold steady," she told the candle, the way she did every night.

***

The flame shrank. Liora saw it from the corner of her eye, and turned, and watched it pull down small and smaller until it was no bigger than a blue bead on the wick. The gold light went thin. The dark leaned in close.

Above her, the Swan shivered. One wing came loose from its place and drifted, slow as a leaf, toward the horizon.

"No," said Liora. "No, no. Hold."

She crouched and breathed on the wick, gently, the way her mother blew on soup. The blue bead flickered but would not grow.

"Please," she whispered. "Just a little more."

The red star slipped sideways. The Plough tilted, one handle coming free.

Liora stood up and looked down the long stair. Halfway down, on a ledge, she kept a spare candle in a tin box. She had never needed it before. She gathered the evening candle into its holder and tucked it under her arm, and she began to climb down fast.

Below her, the village slept on, all those dark windows, none of them knowing.

"Wait for me," she said to the sky. She said it out loud, and her voice was small in all that dark. "Wait for me, I'm coming."

The stars kept drifting. Liora took the steps two at a time, her breath loud in her ears, the blue bead of flame cupped in her hand and burning cold against her palm.

The tin box sat on the ledge where she had left it, cold and dented and patient. Liora knelt and worked the lid open with her thumbs. Inside, the spare candle lay in its little bed of cloth, pale and straight and perfect.

"There you are," she said.

She set the evening candle down carefully, sheltering its blue bead with her body, and lifted out the spare. Its wick was dry and white. She held it to the bead and waited. For a long breath nothing happened. Then the flame leaned across, thin as a thread, and caught.

"Good," said Liora. "Good."

The new flame was orange and cheerful. She stood up, and the wind came.

It came down the stair like water down a chute, cold and quick, and it took the orange flame off the wick as neatly as a hand. Liora gasped and cupped her fingers around the wick. Nothing. A curl of smoke, and then the wind carried that away too.

"Oh no," she said. She looked up. The stars were sliding, slow but steady, tilting toward the edge of the world. "Oh no, no, no."

Then her palm felt warm. The evening candle, forgotten on the step, still held its blue bead, and the bead had not gone out. Liora scooped it up in both hands and closed them.

"All right," she said, and her voice shook. "All right. Only you, then."

The ember sat in her cupped hands, cold-burning, blue as deep water. She could not carry the holder and the ember both. So she left the holder on the ledge, and she took her shoes off so her feet could feel the steps, and she began to climb, holding the last of the sky's light in her hands like a bird.

***

The stair went higher than she had ever climbed in the dark. Her toes found each step by feel. The ember pulsed between her palms, and once, when she stumbled, it dimmed so far she could not see it at all.

"Stay," she told it, and it came back, faintly blue.

At the top of the stair, the steps stopped being steps and became a wide flat door, round and silver, set into the sky itself. Liora had never seen it before. She had never climbed this high. She lifted one hand from the ember, just enough to knock.

Three soft knocks, and the door opened inward like a slow eye.

The moon sat inside on a curved bench, mending something with a needle of light. It was smaller up close than she expected, and older, with a face like a worn coin.

"You are the one who lights the candle," said the moon. "Your mother did it before you. She was better at it."

"I know," said Liora. "Please. The flame shrank, and the stars are coming loose. May I have a spark?"

"A spark is a serious thing," said the moon. "It costs what you love best. One memory of home, and it is gone from you. Do you still want it?"

Liora looked at the faint blue light in her hands. Far below, the windows of her village were dark, and the stars above them were drifting.

"Yes," she said. "Please."

The moon set down its needle of light and held out one pale hand. "Then give me the memory you love best. Think of it clearly, and let it go."

Liora closed her eyes. The memory came at once, the way it always did. Her mother's voice in the dark room, low and steady, humming the lullaby with the long slow turn in the middle. It was the tune her mother had hummed every night before the fever took her. Liora had kept it the way you keep a stone in your pocket, warm from holding.

"I can feel it," said the moon gently. "Say it is yours to give."

"It is mine," whispered Liora. "I give it."

Something slipped out of her like a breath let go. The lullaby went thin, then faint, then it was only a shape without sound, like a word said too many times. She opened her eyes. The moon's hand held a small bright spark, no bigger than a seed, and her own open palm felt lighter and colder than before.

"Here," said the moon. "Carry it in your cupped hands. Do not let it touch the sky, or it will spend itself."

Then the silver door began to close, slowly, like an eye going back to sleep. Liora turned and ran. Down the stair the wind came at her, but this time it only pulled at her sleeves. Her feet found each step without looking. Above her the stars were sliding sideways, loose and pale, drifting toward the edge of the dark. One of them guttered out and fell, a thin white line.

"Not yet," she told them. "Hold on."

***

At the top of the sky stair the candle stood in its iron cup, and its flame was a bead of blue, no bigger than a drop of rain. Liora knelt, and her knees ached from the climbing, and her hands shook from holding the spark so long. She tipped her palms toward the wick.

The spark slid down like honey and caught. Light ran up the candle in a rush, gold and steady, and the flame stood tall again. It threw its warmth out across the dark, and the stars felt it. One by one they stopped drifting and settled back into their places. The loose ones slid home with small soft clicks, like beads dropping into a bowl. The fallen star left a pale mark that slowly folded shut.

Below, the village stirred. A window went warm, then another, then a long row of them, all the way down the hill.

Liora sat back on her heels and watched the sky hold still. She was tired to her bones, and her hands smelled of smoke and cold air.

She began to hum without meaning to. The tune came out low and steady, with a long slow turn in the middle, and she did not know where she had learned it. It felt like something she had found, not lost.

"That is a good song," said a voice behind her. The moon had come to the top of the stair, leaning on its needle of light like a walking stick.

"I do not know it," said Liora.

"Then it is yours now," said the moon, and went back down to its silver door.
