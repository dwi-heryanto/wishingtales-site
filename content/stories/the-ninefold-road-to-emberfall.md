The cold came first, then the dark. On the first night after the Emberfall guttered out, Wren's village huddled around stubs of candle. Everyone told themselves the lantern would relight by morning. On the second night, the well froze. On the third, the elder called every household to the square and spoke with frost on her breath.

"The Emberfall is out," she said. "The Ninefold Road is the only way. Someone must walk it to the ninth stone and light the flame again. The road takes payment. You know what it takes."

Wren stepped forward before she could think better of it. "I'll go."

Her mother's hand closed on her sleeve. "You're fourteen."

"I'm the only one who isn't needed here. You have the little ones. The elder has the sick." Wren pulled free gently. "And I'm not afraid of the dark."

The elder pressed the last lantern into her hands. It was brass, dented, and heavy with oil. "The road will ask for memories," the elder said. "Each stone takes one. Some ask for little. Some ask for more. You must decide what you can bear to lose."

Wren looked at the frozen well, the shuttered windows, the breath of the village rising in white plumes. "I can bear it."

The elder's eyes were old and sad. "Everyone says that at the first stone. Remember this: the road brightens as you pay. The light is real. But so is the cost."

Wren lit the lantern from the last candle in the square. The flame caught, small and steady. Then she walked past the tanner's shed and the frozen millpond and the signpost that pointed north. She did not look back until the village was only a cluster of dark roofs behind her.

***

The Ninefold Road began as a cart track, rutted and familiar. Then it narrowed. The hedges grew tall and close, and the air tasted of iron and old snow. After an hour, Wren saw the first stone. It was a low pillar of grey rock, waist high, with a hollow cut into its top. As she approached, the hollow filled with a pale light. A voice spoke from nowhere she could point to.

"What do you give?"

Wren's mouth went dry. "What do you want?"

"A memory. One that matters. Not a name, not a face. A sound. A feeling. Something you would miss."

She thought of her mother's lullaby, the one sung low over her cot when she was small. It had a line about the river carrying the moon home. She could hear it now, faint and warm. Wren closed her eyes and offered it up.

The stone flared. The road ahead lit with a soft gold glow. Wren felt the lullaby go out of her like a candle pinched between fingers. She knew it had been there. She knew it had mattered. But the tune was gone, and the words with it, and what remained was a hollow the size of a handprint.

"You may pass," said the voice.

Wren lifted her lantern and walked on. The gold light stayed with her, and the road was easier to see, but her head felt lighter in a way she did not like. She tried to hum the lullaby and found only silence where the melody should have been.

***

By the second hour, the road had climbed into bare hills. The gold light from the first stone had begun to fade. Wren kept her lantern high and her eyes on the ruts. The cold bit through her coat. Somewhere ahead, a bird called once and did not call again.

She stopped at a bend where the road split around a standing stone twice her height. This one had no hollow. Instead, a figure sat on the stone's flat top, swinging its legs. It was a girl about Wren's age, with dark hair and a patched cloak. She smiled as if they were old friends.

"You're late," the girl said. "Most people come through before the second night."

"I didn't know there was a schedule."

"There isn't. I'm just being friendly." The girl hopped down. "I'm Tam. I'm going to the ninth stone too. My sister's there. Or she was. The road took her three winters ago, and I've been paying my way toward her ever since."

Wren studied her. "How many stones have you passed?"

"Seven. This is my eighth." Tam's smile flickered. "I'm running out of memories. But I'm close. What about you?"

"First stone. I gave my mother's lullaby."

Tam nodded slowly. "That's a fair price for the first. The road is gentler at the start. It gets greedier." She fell into step beside Wren. "What's your name?"

"Wren."

"Wren. Like the bird." Tam laughed. "Good. Hold onto that. Names are the last thing to go, or so I've heard."

They walked together through the fading gold light, and Wren found the silence easier with someone else in it. The road curved north. The second stone rose out of the dark ahead, its hollow already glowing.

Tam touched her arm. "Whatever it asks, don't give it more than you have to. And don't look back once you've paid. The road doesn't like regret."

Wren tightened her grip on the lantern. The flame inside burned steady, small and stubborn, and she fixed her eyes on it as they walked toward the light.

***

The second stone took nothing, which should have been a comfort. Wren stepped past it and felt the road brighten under her boots. Tam said nothing, only watched her face as if reading a page. They walked in silence until the gold light thinned to something the color of old honey. The road bent sharply west around a shoulder of black rock.

At the bend, the light stopped.

Something sat in the middle of the road. It had no shape Wren could hold in her eye for long. One moment it was a tall figure in a hood, the next a drift of smoke with a hundred sleeves. Cold came off it like breath off a frozen pond, and the cold made her teeth ache.

"You have a memory that tastes of laughter," the thing said. Its voice came from everywhere at once. "Give it to me and pass."

Tam pulled her back a step. "Don't answer it directly," she whispered. "They love a straight answer. It gives them a handhold."

Wren's heart was a fist against her ribs, but her mouth, she discovered, was braver than her heart. She lifted the lantern. "You want a memory? Then you'll have to earn it, and I don't think you can. You can't even hold a shape. I've seen better shadows on a wall at noon."

The thing swelled, furious, and the cold pressed in until the lantern flame shrank to a blue bead.

"Riddles!" Wren said quickly, and her voice cracked but held. "You want to trade? Then answer me this. What runs without feet, and answers without a mouth, and never leaves but always comes back?"

The shadow hung there, considering. Wren's hands shook so badly the lantern rattled.

"A road," the thing said slowly, tasting the word.

"Wrong," Wren said. "A memory. And you can't have mine, because I've decided to keep it."

She stepped forward. The thing recoiled from the lantern light as if it had been struck, and the cold broke around her like a wave around a rock. She was through, and Tam was through behind her, and the bend was behind them both.

Only then did Wren notice what had gone quiet inside her. She reached for her father's laugh, the deep rolling one he gave when the goat got into the flour. She found a smooth blank place, like a page torn out cleanly. She knew she had loved the sound. She could not hear it anymore.

Tam was watching her. "What did it take?"

"Nothing I can name," Wren said, and walked faster so she would not have to say more.

***

The road ran flat and grey to the fifth stone, and the fifth stone had a hollow worn deep by other hands. Wren paid it without being asked. Something went out of her that she could not quite locate, and she did not let herself look back.

It was at that stone she met the boy.

He sat on the grass at the road's edge with his knees drawn up and his chin on them. He stared at the hollow as if it were a well he had dropped something into. His coat was too thin for the cold, and his boots were wet through.

"You're going the wrong way," Wren said, because it was the first thing that came to her.

"I'm not going anywhere," the boy said. "I'm deciding."

"Deciding what?"

He looked up. His eyes were red-rimmed, and there was a stubbornness in his jaw that Wren recognized, because she had felt it in her own face that morning. "Whether I can pay what's left and still be me at the end of it. My name's Tam. I've come from the east, and I've paid six stones already. This one wants the last thing I have of my brother. His voice, mostly. The way he used to sing while he mended nets."

Wren sat down beside him, because her legs had decided it for her. "Then don't pay it."

"And go home with the village still dark?" Tam shook his head. "My sister's little. She's afraid of the dark, and she's been afraid of it for three nights now. I'd rather be a smaller person and a useful one." He laughed, and it was a thin, unhappy sound. "My mother used to say I was stubborn as a mule. I didn't think she meant it kindly."

"Mine said the same," Wren said. "I always took it as a compliment."

Tam looked at her properly then, and something eased in his face. "You're going to the Emberfall too."

"Yes."

"Then we'll pay together," he said, and stood, and offered her his hand. "It's easier with someone beside you. I've been alone since the third stone, and alone is a poor companion on this road."

Wren took his hand. It was cold, but it was a hand.

"Wren," she said. "Like the bird."

"I know," Tam said. "I heard you tell the shadow thing. You were very brave and very stupid, and I've been thinking about the rhyme ever since."

They walked together. The fifth stone's hollow glowed behind them. Wren reached for the face of her best friend at home, the one who had braided her hair that morning and told her she would come back. She found only the braid and the promise, and no face to put them to. She kept walking. Tam did not ask what it had taken, and she was grateful for that, more than she could say.

Ahead, the sixth stone began to shine.

***

The seventh stone took nothing. Wren had braced herself for it, hand tight on the lantern's cold handle, but the light in the hollow only brightened and let her pass. Tam laughed, a short, surprised sound. "The road gives one for free," he said. "My gran told me that. I'd forgotten until now."

Wren looked at him. His face had thinned in the last hour, the way a candle thins when the wick burns low. She was afraid of what the eighth stone would ask.

The eighth stone sat at the top of a long rise. From it she could see the Emberfall, a red seam in the dark where the road ended. Tam stopped when he saw it. His hand went to his chest, the way people do when they have lost something and are checking whether it is still there. "What's that light?" he asked. "Why are we walking toward it?"

"You know why," Wren said. "You told me. You told me at the fifth stone."

Tam frowned at her as if she were a stranger who had used his name. "Did I?" He looked down at his own boots and did not seem to recognize them either. "I'm sorry. I don't remember telling you anything. I don't remember getting here."

Wren took his arm. The hollow of the eighth stone was already glowing, patient as a mouth. "Tam. Listen. You're Tam. You came from a village east of mine, and you paid the road to get here, and you've been walking with me since the fifth stone. That's what you told me." She was talking fast. "That's what you know."

"Those are just words," he said, and his voice was quiet and awful. "I know words. I don't know why I'm standing here."

The stone took its price. Wren watched it happen. She saw the exact moment Tam's face went smooth. She saw the question leave it. She saw the reason he had come lift off him like smoke from a snuffed wick. He stood in the road blinking. "Where am I?" he said. "Who are you?"

Wren held his arm and felt the whole long climb in her legs. She could leave him here. He was not paying anymore, he had nothing left to pay, and the road would not stop him walking back down. She could go on alone to the Emberfall and light the lantern and be quick about it. The village would be warm by morning, and Tam would find his own way home with whatever was left of him.

He looked at her with mild, empty curiosity, waiting for an answer, and she could not do it. Not to a hand that had been offered to her in the dark.

"You're coming with me," she said, and took his hand. "That's all you need to know. I'll remember for both of us."

Tam came. He came because she pulled him, and because a road that eats memories does not eat legs. They climbed the last rise together. Wren talked the whole way. She told him his name and her name and the rhyme she had used on the shadow thing. She told him the braid she could not put a face to. She told him everything she still had, out loud, so that someone in the world would be carrying it with her.

***

The Emberfall was not a fire. It was a place in the road where the dark wore thin, and through the thin place came a light like the light under a door in a warm house. Wren lifted her lantern to it and the lantern drank. The wick caught with a sound like a held breath let go.

She stood there a moment with the light in her hand. Then she went down.

The road down took nothing at all. It was the same road, and it was kind now, and it carried them both. Wren kept Tam's hand in hers and kept talking. His name. Her name. The braid. The promise. She had been saying it so long it had worn smooth and lost its meaning, but she said it anyway, because meaning is not the only thing a word can do.

Her village showed in the dark from a long way off. Windows. Doorways. A woman in the square with a candle, and another woman behind her with a lantern. Then the whole square lit, and the light of the Emberfall ran down the lanes like a tide, and the cold let go of the walls.

Wren walked into the square with her lantern up and Tam trailing beside her. The people turned and looked at her, and she looked back at them and did not know a single face. The braid belonged to no one. She turned to Tam, and Tam only smiled, as blank as a cup.

A woman pushed through the crowd. She had Wren's own sharp chin and a scarf Wren half remembered. She stopped in front of her and looked at her as if she had been watching the road for three days.

"Wren," she said. "Oh, Wren."

The name went into her like the light into the lantern, and it caught. She knew the woman. She knew the square. The face of her best friend came back to her, and then her father's laugh, and her mother's lullaby, all of it coming home with the name.

"I went and got it," Wren said, and her voice shook. "I brought the light back."

Her mother put her arms around her and held on. "I know," she said. "I can see."

Wren pulled back and turned to the boy standing behind her. He was looking at the lit square with his mouth open, seeing it for the first time. "This is Tam," she said. "He walked the whole road with me. He's going to stay with us tonight."

Tam looked at her. Something in his face was starting to move again, slowly, like a wick taking the flame. "Tam," he said, trying it. "That's my name."

"Yes," said Wren. "I told you I'd remember for both of us."
