The last box said KITCHEN, and it wasn't true. Maya's mother had written it with a fat marker at the old apartment. Inside were three lamps, a bag of forks, and one roller skate nobody had ever explained.

"We'll sort it later," her mother said, and set the box by the door.

Maya carried her own box up the stairs. The house was old the way a tree is old. The floorboards dipped in the middle, and the banister wobbled when she held it. Her room had a window with wavy glass, and the window looked out at a yard full of dark bushes.

By suppertime, every box was inside, and the house still smelled of dust and lemon soap. Maya's father ordered noodles. They ate them sitting on the floor because the chairs were buried somewhere.

"First night in the new house," her father said, lifting his paper cup. "Cheers."

Maya clinked her cup against his. Then she heard it.

A whisper. Soft, and quick, like someone reading a list very fast. It came from the staircase.

"Did you hear that?" Maya asked.

Her mother tilted her head. "The fridge?"

"No. Voices."

Her father smiled. "Old houses make noises. Pipes, wood, wind. It's a whole orchestra."

Maya listened. The whispering had stopped. She finished her noodles and tried to feel brave about the dark hallway. It didn't work very well.

When the lights were off and her blanket was up to her chin, the whisper came again. It climbed the stairs, slow and steady, like footsteps made of breath. Maya lay still and counted her own heartbeat.

By the time she fell asleep, she had counted a lot.

***

Morning made the house look ordinary. Sunlight found the wavy glass and made little pools on the floor. Maya ate cereal standing up and decided she had dreamed the whispering.

Outside, a boy was bouncing a ball against the fence. He had a blue jacket and a haircut that looked like his mother had done it.

The ball rolled through the gate and stopped at Maya's shoe. She picked it up.

"Are you the new one?" the boy asked. "In the tall house?"

"I'm Maya. We moved in yesterday."

"Leo." He took the ball and didn't throw it. "How was your first night?"

"Fine," Maya said. "Except the stairs whisper."

Leo's eyebrows went up. "They do that. My brother says the house is haunted."

"Haunted how?"

"Haunted like, nobody knows exactly. But kids have always said things about it. My nan says she used to run past your gate after dark." Leo bounced the ball once, quiet. "She says someone's still up there."

Maya looked back at the tall house. The wavy window looked down at her like an eye.

"How long has it been empty?" she asked.

"Long time," Leo said. "You're the first family in years."

"So the whispering is real."

Leo shrugged. "I've never been brave enough to listen." He tossed the ball to her. "I'll listen with you, if you want."

Maya caught it. Her hands were steady, which surprised her.

"Tonight?" she said.

"Tonight," Leo said.

That night Maya lay still until the house went quiet. Her parents' door clicked shut. The hallway light went dark. Then the whispering started, soft as a sleeve brushing the wall.

She slid out of bed and pulled on her cardigan. On the landing, the cold of the stairs came up through her socks. "Hello?" she said, quiet. The whisper went on, a thread of sound, leading her up.

Step by step she climbed. The fourth stair creaked. The seventh hummed. At the eleventh, the whisper stopped so suddenly she put out a hand to steady herself. Her fingers found the banister, and under her foot the board moved.

"Leo?" she whispered. A shadow shifted at the bottom of the stairs. "I'm here," he said. "I came in the back door. Your mum left it open for the cat." He crept up beside her. "What did you find?"

"This step. It's loose." Maya knelt and pressed the edge. The board lifted a finger's width, then dropped. "There's something under it. I can feel a corner."

Leo crouched next to her. "Let me." He slid his fingers into the gap and wiggled the board. It came up with a dry rasp, and they both leaned over the dark space beneath.

"It's a box," Maya said. "A tin box." She reached in and drew it out. It was small and cold, and the lid was stuck with rust. "Someone hid this here."

"Open it," Leo said. His voice had gone low and excited.

The lid resisted, then gave. Inside was a notebook with a cloth cover, and on the first page, in careful pencil: Eliza.

Maya turned the pages. The writing was small and neat, with dates in the corner of each entry. "It's a diary," she said. "Listen. 'Today I hid this under the stair. If we move, I want a piece of me to stay in the house.'"

Leo leaned closer. "Who's Eliza?"

"She lived here," Maya said. "A long time ago." She read on, her finger moving under the words. "'The stairs whisper when I walk on them. I think they're trying to tell me something. I told them a secret, and now they keep it.'"

"That's creepy," Leo said, but he was smiling.

Maya turned another page. "'My family says we must go. I don't want to. I hid my diary so the house would remember me. If you find it, please leave it here.'" She looked up. "She wanted it to stay."

"So the house isn't haunted," Leo said slowly. "It's just... remembering."

Maya closed the diary and held it against her chest. The whispering had stopped. The stairs were still.

"We should put it back," she said.

"Or we could read more," Leo said. "There's heaps of pages."

"Tomorrow," Maya said. "We'll read it together. And then we'll decide." She slid the tin back under the step and pressed the board down. It clicked into place, and the stairs seemed to settle under her feet, like a breath let out.

The next morning, Maya and Leo sat on the back step with the diary open between them. The pages were soft and smelled of old wood. "Look," Maya said, pointing to a drawing of a house with smoke curling from the chimney. "That's this house. She drew it from the garden."

Leo leaned closer. "She's good. She got the crooked window right."

Maya turned the page. "Listen to this. 'Papa says we must go to the city. He says the house is too big for us now. I don't want to leave my room or the apple tree or the stairs that whisper.'" She paused. "She was sad."

"Keep going," Leo said.

"'I hid my diary under the loose step. I told the house to keep it. If someone finds it, they will know I was here. They will know I loved this place.'" Maya looked up. "She wanted someone to remember her."

Leo frowned. "But we're here now. We found it."

"Yeah," Maya said. "But she doesn't know that."

They read on in silence for a while. Eliza wrote about her cat, a grey one named Pepper, and about the way the sun came through the window in the morning. She wrote about a friend called Ruth who lived next door. On the last page, she had drawn a small heart and written, 'Goodbye, house. Be good.'

Maya closed the diary. "She left it for us," she said. "For whoever came next."

Leo nodded slowly. "So what do we do?"

Maya looked at the stairs. "We leave it there. But we tell her we found it."

That evening, Maya sat at the kitchen table with a pencil and a piece of paper. She wrote: 'Dear Eliza, We found your diary. We read it. We like your house. We'll take care of it. Thank you for leaving it for us. From Maya and Leo.' She read it twice, then folded it small.

***

The next day, Maya went up the stairs alone. The whispering started as soon as her foot touched the third step. It was soft, like a curtain moving in a breeze. She didn't feel scared. She felt like someone was listening.

She knelt by the loose step and lifted the board. The tin was still there. She opened it and placed the diary gently inside, then laid her note on top. She pressed the lid down.

"Here you go," she said quietly. "I found it. You don't have to whisper anymore."

The whispering stopped. For a moment, the staircase was perfectly still. Then Maya heard something else. It was a small sound, like a sigh, and it came from everywhere and nowhere.

She put the board back and pressed it until it clicked. Then she stood up and brushed the dust from her knees.

Downstairs, Leo was waiting. "Did you do it?" he asked.

"Yeah," Maya said. "And I told her we're here now."

Leo grinned. "Do you think she heard you?"

Maya looked back at the stairs. The afternoon light fell across the steps in warm stripes. "I think so," she said. "The house feels different."

"Different how?"

"Like it's not waiting anymore."

That night, Maya lay in bed and listened. The house creaked and settled around her, but there was no whispering. Just the ordinary sounds of an old house at night. She pulled the blanket up to her chin and closed her eyes. She thought about Eliza, who had loved this house and left a piece of herself behind. And she thought about herself, the new girl who had found it.

In the morning, she would go down and eat breakfast with her family. She would look at the crooked window and the apple tree and the stairs that used to whisper. And she would feel, for the first time, that this was her home too.
