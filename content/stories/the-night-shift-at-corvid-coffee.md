The espresso machine died at 11:07 p.m., right in the middle of a shot, with a sound like a cough and then nothing.

Wren stared at the portafilter, then at the dark gauge on the front. She pressed the button again. The machine offered a sad gurgle and went silent.

"Okay," she said, to the empty café. "Okay."

The mop bucket sat in the corner. The chairs were stacked, then unstacked, because her mother had told her never to stack them before ten. The big window showed the street and the street showed nothing. Wren wiped her hands on her apron and pulled out the manual, which was laminated and held most of its pages.

That was when the knocking started.

A man in a good coat, swaying slightly, pressed his face to the glass door and rapped with the flat of his hand. "Open," he said, muffled through the glass. "I can see you."

"We're closed," Wren called.

"You're not closed. You're standing there."

She flipped the lock and opened the door exactly four inches, the way her mother did. "We close at ten. There's a bench outside. There's a phone in your pocket."

The man looked at the bench. He looked at his coat. Then he nodded, once, and sat down, and Wren closed the door and locked it and leaned her forehead against the cool wood for a moment.

Her phone buzzed on the counter. Her mother's voice, recorded at some hour when her mother had been too tired to be careful: "Wren, honey, I'm sorry. I'm going to be late. Maybe very late. There's a thing with the lease: the landlord wants to talk again. I don't know what I'm going to tell him."

A pause, and the sound of a car passing.

"One month," her mother said. "He says one month. Don't wait up, okay?"

Wren stood in the middle of the floor with the dead machine and the stacked chairs and the man on the bench outside. Then she took the ledger down from the shelf, the big clothbound one with the coffee rings on the cover, and she opened it to tonight's page and wrote the date at the top, because that was what you did. The dates went back years. Her mother's handwriting, then hers under it, then her mother's again.

"Right," she said. "One month."

***

The nurse came in first, as she always did, at 11:30 exactly, still in her scrubs with a badge clipped to her chest. Her name was Delia and she had been coming here for four years and she ordered a large drip and a bagel, and she sat at the counter, not the booths, because the counter meant company.

"Machine's dead," Wren said.

"I can see that from here. It's got that look." Delia slid onto her stool. "Drip's fine. Drip's what I want anyway. Nobody needs foam at midnight."

Wren poured it and set it down and Delia wrapped both hands around the cup like she was warming them on a fire.

"Long one?" Wren asked.

"Eleven hours. One of the guys on the ward has been singing the same song since Tuesday. He doesn't know the words past the first line." Delia took a sip. "How's your first night alone?"

"The machine died and a man banged on the door and my mother left a message about the lease."

"That's a full first night." Delia tore her bagel in half. "You handled it."

The door chimed at 11:52 and the taxi driver, Mr. Okonkwo, came in shaking rain off his jacket that had not been raining when Wren looked last. He ordered tea and a slice of the lemon cake and asked, "The machine?"

"Dead."

"Ah. Well." He sat in the far booth and spread his newspaper out, and every so often he read a line aloud to nobody in particular.

At 12:20 the student came in: the girl with the canvas bag and the heavy eyes, who never ordered more than a tea and a refill. Wren had seen her a dozen times but did not know her name. She bought her tea and sat near the window and opened a notebook and did not write in it.

Wren wiped the counter and watched them: Delia at the bar, Mr. Okonkwo in his booth, the girl by the glass. Three people, in the middle of the night, in a café that was supposed to close in a month.

"You know," Delia said, quietly, "I could go home. But home's just a bed and then it's back. This is the part of the night where I'm a person."

Wren looked at the ledger on the shelf. Her mother's handwriting. Her own beneath it. The date at the top, and under it, in ink: Delia, drip, bagel. Okonkwo, tea, cake. It was a list of nothing. It was, she was starting to think, the whole thing.

The pipe went at 1:40, during the quiet stretch when Wren was restocking the pastry case and Mr. Okonkwo had finally stopped talking about the mayor's road plan. She heard it before she saw it: a hiss like a kettle deciding something, then a wet snap under the sink. By the time she got around the counter, water was already running over the tile in a flat, determined sheet, and the towel she threw down went dark in seconds.

"Ah," said Mr. Okonkwo, standing up. "That's the elbow joint. Those old copper ones go."

"It's fine," Wren said. "It's fine. I've got a rag." She had two rags and a mop and no idea what to do with any of them. The water kept coming. She crouched and looked under the sink at the brass fitting, and the fitting looked back at her, split clean along its seam, and she thought: call her. Call Mum. That was the thing you did. That was the whole point of having a mother and a phone and a café with her name on the sign. She had the phone out of her pocket before she decided not to use it. Her mother worked days. Her mother had been awake since six. Her mother would come, because she always came, and Wren would watch her face do the thing it did now whenever she looked at the account book.

"Shutoff's behind the machine," she said. "Delia, can you…"

"Already there." Delia had her sleeve pushed up past her elbow. "Tell me when."

"Now."

The hiss died to a drip. Wren put her hand flat on the wet floor and breathed. Then she stood up, got the wrench from the drawer, and got down again, and it went badly. The wrench slipped. The nut was seized. She scraped her knuckle open on the pipe and said a word her mother would not have liked, and Delia laughed from behind the machine, and Mr. Okonkwo said, "Turn it the other way, child," and she turned it the other way and it gave, and water came out in a little apologetic burp and then stopped.

"There," Delia said. "Now you're a plumber."

"I'm a person with a wet floor."

"Same thing, most nights." Delia took the mop out of her hands without asking. She moved like someone who had mopped a hundred hospital corridors and had opinions about it. Wren stood with her bleeding knuckle under the tap and watched the nurse work, and something in her chest that had been braced all night let go half an inch.

"You could have called me," Delia said, not looking up. "I've got hands."

"I forgot people had them," Wren said. It came out more honest than she meant.

***

The student was still by the window. She had not moved through any of it, not the hiss, not the shouting, not the mop, and now she had her hands wrapped around the cold tea and was watching the street. Wren brought her a fresh cup without being asked and sat down in the chair across from her, which she had never done, because her mother never did it either.

"On the house," Wren said. "We had a flood. It's a whole thing."

"I heard." The girl looked at the cup. Her eyes were red at the rims, the way eyes get at three in the morning when sleep has been ruled out. "You didn't have to."

"I know."

For a while neither of them said anything. The refrigerator hummed. A car went past on the wet street and threw a smear of light across the wall and was gone. The girl's notebook lay closed under her elbow, and Wren could see a corner of a page with a single line written on it and then scratched out.

"Can I ask you something?" the girl said.

"Sure."

"Why's this place open at night?" She said it fast, like she had been holding it. "Nobody comes. I mean, a few people. But it can't make money in the dark."

"It doesn't," Wren said.

"So why?"

The honest answer was her mother, and the sign, and the twenty-two years, and the ledger on the shelf. Wren looked at the girl and gave her the other one. "Because my flat's empty and loud," she said. "When I'm there at night I can hear the neighbors' television and the pipes and my own head. Here there's the machine and the radio and someone breathing at the counter. So I guess I come here so it isn't just me."

She heard herself say it and stopped. She had not known it was true until it was in the air.

The girl nodded slowly, as if Wren had handed her something and she was checking its weight. "My flat's the opposite," she said. "There's nothing in it. My roommate left in September and took the sofa. It's so quiet I can't sleep. I keep the shower running sometimes just to have a sound."

"That's terrible for the bill."

"I know." The girl almost smiled. "And the walls are thin, so when someone's home next door I can hear them living, and I hate it, and then when they're not I hate that more." She turned her cup a quarter turn on the saucer. "That's why I come. It's loud in a way that doesn't ask anything of me."

Wren looked across the room at Delia, who had propped the mop by the door and was back on her stool, and at Mr. Okonkwo, asleep with his chin on his chest, and at the light lying yellow on the wet floor. A café that's loud in a way that doesn't ask anything of you. She thought about the ledger and the date at the top of the page and the month she had left, and she thought, for the first time all night, that she might have something worth arguing with.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Priya."

"Priya." Wren got up and went to the ledger and wrote it down. Tea, refill. Then she wrote, under it, in her own small hand: stayed till morning.

At ten past four the bell over the door rang, and Wren looked up expecting Priya's roommate or the man who sometimes bought a whole cake at dawn. Instead her mother stood in the doorway with her coat half-buttoned and her hair flat on one side from sleeping in the van.

"You're supposed to be at home," Wren said.

"I couldn't sleep." Her mother crossed to the counter and put both hands flat on it, as if she needed the wood to hold her up. "I did the numbers again at two in the morning. Then I did them again at three, in case I'd been kind to myself the first time. I hadn't."

Delia was already sliding off her stool. "Sit down, Ruth. You look like a dropped pie."

"I feel like one." But she sat. Wren poured her the last of the coffee and watched her wrap her hands around the cup like it was a coal she was trying to keep alive.

"There's the rent," her mother said, "and the machine's on its last legs, and the man from the bank has a voice like a closing door. I think we should give notice at the end of the month. Go quietly. Owe less."

Wren had known this was coming. She had rehearsed a hundred arguments in her head and each one had come out sounding like a child whining for a toy. So instead of arguing, she fetched the ledger. She set it on the table and turned it around, and then she turned it again, so it was facing her mother.

"Read the last page," she said.

Her mother looked at it, then at her. "It's a list of orders."

"It's a list of people."

There was Delia, night shift at the hospital, two espressos, one to drink and one to carry. There was Mr. Okonkwo, who drove a taxi until the small hours and slept in the corner booth between fares, and who had once fixed the door latch with a screwdriver he kept in his glove box. There was Priya, tea, refill, stayed till morning. There were a dozen names above them and a dozen small things written beside the names.

"Delia brought me ice at two o'clock," Wren said. "A pipe burst and I didn't know what to do, and she knew. Mr. Okonkwo can't sleep in his own flat because it's too quiet now his wife's gone to her sister's. He can sleep here. Priya's place is so empty it echoes, and she comes here to sit in a room that's full."

"That's very sweet," her mother said, and her voice had gone thin and careful, the way it did when she was trying not to cry. "It doesn't pay the rent, love."

"No. But it's a reason, and reasons are worth something." Wren put her finger on the page. "Give me one more month. Not the shop. Me. One month of nights. If the numbers are the same at the end of it, we give notice and I'll never bring it up again."

Her mother was quiet for a long moment. Then she took the pencil from beside the till and turned the ledger to a fresh page, and at the top she wrote the date, and beneath it she wrote: Wren, nights, one month.

***

The sky was going grey at the edges by the time the espresso machine gave its last gasp. Wren was on her knees in front of it with the back panel off and a roll of electrical tape in her teeth when her mother came around the counter and crouched beside her.

"That's not where that wire goes," her mother said.

"It's where it's staying."

They worked together without talking much, her mother's hands remembering the machine from years before, Wren handing her what she asked for. When they got the pump to catch, it made a noise like a cat being stepped on, then settled into a ragged hum.

"It'll do for a week," her mother said. "Maybe two."

"Then we get two weeks."

Priya was first to the counter, her tea already half gone from the bottom of her cup. "You fixed it?"

"We taped it," Wren said. "It's the same thing, if you don't look closely."

The first proper espresso came out bitter and half the size it should have been, and Delia drank it standing up and pronounced it the best she'd had all night, which was a kind of lie and a kind of truth. Mr. Okonkwo woke, blinked at the light coming through the window, and ordered his usual, then sat and drank it slowly, watching the street come alive.

"Same time tomorrow?" he said, on his way out.

"Same time tomorrow," Wren said.

By half past six the café was empty. Her mother had gone to sleep in her own car in the lot, and Wren stacked the chairs and wiped the counter and did not count the money, because the money would still be there later. Then she climbed into the corner booth and pulled her coat over herself and lay with her cheek on her folded arms.

Through the window the street was gold. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere behind her the espresso machine hummed its ragged hum, holding on.

She slept, and the café stayed open.
