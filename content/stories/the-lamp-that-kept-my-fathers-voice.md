The storm came in from the northeast the way the old men said the bad ones always did. It came low and grey and patient. By the time the harbor bell started clanging, the *Nell* was already past the point.

Sena heard the bell from her bedroom, where she had been pretending to sleep. It rang three times, then four, then stopped. The ringer had lost count, or lost hope. She got up and went to the window. The sea beyond the breakwater looked like a field being plowed by something invisible. Rain hit the glass in flat, hard sheets.

Downstairs, her mother was standing at the kitchen table with both hands flat on the wood. It was as though the table might otherwise float away.

"He'll run for the cove," her mother said, before Sena had asked anything. "He knows the cove. He's run for the cove a hundred times."

"Then he'll be home by morning."

"By morning." Her mother nodded, too many times. "Put the kettle on. We're not going to sit here like stones."

They did sit, mostly. The kettle boiled and they drank the tea and the tea went cold in the cups. Wind pressed against the windows, and the house made the small complaining noises houses make when they are frightened. Around three in the morning the bell rang again, and this time it did not stop. Sena counted eleven rings. Then she understood that the man ringing it was not counting the hours. He was calling out across the water, over and over, to anyone who might still be listening.

By dawn the storm had moved inland and the sea lay flat and grey as a tabletop. Fishermen went out. They came back with the *Nell*'s stern locker, one oar, and nothing else. Sena's father had gone down off the point in the worst of it, they said, and the point had never given anyone back.

Her mother did not cry that day. She washed the cups and put them away, and when she set the second cup on the shelf, she set it carefully, face out, as if he might come down for it later.

***

The shed behind the house was where her father kept everything he could not bear to throw away. Rope ends, a tin of screws, three broken reels, the smell of tar and cold metal. Sena went out there a week after the funeral, because her mother had asked her to find the good hammer, and because the house had grown so quiet that she could hear herself breathing in it.

Rain ticked on the shed's tin roof. Sena moved a crate of paint cans and found a lamp underneath. Brass, heavy, dented at the base, with a handle worn smooth by a hand that had held it many times. She knew it. It used to hang from the hook by the back door on summer nights when the power went. Her father would carry it out to check the boat lines. The light would swing along the path ahead of him like a small tame star.

"You're still here," she said to it, which was a foolish thing to say to a lamp.

She found a stub of candle in the tin of screws, wedged it into the holder, and struck a match. The wick caught. The light was small and orange and did not reach the corners.

Then the lamp spoke.

"Sena."

Her hand jerked and the matchbox fell. The voice had come from everywhere and nowhere, from the brass and the air above it. It was her father's voice, his ordinary voice, the one he used at the table with his mouth full.

"Sena, girl, don't look so startled. You look like a caught fish."

She could not move. The flame stood perfectly still, though the shed door was open and the wind was moving everything else.

"How," she said. Her throat closed on the rest.

"Never mind how." The voice was warm. It was patient. It sounded exactly like him. "I've been waiting in the dark a while. Tell me what's happened at home. Is your mother eating?"

Sena sat down on the paint crate and told him. Her voice shook and her hands shook, and the lamp listened. When she stopped, it said, "There now. There now," and the two words settled over her like a blanket. She wept in the cold shed with the rain ticking above her, and for the first time in a week the house did not feel empty at all.

The lamp sat on the kitchen table now. Sena had carried it in from the shed, and her mother had not asked where it came from. She had only looked at the brass, at the wick, and turned away. But at night, when the wind scraped the windows, Sena lit it. The voice came soft as a hand on her hair. "How was school?" it asked, and she told it about the algebra test, about Mira's new boots, about the gull that had landed on the woodpile and refused to leave. The lamp laughed at that, a low rumble she knew in her bones. Each time it spoke, the room felt warmer, and Sena slept without dreaming of water.

But in the morning, the blue tin of tea was gone from the shelf. Her mother stood with her hand on the empty spot.

"Did you move it?" she asked.

Sena shook her head. They searched the cupboards, the pantry, the cold pantry, and found nothing. Her mother's mouth went thin. "We had it yesterday. I made tea at four." They did not speak of it again, but her mother's eyes kept sliding to the lamp, which sat cold and dull on the table.

The next night, Sena lit it again. The voice told her a story about a dog that could count, and she laughed until her ribs hurt. When she woke, the framed photograph of her father on the dock was gone from the wall. Only a pale square remained, and her mother was standing in front of it with her fists at her sides.

"Sena," she said. "Where is the picture?"

"I don't know."

"You don't know." Her mother's voice was flat. "First the tea, then the picture. Things are leaving this house." She turned. "And you sit there night after night talking to that thing."

"It's not a thing. It's..." Sena stopped. She could not say it.

"It's what?" Her mother waited. When Sena said nothing, she walked out into the rain and stood on the porch, and Sena watched her shoulders shake through the glass.

That night, the lamp spoke again. "Your mother's angry," it said. "She was always quick to anger. Remember the time she threw the kettle?" Sena did remember. She had been six. The kettle had dented the wall. But her father had laughed and called her mother a storm in a teacup, and the storm had passed. Now the memory felt thin, like paper held up to light. She looked around the kitchen and tried to count what was missing. The blue tin. The photograph. The chipped mug her father had used for coffee. The wool blanket from the back of the sofa. Each one had gone after the voice had spoken. Each one had been his.

"What are you doing?" she whispered.

"Talking to my girl," said the lamp. "Isn't that what you wanted?"

***

By the third day, the house had begun to look like a house nobody lived in. The hooks by the door were empty. The shelf where her father's books had stood was bare. Her mother moved through the rooms with a broom, as if she could sweep the absence away, and her face was gray.

"We have to get rid of it," her mother said. She stood in the kitchen doorway with her coat still on. "That lamp. It's eating the house."

"It's not eating anything." Sena's voice came out sharp. "It's just a lamp."

"Then why do you talk to it every night?" Her mother stepped closer. "Why do you look at it like it's him?"

Sena looked at the brass, at the blackened wick. The flame had not been lit since morning, but she could feel it waiting. "Because it sounds like him," she said. "Because when it talks, I don't have to remember he's gone."

Her mother's face crumpled. She sat down on the floor, right there in her wet coat, and pressed her hands to her eyes. "I miss him too," she said. "But this, this is not him. This is a thing that takes. It took the photograph. It took the blanket I wrapped you in when you were small. It took the mug I washed every morning for sixteen years." She looked up. "What will it take next, Sena? The house? Me?"

Sena wanted to argue. But she thought of the empty hooks, the bare shelf, the pale square on the wall. The voice had been a comfort, and the comfort had a price. She had known it for days and had not let herself count.

"One more night," she said. "I'll take it out to the cliff tomorrow. I'll let it speak once, and then I'll put it in the sea."

Her mother stared at her. "Why once more?"

"Because I need to hear what he left me," Sena said. "Not the things. The words." She did not know if that was true. She only knew she could not drop the lamp into the water without saying goodbye.

Her mother rose slowly. She took off her coat and hung it on a hook that was still there. "Then I'll come with you," she said. "I won't let you stand on that cliff alone."

That night, Sena did not light the lamp. It sat on the table, cold and silent, and the house around it was smaller than it had been, and quieter, and somehow more hers. She lay awake and listened to the wind, and she practiced the words she would say to the voice in the morning.

***

By first light the storm had not lessened; it had only changed its mind about direction. Snow came sideways now, hard as sand, and the sea beyond the point was a churn of grey and white that made Sena's stomach turn. Her mother stood in the doorway with two coils of rope over her shoulder and her mouth set in a line Sena had learned not to argue with.

"Tie yourself to me," her mother said. "And tie the lamp to yourself. If the wind takes one of us, it takes all of us."

They went out together. The path to the cliff was drifted over, and twice Sena sank to her knees and had to be hauled up. The brass lamp hung against her hip, cold through her coat, and she could feel it waiting. When they reached the flat rock above the point, the wind screamed so loud that speech was useless. Her mother pointed at the rock, at the rope, at Sena's hands. Sena understood: here, tie off, do it now.

She knelt and set the lamp on the stone. Her fingers were so numb she could barely work the little catch, but the wick caught on the third try. The flame that rose was small and steady and did not blow out.

"Sena," said the voice. Warm, patient, the way he had sounded on winter mornings when he came in from the boat and stamped the snow off his boots. "You've come a long way in bad weather."

"I'm putting you in the sea," she said. The words came out cracked. "You're eating the house. You're eating us."

The voice was quiet for a moment. "I know," it said. "I knew the first night. A voice like mine doesn't belong in a lamp. It belongs in a man, and the man is gone."

Her mother's hand closed hard on her shoulder.

"Then why did you keep talking?" Sena shouted. The wind took the words and flung them.

"Because you kept listening," the voice said, gently. "And because there was one thing I never said while I could. I was so busy going out that I never told you what I saw when I looked back. The two of you in the window. That was the whole of it, Sena. Not the boat. Not the catch. The window, and the two of you in it."

Sena's face was wet and she did not know if it was snow or not.

"That's what I left you," the voice said. "Not a voice in a lamp. A way of looking back. Use it on each other and not on me."

The flame guttered, then stood straight again, and the voice said, very softly, "Now. Before I forget how to stop."

Sena picked up the lamp. It was warm in her hands, warmer than it had any right to be. She drew back her arm and threw it out over the point. The brass turned once in the air, catching the grey light, and then the sea took it without a sound she could hear.

***

Going back was harder than going out. The wind was at their faces now, and the snow had buried the path entirely, so they walked by the feel of the ground and the shape of the land. Her mother went first and Sena held the rope and followed the tug of it. Neither of them spoke until the house loomed up out of the white, its one window glowing where her mother had left a candle.

Inside, the silence was enormous. Sena stood in the middle of the kitchen and turned slowly, taking stock. The clock was gone from the mantel. The chair by the stove was gone. The shelf where his pipe had sat was gone, and the wall behind it was bare boards, not even a mark to show where the nail had been. The house was smaller than it had been. It was also, she thought, honest.

Her mother hung her coat on the hook and stood looking at the empty place by the stove for a long moment. Then she pulled out a chair that was still there and sat down.

"Say something," she said. "Anything. I can't stand the quiet."

"The clock's gone," Sena said.

"I know." Her mother's voice was flat. "I counted them this morning. Eleven things. I could tell you every one."

"Then let's not count anymore," Sena said. She pulled the other chair around and sat facing her. "Let's talk instead. About something that isn't him."

Her mother looked at her sharply, and then, slowly, her face changed. "All right," she said. "The roof needs mending in the spring. I've been meaning to tell you. The beam over your room is soft."

"I know," Sena said. "I can hear it creak when the wind's from the east."

"Then you can help me fix it." Her mother reached across the table and took her hand. "We'll need a ladder, and nails, and a good deal of stubbornness."

"We've got stubbornness," Sena said.

Her mother laughed, a short, surprised sound, as if she had forgotten she could. The candle on the table guttered, and Sena got up to trim the wick. Outside, the storm was easing; the wind had dropped to a low mutter, and the snow was coming down straight now, soft and ordinary.

"Tell me about the boat," Sena said, sitting back down. "Not the last day. The first one. The one he built."

And her mother began to talk, and the house, smaller and quieter and more theirs than it had ever been, filled slowly with the sound of a voice that was alive.
