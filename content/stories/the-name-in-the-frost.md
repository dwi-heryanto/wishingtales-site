The lamp was on at six, as always. Otis set the ledger on the sill and opened it to the last page. The wind came up the hill in long pushes, like someone breathing on the glass. He wrote: Wind west, gusting. Rain by nine. The pen scratched. The house settled.

Then he looked at the line above his. It was not his.

*The hill is listening.*

Otis put the pen down. He had asked the ledger, three nights ago, what he should call the hand that answered him. He had written the question small, at the bottom of the page, and closed the book. Now here was the answer, and it was not a name at all.

"Right," he said, to the room. "That's not what I asked."

The hand did not write again. Outside, the rain came exactly when he had said it would.

He picked up the pen. Under the line, he wrote: *What should I call you, then?*

The page stayed white. The lamp hummed. Otis waited until his tea went cold, then wrote his evening entry anyway, honest and plain: Rain, heavy at nine, easing by eleven. Frost likely. He underlined frost. Then he turned off the lamp and went to bed.

***

Morning came white and quiet. Otis was up before the kettle because the light through the curtain was wrong, too bright and too still. He pulled the curtain back.

The hill was frost. That was normal for October. What was not normal was the pattern. Frost lay in long, even lines running up the slope, and between them the grass showed dark. It looked like writing on a page.

Mira arrived at half past seven with the post and her bicycle. She leaned it against the wall and looked up at the hill with her chin lifted.

"That's frost," she said.

"It's writing," said Otis.

"It's frost, Otis. It does that. It goes where the water goes." She stamped her boots on the step. "You've been up half the night again."

"I asked it what to call it," he said. "And it gave me a line about the hill listening. Now the hill's got letters on it."

Mira folded her arms. "Letters are in books. That's a field with cold grass on it."

"Come up and look."

She looked at the hill. Then she looked at him. "I've got post," she said. "And I'm not carrying your notebook."

But she didn't get back on the bike. She stood there, and the morning got quieter, and the frost stayed exactly where it was, too even to be chance.

"After the post," she said. "And I'm still not carrying your notebook."

Otis went up the hill at nine, when Mira had finished her round. She caught him at the gate and took the notebook out of his hands without asking. "I'll carry it," she said. "You'll drop it in a ditch." The frost began at the cattle grid and ran uphill in long straight lines, like a person ruling a page. They walked up the wet side, where the grass showed dark, and did not speak. Near the top the lines bent and crossed. Otis stopped. "There," he said. "Look at that." The frost had made four letters, tall as a door, white on the green. They spelled a word: HERE. Mira crouched and put her glove on the bottom of the H. It came away white. "It's frost," she said, but she said it quietly. "It's a word, though," said Otis. "It's a word that means nothing," she said. "Here is where you're standing. Here is everywhere." He took the notebook back and drew the letters, and the shape of them, and where the lines bent. His pencil shook a little, so he drew slowly. "It's not a name," he said. "I asked it what to call it, and it wrote Here." Mira stood up and looked down at the seven houses. "Maybe it's not a name because it hasn't got one," she said. "Or maybe it's telling you where it lives." Otis wrote that down too, because it was the first useful thing she had said all morning.

***

That evening Otis opened the ledger at the new page and wrote the word in his best hand, in the question column, as neatly as he could. HERE. Then he sat with the pen and waited, and the lamp hummed, and his grandfather slept in the next room. Nothing happened for a long time. He was nearly asleep in the chair when he heard the small dry sound of a nib touching paper, and he sat up straight. Under his word, in the old-fashioned hand, a line had been written. It said: A word is not a name. A name is what you are called by someone else. I have been Here a long while, and nobody has called me anything. Otis read it three times. His first thought was that it was a riddle, and his second was that it was not. "Right," he said, out loud, to the empty kitchen. He dipped the pen and wrote beneath it: So what do I call you? The nib moved at once, quick and sure. Call me what your grandfather called me. Ask him. Otis looked at the door to the next room. The lamp ticked. Outside, the frost was still lying in its letters on the hill, and the hill was not a field any more, and he was not sure when that had happened.

Otis pushed the door open with two fingers. The room smelled of blankets and the lavender Grandfather kept in a tin by the bed. A slice of moonlight lay across the floor. Grandfather was awake. His eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling, and he was smiling at something Otis could not see.

"You're up late," Grandfather said, without turning his head.

"I was writing the ledger," Otis said. "The weather."

"Ah." Grandfather's voice was dry as a seed. "The weather. It's been asking after you."

Otis went very still. "What do you mean?"

"It used to ask me things too. When I was your age." Grandfather turned his head then. His eyes were bright and clear, the way they sometimes were. "Did it give you a word?"

"A word," Otis said. "Yes. It said to ask you what you called it."

Grandfather laughed, a small, papery sound. "I called it the Neighbour. Because it was always there, over the hill, and never came in. Your great-grandmother called it the Hill. But I liked Neighbour better." He closed his eyes. "It's a good name. It means you don't have to be afraid of it."

"I'm not afraid," Otis said.

"No," Grandfather agreed. "You're careful. That's different." He was already drifting. "Write it down, boy. The name. It'll like that."

The next morning the frost was gone, melted to a grey damp, and the hill looked ordinary again. Otis did not write the name in the ledger until evening. He waited until the lamp was lit and the entry was done. Wind from the west, he wrote, and a hard frost before dawn. Then, underneath, in his smallest, neatest hand: Hello, Neighbour.

He watched the page. Nothing moved. The lamp ticked. He thought perhaps that was answer enough, and he was not disappointed. A name given was its own kind of answer.

He set the pen down and turned the lamp low. Outside, the hill stood black against the last of the light, and the sky above it was full of early stars. Otis looked at it for a long moment. It did not look like a field or a slope or a thing on a map. It looked like a neighbour, standing at the edge of the garden, patient and quiet, waiting to be spoken to again.

"Goodnight," he said, to the window. Then he went to bed, and slept without dreaming, and the ledger lay open on the table with its new name in it, drying in the dark.
