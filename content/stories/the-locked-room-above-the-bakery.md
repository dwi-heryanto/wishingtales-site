The bakery smelled of yeast and old smoke, even now, three weeks after the ovens had gone cold. Nell carried the last box of pans up the narrow stairs, her arms aching, while her mother called out from the shop floor below.

"Put them in the back room, love. Not the front one. Not yet."

The front room upstairs had been her grandmother's. Nell had never been inside it. As a child she'd been told it was full of sharp things and dusty air, and later, when she was old enough to be curious, Gran had simply said, "That door stays shut." Now Gran was gone, and the door was still shut, but the whole building belonged to them.

Nell set the box down and looked at the door. It was a plain thing, painted the same dull green as the walls. But the lock was wrong. It was bright and new, a heavy brass deadbolt screwed into the wood with fresh screws, their heads still sharp. The door itself was old, its panels warped and its handle loose. Someone had put a new lock on an old door, and not long ago.

Her mother came up the stairs, wiping her hands on her apron. "What is it?"

"The lock. It's newer than the door."

Her mother glanced at it and then away, too quickly. "Your gran was careful. She liked things locked."

"It's a deadbolt, Mum. You don't put a deadbolt on a storeroom."

"Leave it, Nell." Her mother's voice had a flat edge. "We've got enough to do without digging through old junk."

But that night, lying in her old bed with the window open to the warm street, Nell thought about the lock. A new lock meant someone had wanted to keep people out. Or keep something in. And Gran had never once, in all the years, mentioned a man named Hollis.

***

The lock gave with a soft click. Nell had practised on the shed padlock for two days, using a bent hairpin and a strip of metal from a broken ruler. It wasn't a skill she was proud of, but it was a skill.

The room beyond was smaller than she'd imagined. Dust lay thick on the floor, and the air was cold and still. A single window, painted shut, let in a grey light. Against one wall stood a wooden desk, and on the desk, a ledger bound in green cloth.

Nell opened it. The pages were covered in her grandmother's handwriting, neat columns of names and numbers. Mrs. Okafor, three pounds. Mr. Bell, two pounds ten. The list went on, page after page, each entry marked paid in the same firm hand. At the back, tucked into the fold, was a photograph.

It showed three people outside the bakery. Her grandmother, young and unsmiling. A man with a wide face and a hat. And between them, a second man, his features scratched away with something sharp, leaving only a pale hole in the paper.

Nell's hands were shaking. She took the ledger and the photograph downstairs.

Her mother was in the kitchen, staring at the cold oven. When she saw what Nell held, her face went white.

"Where did you get that?"

"The room. The locked room." Nell put the ledger on the table. "Who's Hollis?"

Her mother sat down slowly. "A man who used to come round. Before you were born. He had a way about him. People paid him to keep trouble away."

"Paid him? For what?"

"For nothing. For the idea of him." She touched the scratched photograph. "Then one night he was gone. No one asked where."

"And Gran?"

"Your gran was one of the ones who paid." Her mother's voice was barely a whisper. "And she never said another word about him. Not one."

The ledger was a small thing, bound in cracked brown leather, the pages thin as onion skin. Nell opened it on the kitchen table under the lamp. Her mother had gone to lie down, and the bakery was quiet except for the tick of the oven cooling. Each page held a name in her grandmother's careful hand, a date, and a sum. Mrs. Ardley, three pounds. The Coopers, two pounds ten. Mr. Fenn, four pounds. On and on, a dozen names, all from the street, all paid monthly to someone called H. Hollis. Nell knew most of them. Mrs. Ardley ran the post office and gave out free sweets to children. Mr. Fenn mended shoes and never charged for a stitch on a school bag. They were not the kind of people who paid for protection.

She took the ledger to Mrs. Ardley the next morning, before the shop opened. The post office smelled of paper and dust. Mrs. Ardley was behind the counter, sorting letters into pigeonholes, and when Nell put the ledger down and opened it to her name, the old woman's hands went still.

"Where did you find that?" she said.

"Upstairs. In the locked room."

Mrs. Ardley looked at the page for a long time. "Your gran kept the book," she said at last. "She was the one who collected. We all paid her, and she passed it on."

"Passed it to Hollis?"

"To Hollis." Mrs. Ardley straightened a stack of envelopes. "He was a big man with a soft voice. He came round every month and asked after your family, and you gave him what you could, and nothing bad happened. That was the arrangement."

"Nothing bad like what?"

"Like a window broken. Like a dog going missing. Like a letter from the council about the roof." She met Nell's eyes. "It sounds foolish now. It sounded sensible then."

Nell walked to the shoe mender's next. Mr. Fenn was bent over a boot, his hands black with polish, and he did not look up when she came in. She told him what she had found, and he set the boot down.

"Your gran was a good woman," he said. "She paid her share same as the rest of us. More, some months. She said it was the price of a quiet street."

"And was it?"

"It was quiet," Mr. Fenn said. "Until it wasn't." He picked up the boot again. "Ask Pell across the road. He knows more than he lets on."

***

Mr. Pell's bakery stood directly opposite theirs, and it had stood there longer, and it did not want for customers. Nell found him on the pavement at closing time, sweeping flour dust into the gutter. He was a thin man with grey stubble and a way of looking at you side-on, as if he were weighing something.

"You've been asking questions," he said, before she had said a word.

"I found a ledger. It has your street on every page."

Pell's broom stopped. "That book belongs to the past," he said. "Leave it there."

"A man took payments from my grandmother. I want to know who he was."

"Hollis was nobody." Pell said it too quickly. "A man who came and went. If you've any sense you'll put the book back where you found it and lock the door."

"Why does it matter to you?"

He looked at her properly then, and for a moment his face was not hard at all. It was tired. "Because I knew him," he said. "And because some doors, once you open them, don't close." He turned back to his sweeping. "Go home, Nell. Mind your ovens."

She went home, but she did not sleep well. The house made its night noises: the pipes knocking, the sign creaking on its bracket. Near two in the morning she heard something that did not belong, a soft scrape from the shop below, then the click of the back door. She lay still, her heart going, and heard footsteps on the stairs. They went past her room to the landing, and then to the locked room, and the new lock turned with a sound like a tooth coming loose.

She did not go out. She lay under the blanket with her eyes open until the footsteps came back down and the back door closed, and the street was silent again.

In the morning the locked room stood open. The shelf was empty. The ledger was gone, and so was the photograph, and on the floor where they had been lay a single dusting of flour, pale against the boards.

Nell did not go to school. She stood in the shop with her apron still untied, staring at the empty shelf in the upstairs room until the morning light moved across it. Then she took the ledger's shape out of her memory—the cracked spine, the names—and walked across the street to Pell's bakery.

The shop was warm and smelled of caraway. Pell was pulling a tray of rolls from the oven, his sleeves rolled past his elbows. He did not look surprised to see her.

"You're early," he said. "Or late. Depends what you came for."

"Someone came into our house last night," Nell said. "They took the ledger. They took the photograph."

Pell set the tray down. For a moment his face did something complicated, like a door opening and closing very fast. "Sit," he said, and pushed a stool toward her with his foot.

She did not sit. "You knew about the room. You knew about Hollis. You told me to leave it alone, and then someone came in the night and took it. So tell me now."

Pell wiped his hands on a cloth, slowly, as if the cloth were the only thing in the world. "Hollis was my brother," he said. "Younger. He had a way with people—made them feel safe, made them feel seen. Then he made them pay for it. Protection, he called it. A few shillings a month and nothing bad would happen to your shop. But bad things had a way of happening to the ones who didn't pay."

Nell felt the floor steady under her. "My grandmother paid him."

"She did. For a year. Then she stopped." Pell looked up. "The night of the fire on Carrow Street—you've heard of it? Three shops burned. Hollis set it. Not on purpose, maybe, but he set it, and a man died in the smoke. After that, Hollis came to your grandmother. She hid him. Got him out of the city, gave him a new name, and never told a soul. Not even your mother."

"Why would she help him?"

"Because she was the only one who could," Pell said. "And because she knew if she didn't, he'd hang. She wasn't a soft woman, your grandmother. But she was a fair one. She thought he deserved a chance to be someone else."

Nell thought of the ledger, the neat column of names, her grandmother's own among them. "The ledger—"

"I burned my own copy years ago," Pell said. "But I couldn't burn hers. She kept it as a kind of promise. Proof of what he'd done, in case he ever came back and tried to be Hollis again." He paused. "I didn't take it, Nell. I swear that on the ovens. But I know who did. His son. My nephew. He's been asking around for months, trying to find his father's old papers. He thinks there's money in them."

***

Nell went back to the bakery and stood in the flour room. The bins were old, wooden, scarred with decades of scoops. She lifted the lid of the third one, the one her grandmother had always kept half full, and dug her hand into the soft white drift.

Her fingers found the ledger's edge.

She pulled it out, dusted it off, and opened it on the counter. The names were all there, the small debts, the careful sums. But tucked into the back cover, where the pages had been cut to make a pocket, was a folded sheet in her grandmother's hand. Nell read it twice.

It was a final entry, dated the week before her grandmother died. "Hollis is gone," it said. "Truly gone this time. I paid for his passage north and he will not come back. The ledger is the only record of what he did. If I am gone and someone finds this, give it to the one who has the right to judge. Not the police. The brother."

Nell stood very still. The flour dust settled around her.

She thought about the police, about the neat official way they would take the ledger and the photograph and the story, and turn it into something that belonged to them. She thought about Pell, who had swept his step and warned her away and lied to protect a brother who had done terrible things. She thought about her grandmother, who had hidden a man and kept a promise and left the choice in a flour bin.

She carried the ledger across the street. Pell was closing up, the shutters half drawn.

"I found it," she said. "It was in the flour."

Pell's face went still. "And?"

"It says to give it to you." She held it out. "So I am."

He took it with both hands, the way you take something that might break. "You could have taken it to the police."

"I could have," Nell said. "But she didn't want that. And I think she was right."

Pell nodded slowly. He opened the ledger, looked at the last page, and closed it again. "Thank you," he said. "I'll keep it. And I'll keep the door locked."

Nell walked back across the street. The bakery sign creaked on its bracket, and the ovens were still warm, and the house above was quiet. She locked the back door behind her, and this time the lock turned clean.
