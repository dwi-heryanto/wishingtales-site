The lamp had to be lit before dark, and it was lit. Nell stood on the iron step with the matchbox in her fist and watched the wick catch, a small orange seed opening into a flame. She held her breath until the flame settled. Then she turned the little wheel and the whole room moved, faint and slow, like a held breath.

"Supper," Tessa called up the stairs. "It's only bread and the end of the soup. Do not complain."

"I never complain," Nell said, coming down. The kitchen was warm and small. Steam sat on the window. Their father's boots were gone from the mat, which meant a boat had come in and taken him off to the pier.

After they ate, Nell went to the hook by the door for the brass key. It was the key that opened the lamp's spring housing, so the weight could be wound back up in the night. Her fingers found the nail. They found the wall. They found nothing.

"Tessa." Nell's voice came out flat. "The key isn't here."

Tessa looked over from the sink with a plate in her hand. "It's on the hook."

"It's not." Nell held up her empty palm. "Feel the nail. Nothing."

Tessa set the plate down and felt the nail herself, twice. "Dad had it today. He oiled the gears."

"He always puts it back on the hook."

"Then it fell," Tessa said, and shrugged. "Things fall."

***

Nell lit the small lantern and set it on the table between them. Its light made the kitchen smaller and the corners darker.

"We have to find it tonight," she said.

Tessa shook her head. "The light is already burning. It will burn till morning on its own. Dad says that."

"Dad says it'll burn most of the night. He doesn't say till morning." Nell pulled her notebook out and found the page, and turned it round so Tessa could see the numbers. "The spring runs down after midnight. Then the weight sits at the bottom and the light goes small."

"Small isn't out."

"On a black night, small is out," Nell said. "That's what it means."

Tessa crossed her arms. "You want to go out in the dark and poke about the harbour for a key the size of your thumb."

"I want to wind the lamp before dawn," Nell said. "That's all I want."

"Then wait for Dad."

"Dad's on the pier with a boat and he'll be gone an hour. The wind is getting up." Nell pulled on her coat. It was too big at the shoulders and smelled of old rope.

"Nell."

"I'll retrace my steps. The stairs, the lamp room, then down to the pier." She took the lantern by its handle. "You can stay. Someone has to stay with the light."

Tessa said nothing at all. The door banged behind Nell, and the cold came in where she went.

The lamp room was warm and smelled of oil and hot brass. Nell set her lantern on the floor and searched with both hands. She checked the shelf where the key belonged, though she had already checked it. She felt along the sill, under the logbook, inside the empty tea tin. Only dust and a dead moth.

"Key," she said to the room. "Key, key, key."

The brass was cold when she touched the winding mechanism. The spring was still tight from the evening wind, but by three in the morning it would be slack. She knew the numbers in her notebook. She did not need to look.

On the stairs, the lantern threw her shadow up the wall, huge and stooping. She went down slowly, feeling each step. The stairwell was narrow and the wind sang in the vent. Halfway down, her boot slid on something small. She picked it up. A button, bone-coloured, with two holes. Not hers. Not Tessa's.

"Who drops a button on a stair?" she asked the dark.

The dark did not answer. She put the button in her pocket and kept going. At the bottom, the door to the outside banged once, then settled. Nell's breath came short. The night was getting deeper, and the key was not in the tower.

***

The pier was a black line with white water beyond it. Nell held her lantern low so the wind would not take it. Boats knocked together and ropes creaked. Halfway along, a shape straightened up from a coil of net.

"Who's that?" said Mr Caddy.

"Nell Avery."

"You're out late." He came closer. His coat shone with wet. "Your dad's up the far end with the Mary Ellen. Engine trouble."

"I'm looking for the lamp key. It's gone from its hook."

Mr Caddy rubbed his chin. "Gone? You'll have left it in the boathouse. I saw a key on the bench there this afternoon. Brass, big."

Nell's heart lifted. "The boathouse?"

"Down past the slip. Door's not locked. Go careful, mind. The boards are wet."

She thanked him and started away. Then she stopped. "Was it on a leather loop?"

"Couldn't say. A key's a key." He turned back to his net. "You'll want to hurry. That wind's turning."

Nell ran, the lantern swinging. Behind her, Mr Caddy called something about the tide, but the wind took it.

The boathouse loomed at the end of the pier, black against black. The door stood ajar. She pushed it open and went in.

The boathouse smelled of tar and old rope. Nell held the lantern high. Light ran along a bench, a tin bucket, a heap of net. No key.

She stepped further in. Her boot knocked something that rolled. She crouched. There, beside a coil of rope, lay the brass key. It was still on its leather loop. She picked it up and felt the weight of it in her palm.

"Got you," she said.

Outside, the wind had changed. It pushed at the door and shoved her sideways on the steps. The harbour was all noise now. Water slapped the pier. Somewhere a loose halyard rang against a mast.

Nell ran. The lantern threw her shadow long and jumping. At the top of the slip she stopped to catch her breath. The tower was a black shape against a sky full of moving cloud. The light was still burning. She could see it, small and steady, high above the headland.

"Not long now," she told it.

The path up the headland was steep and stony. Grass whipped at her ankles. Her side ached. She kept her eyes on the light and climbed.

Halfway up, the lantern guttered and nearly went out. She cupped her hand around it and kept going. The key was cold and solid in her fist.

At the tower door she leaned on the frame for a moment. Then she pushed it open and started up the stairs.

***

Tessa was at the top of the stairs. "Where have you been?" she said. Her voice was sharp. "I've been watching the light. It's been getting dimmer."

"I know," said Nell. She held up the key. "I found it. In the boathouse."

Tessa stared at it. "Mr Caddy was right, then."

"He was wrong about the loop. It was still on it." Nell went to the lamp. Her hands were shaking a little. She fitted the key to the winding square and turned it. The spring took up the slack with a soft click, click, click. The light steadied and grew strong again.

Tessa let out a breath. "I thought it would go out."

"So did I," said Nell.

For a moment neither of them spoke. The wind pressed at the glass. Then Tessa said, "I'm sorry I said we didn't need it. I was worried."

Nell looked at her. "I know."

Tessa filled the kettle. "Tea?"

"Yes," said Nell. She sat down on the bench by the window. Her boots were wet and her hands smelled of rope. The lamp burned on, steady and bright.

They drank their tea without saying much. Outside, the wind began to ease. By the time the sky turned grey, the light was still burning. Nell wound the lamp one last time as the sun came up. Then she put the key back on its hook and went to bed.
