The cave behind Milo's house had been there his whole life, and he had never once been inside it. His grandmother used to say it was a fox den and a snake den and a den for things with no names at all, and he had believed her until he was eleven. Now he was thirteen and the hill was going to be dug up for a new water line, and if he didn't look in the cave now, he never would.

He brought the small torch from the kitchen drawer and a cheese sandwich, because his grandmother also used to say that nobody ever regretted bringing a sandwich. The entrance was low and narrow, a mouth in the chalk. Milo got down on his hands and knees and went in sideways, and the torch beam swung over walls that were smoother than he expected, worn round by old water.

Inside, the cave opened up like a held breath. It was dry and cool and it smelled of dust and stone. Milo stood and swept the light around.

"Just a hole," he said out loud, to hear a voice in it. His voice came back flat and small.

Then the beam caught something at the back, on a shelf of rock that looked almost deliberately flat. A lamp. A squat brass thing, green with age, with a handle and a spout and a lid that didn't quite fit. Milo crouched and picked it up. It was heavier than it looked, and cold, and then it wasn't cold at all.

Light came out of it. Not a flame, not a glow from the spout, but a soft amber haze that spread from under his fingers and filled the air around his hand like heat you could see. Milo made a noise and nearly dropped it. The light didn't flicker. It sat there, steady, as if it had been waiting a long time to be touched.

"Okay," he said, very quietly. "Okay."

He turned it over. The bottom was scratched with marks he couldn't read, and the handle had worn to the shape of a hand. Not his hand. Somebody else's, a long time ago.

The light dimmed when he set it down and came back when he picked it up again. Milo stood in the cave for a long while, holding a lamp that liked him, and outside the hill the water line was coming.

***

At home, Milo put the lamp on his desk and shut his door. The light had gone out on the walk back, or he'd stopped noticing it, but now it woke again the moment his fingers closed on the handle. He sat and held it and thought about what to do.

Nothing happened for a while. Then the light gathered in the air above the spout, thickened, and made a picture.

It was the square in town, but not the square he knew. The cobbles were the same, and the war memorial was there, but the shops were different and there were horses tied at the rail outside the bakery. A girl stood in the middle of the road with her sleeves rolled up, laughing at something out of frame. Milo knew her face before he knew he knew it. The sharp chin, the eyebrows that went up at one corner, the way she stood with her weight on her back foot like she was about to be persuaded into trouble.

"Nan," he said.

The picture didn't hear him. The girl—his grandmother, fifteen or sixteen, younger than he was now—picked up a bucket and walked off down a street that Milo had walked a thousand times, and the light folded in on itself and went out.

He sat in the dark of his room with his heart going.

When he touched the lamp again, the light came up slower, thinner. Another picture. The same girl, older now, standing in the doorway of the house Milo lived in, holding a baby. His father. Behind her the kitchen was the same yellow his mother had painted it, which meant the paint had been there longer than anyone thought.

Milo set the lamp down carefully and found his hands were shaking. Every picture was a piece of the town. Not the town in books, not the dates on the memorial, but the actual light of it, the way the cobbles shone after rain, the way his grandmother laughed with her head back. It was all in there, kept, like jam in a jar.

He went to the window. Down the hill, the town was lighting up for evening. Somewhere in it was everyone he knew, walking streets that had been walked before, and the lamp on his desk held all of it, quietly, waiting for a hand that fit the handle.

"You kept us," Milo said to it. "All this time."

The lamp didn't answer. It didn't need to. He sat back down and held it and watched the town go dark, one window at a time, and the light under his fingers stayed warm as a held hand.

The next afternoon Milo brought Nell up to his room, the lamp wrapped in a tea towel so it wouldn't clink. He'd thought about it all morning — how to explain, whether to explain at all. Nell was the kind of friend who'd believe him or laugh at him, and either way she'd stay.

"Okay," she said, sitting on the floor with her back against his bed. "Show me the thing."

He unwrapped the lamp and set it on the rug between them. Brass, dull, ordinary. Nothing happened.

"That's a lamp," Nell said.

"Touch it." He pushed it toward her. She laid one finger on the spout and waited. The brass stayed cold and grey, and after a moment she took her hand back.

"What am I supposed to be feeling?"

"Nothing," Milo said. "Watch." He put his own hand on the handle, and the light came up at once, soft and gold, the way it had before. Nell leaned forward and the glow stuttered, like a candle in a draught, and dimmed to almost nothing.

"It's doing it," she breathed. "It's actually doing it."

"It was brighter a second ago." He took his hand away and the light died. He put it back and it came again, but weaker, and when Nell shuffled closer on her knees it faded until the brass looked like any old lamp in any old house.

Nell sat back. "Move away," Milo said. She did, and the light steadied. She came forward again, and it sank.

"It doesn't like me," she said, and there was something flat in her voice.

"It's not that." He turned the lamp in his hands. "It's like — it's shy. Or it's got a small voice and you're noise."

"Great. I'm noise." She pulled a face, but she was watching the lamp. "Try something. Make it show you something."

Milo closed his eyes and thought of his grandmother's kitchen, the peel of an apple coming off in one long curl. The light swelled under his palm, and for half a breath he saw it — the curl of peel, the old knife, the yellow wall — and then Nell said, "What? What is it?" and the picture broke like a bubble.

"Nothing," he said. "It only works for me." He said it lightly, but it sat in the room like a stone in a shoe. Nell got up and brushed off her knees.

"Well," she said. "At least you've got a secret now. Everyone should have one." She smiled, but it was the smile she used when she'd decided not to mind about something. At the door she stopped. "Does it work for your mum?"

"I haven't tried."

"Don't," she said. "Some things are yours." Then she went down the stairs, and Milo heard her say hello to his mother, and the front door, and the quiet after.

***

For three days Milo kept the lamp on his desk and did not touch it. He did his homework beside it, and ate toast beside it, and at night he lay in the dark and could feel it there, warm the way a cat is warm. On the fourth day he gave in.

He wanted the market. His grandmother had told him about the old market, the one that used to fill the square on Saturdays before the supermarket came, and he wanted to see it. So he put his hand on the handle and asked, and the light bloomed and showed him: stalls with striped awnings, a woman selling eggs out of a basket, a boy running with a paper cone of chips. It lasted longer this time, maybe ten seconds, and when it went out he sat blinking in the ordinary grey of his room.

That was when he noticed the lamp had changed. The glow, when he lifted his hand, was thinner. Paler. He turned it over and there was a hairline crack in the brass near the base, and when he shook it gently something inside shifted, dry as sand.

He tried it again the next morning, and the picture — his grandmother at the river with her shoes in her hand — came slower, and went sooner. The crack had widened. By evening he could see a faint grey line running up the spout like frost on a window.

"No," he said out loud. "No, no." He held the lamp to his ear. It was silent, but he could feel the light in it going out the way a bath goes cold, from the edges in.

He counted on his fingers. The first memory, the yellow paint, had been bright and long. Then the kitchen. Then the market. Then the river. Each one a little dimmer, each one shorter. It was spending itself. Every time he looked, he was using it up.

His mother called him down for dinner. At the table he pushed his food around and she asked if he was sick, and he said he was tired, and she said he'd been up in his room a lot lately. "You're not getting strange on me, are you?" she said, half joking.

"No," Milo said. "Just thinking."

Upstairs afterward, he sat on the edge of his bed with the lamp in his lap. It was still warm, but faintly now, like a cup of tea left too long. He thought of all the memories he hadn't seen yet — the square before the fountain, his grandmother's wedding, the flood, the fire, the first day of school for a hundred years of children. All of it in there, going out.

He put his hand on the handle. The light came up, weak and trembling, and showed him nothing at all.

Milo set the lamp on his desk and stared at it. The glow had shrunk to a coin of light no bigger than a thumbnail, and it pulsed like a slow heartbeat. He knew what it meant. The lamp had one more memory in it, maybe two, and then it would be dark for good. He could feel the weight of all the town's history pressing on him, the flood and the fire, the first day of school, the square before the fountain. All of it would be gone if he spent the last light on himself. But there was one memory he hadn't seen. His grandmother as a girl, standing in the kitchen with flour on her hands, laughing at something off-screen. He'd glimpsed it once, in the corner of another memory, and he hadn't been able to stop thinking about it.

"You're still up," his mother said from the doorway. She had a cup of tea in her hand and her hair was down. "I brought you this. You looked cold."

Milo took the cup. The warmth seeped into his fingers. "Thanks."

She sat on the edge of his bed, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Then she nodded at the lamp. "That's pretty. Where'd you get it?"

"Up on the hill," Milo said. "In a cave."

"A cave," she repeated, and smiled. "You're not getting strange on me, are you?" It was the same joke from dinner, but softer now.

"No," Milo said. "Just thinking."

She reached out and touched the lamp's brass rim. The light didn't change. "It's cold," she said. "I thought it was lit."

Milo's throat tightened. "It's just the metal."

She looked at him for a long moment, then stood. "Don't stay up too late," she said, and kissed the top of his head. When she was gone, Milo put his hand on the lamp. The light swelled, and he saw it: his grandmother, maybe fourteen, in a kitchen that smelled of cinnamon. She was laughing, her hair tied back with a ribbon, and someone off-screen was saying something that made her cover her mouth. Milo's chest ached. He wanted to stay there. He wanted to keep her.

But the light was already fading. He pulled his hand away and the memory winked out. The lamp was dark.

***

For a long time Milo sat in the quiet. The lamp was dark, and the room was dark, and somewhere downstairs his mother was washing dishes. He thought about the cave on the hill, the way the lamp had glowed when he first touched it. He thought about all the memories he'd seen and all the ones he hadn't. Then he thought about his grandmother, not as a girl in a memory, but as she was now: sitting on the porch with her knitting, calling him by the wrong name and laughing about it, teaching him to make pie crust. That was a memory too, and it wasn't in the lamp. It was in him.

He picked up the lamp and carried it downstairs. His mother was at the sink, her sleeves rolled up. "Mum," he said. "Can we go see Gran tomorrow?"

She turned, surprised. "Of course. Is everything all right?"

"Yeah," Milo said. "I just want to."

His mother dried her hands. "You know she might not remember you," she said gently. "Some days are better than others."

"I know," Milo said. "I'll remind her."

He set the lamp on the kitchen table, and the brass caught the light from the window. It was just a lamp now. But it had shown him something true: the past was a place you could visit, but you couldn't live there. Home was here, in the warm kitchen, with his mother and the dishes and the sound of the radio. Home was the living.

The next morning they drove to the care home. His grandmother was in a chair by the window, a blanket over her knees. She looked up when Milo came in, and for a second her eyes were blank. Then she smiled. "There you are," she said. "I was waiting for you."

Milo sat beside her and took her hand. "I brought you something," he said, and he told her about the lamp, and the cave, and the girl in the kitchen with flour on her hands. She didn't remember, but she listened, and she held his hand, and that was enough.

The lamp stayed on the kitchen table at home, dark and quiet. Sometimes Milo picked it up and felt its weight, and remembered the light. But he didn't need it anymore. He had the real thing.
