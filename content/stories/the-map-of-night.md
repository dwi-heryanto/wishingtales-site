The brass bell above the door gave a small, polite ring, and a girl stepped into the shop. She was about Juno's age, with a red raincoat dripping onto the floorboards and a look on her face like she had walked a long way to get here.

"I've lost my home," she said.

Oda looked up from the counter, where she was winding a ball of string. "Have you now."

"Yes," said the girl. "I'm Elara. And I know it sounds strange, because I still live in the same house. But it doesn't feel like home anymore. It hasn't for a while."

Juno set down her pencil. She had been drawing a map of the bakery's back garden, mostly to practise the fold lines.

"You still live there," Juno said, "but you've lost it."

"That's what I said." Elara pulled a damp strand of hair from her cheek. "My dad used to tell me stories at night. About stars, mostly. He'd point out the window and make up names for the shapes. Then he got a new job, and he works nights now, and the stories stopped. The house is the same. The room is the same. But it's just a room."

Oda set down the string. "And you want a map."

"I want to find my way back," Elara said. "To how it felt."

The shop was quiet, except for the rain on the window. Juno watched her grandmother, waiting for the usual question that wasn't the question.

Oda reached under the counter and brought out a sheet of brown paper. She laid it flat. "Then we'd better draw one."

***

Oda sharpened the black pencil with a small knife, turning it slowly, letting the shavings curl onto a saucer. Juno stood beside her, close enough to see the grain of the paper.

"The old house," Oda said. "The one before this one. Where Elara's father grew up. That's where she should go."

Juno frowned. "She said she still lives in the same house."

"People lose more than one home," Oda said. "The map should show the way to the first one. It's in the next town. A bus ride and a short walk. We can draw the route."

Elara sat on the stool by the window, watching the rain. "I've never been there," she said. "Dad talked about it, but we never went."

"Then it's a good place to start," Oda said.

Juno picked up the black pencil. She held it over the paper, but she didn't draw a route. She drew a small circle in the middle, and around it, nothing.

"What are you doing?" Oda asked.

"The old house isn't what she lost," Juno said. "She lost the stories. The night. The way her dad made the ceiling into a sky."

"The old house might bring that back," Oda said.

"It might," Juno said. "Or it might just be an empty house in another town."

Elara turned from the window. "I don't want to go to the old house," she said. "I want to go home. To my room. But I want it to feel right."

Oda was quiet for a moment. Then she set the knife down. "Then draw what she misses," she said. "Not what she asked for."

Juno looked at the blank paper. She thought about night, and stories, and a ceiling that was a sky. She reached for the red pencil, the one for what is not there.

"What are you going to draw?" Elara asked.

Juno smiled, just a little. "A roof made of stars."

Juno picked up the red pencil. It was short and blunt, worn down from years of use. She turned the paper so the long edge faced her.

"Red is for what is not there," she said, more to herself than to Elara. "So I'll draw the things you miss. The things the house used to hold."

Elara leaned in. "Like what?"

"Like your dad's voice at bedtime," Juno said. "And the way the ceiling looked when you lay on your back."

She drew a line first, a long curve that started at the bottom left and swept up and over, like the ridge of a roof. Then another line, meeting it at the top. A triangle, but open at the bottom. A roof without walls.

"That's the shape of the night sky," Juno said. "The part you can see from your window."

"It's just a triangle," Elara said.

"Wait." Juno switched to the black pencil. She drew small dots along the roof lines. Then she connected them with faint lines, careful and slow. Seven dots on the left slope became a bent leg and a knee. Five dots on the right became a shoulder and an arm. At the peak, three dots made a head.

"That's a person," Elara whispered.

"That's a constellation," Juno said. "I don't know its real name. But it's a person lying down, looking up."

She added more dots below the roof, a scatter that became a door. Two dots for a handle, one for a hinge. The door stood open.

"The door goes into the sky," Juno said. "Because that's where your stories were. Not in the house. In the dark, above it."

Elara traced the door with her finger. "I remember the stories," she said. "But I can't hear them anymore. Not in my head."

"Then we'll go find them," Juno said. "Tonight."

***

The hill behind the shop was not tall, but it was the highest thing on Corvid Lane. Juno had walked it many times, usually alone, usually at dusk. Tonight Elara walked beside her, hands in her coat pockets, breath making small clouds.

"I don't see any stars yet," Elara said.

"They come out slowly," Juno said. "Like they're checking if it's safe."

They climbed until the bakery chimney was below them and the bus stop was a small blue light. Juno spread her jacket on the grass. "Sit. We have to wait for full dark."

Elara sat. She pulled her knees up. "What if I don't remember?"

"Then you don't," Juno said. "But you will. The map says so."

"The map is paper."

"The map is a promise," Juno said. "My grandmother says that. She says a map is just a way of agreeing to look."

They waited. The sky turned from blue to grey to a deep, soft black. One star appeared, then another, then a dozen. Juno lay back on the grass and pointed.

"There," she said. "The bent leg. See it?"

Elara lay down too. Her hair spread out on the jacket. "I see it," she said. "And the arm. And the head."

"That's your roof," Juno said. "Now find the door."

Elara was quiet for a long time. Then she pointed at a cluster of faint stars near the horizon. "There," she said. "The open door."

Juno smiled in the dark. "What's behind it?"

Elara didn't answer. But Juno heard her breathing change, slow and even, like someone listening to a story.

Elara lay still for a long moment. Then she breathed out, a long slow breath that seemed to come from somewhere deep.

"My father used to tell me stories," she said. "Out on the back step, when the kitchen was too hot and the day was too loud. He'd point up and say, 'That's the roof, Els. That's the door. That's the chimney.' Just like you did."

Juno didn't move. "What was behind the door?"

"A dog," Elara said. "A big shaggy dog that could talk. And a table with too many chairs. And a grandmother who baked stars."

She laughed, a small, wet sound. "I haven't thought about that in years. I forgot he made it up. I thought it was real."

"It was real," Juno said. "You just had it in the wrong place."

Elara turned her head. "What do you mean?"

"You said you lost your home. But your home is a house on Alder Street. You still live there. What you lost was this. The night. The stories. The roof he made out of stars."

Elara was quiet. Then she sat up, slowly, and looked at the sky again. "He stopped telling them," she said. "When I was ten. He said I was too old. I think he was just tired."

"Maybe," Juno said. "Or maybe he forgot how. Grown-ups do that."

Elara pointed at the open door of stars. "I can still see it," she said. "The door. The roof. All of it. It's like he's sitting right there."

"He is," Juno said. "In a way. That's what a map does. It keeps the way open."

They stayed on the hill until the stars turned pale and the air grew cold. Elara didn't speak again, but she wasn't sad. Juno could tell. Her shoulders had dropped. Her hands were loose in her lap. When they finally stood, Elara folded the jacket and handed it back.

"Thank you," she said. "I didn't know that was what I was looking for."

***

The shop was dark when they got back. The brass bell over the door gave a soft, sleepy ring. Oda was at the counter, sorting a tin of buttons by size.

"Find it?" she asked, without looking up.

"Yes," Elara said. "I found it."

Oda nodded. "Good. The walk home is short. Mind the bakery step. It's loose."

Elara smiled. "I know. It's always loose." She paused at the door. "Goodnight, Juno. Goodnight, Oda."

"Goodnight," they said together.

The bell rang once more, and she was gone.

Juno slid onto the stool behind the counter. Her legs ached from the hill. The brown paper map lay on the wood, creased and smudged. She ran a finger along the red line she had drawn for the missing roof.

"You used the black pencil," Oda said. "And the red. You didn't need the blue."

"I thought about it," Juno said. "For the door. I wanted to make it true."

"The door was true," Oda said. "You just had to let her find it."

Juno looked at the drawer under the counter. The blue pencil was in there, wrapped in a square of cloth. She had never seen it used. She wasn't allowed.

"When do I get to use it?" she asked.

Oda closed the button tin with a click. "When you can draw a map that tells someone what they already know."

Juno frowned. "That doesn't make sense."

"No," Oda said. "Not yet." She took the black pencil and the red pencil and laid them in the tin, side by side. Juno added the brown paper map, folded twice.

The shop was quiet. Outside, Corvid Lane was dark except for the bakery light, still on. Juno thought about the stars, and the roof, and the door. She thought about Elara's father, who had made it all up and then forgotten.

She put the tin on the shelf. The blue pencil stayed where it was, in its drawer, waiting.
