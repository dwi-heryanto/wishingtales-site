Ivy first heard the piano on a Tuesday, just as the streetlights blinked on. She was in bed with a book, and the sound slipped through her window like a thread of cool air. It was a slow, tumbling tune, the kind her grandmother used to play.

She sat up. The house next door had been empty for months. Its windows were dark, its garden full of tall weeds. Nobody had lived there since old Mr. Penrose moved away.

Ivy tiptoed to the window and pressed her nose to the glass. A soft yellow light glowed in the downstairs room. Through the curtains she saw the shape of a piano, and a small figure sitting at it, hands flying over the keys.

Then the light flickered and went out. The music stopped.

"Mum?" Ivy called. "Did you hear that?"

Her mother's voice came from the kitchen. "Hear what, love?"

"The piano. Next door."

A pause. "Next door's empty, Ivy. You're dreaming."

But Ivy wasn't dreaming. She lay awake for an hour, listening to the silence. The tune kept playing in her head, looping over and over. It felt familiar, like a word on the tip of her tongue.

At midnight she got up again. The light was back, and so was the music. This time she saw the figure clearly: a boy about her age, in a stiff white collar and short trousers. He looked up, straight at her window, and then he was gone.

***

Ivy pulled on her cardigan and crept down the stairs. The front door clicked open, and the night air smelled of wet leaves and cold stone. She crossed the path to the empty house and tried the handle. It turned.

The hallway was dark and smelled of dust and old wood. Moonlight lay in stripes across the floor. From the front room came the piano, playing the same tumbling tune, but slower now, as if it were tired.

She pushed the door open. The room was empty except for a grand piano in the middle, its lid propped up. Dust lay thick on the keys, but they were moving, pressing down by themselves. No hands. Just keys dancing up and down.

Ivy's heart hammered. She wanted to run, but her feet stayed put. The tune slowed, then stopped. One last note hung in the air.

A voice came from the piano stool. "You can see me, can't you?"

Ivy spun around. The boy was sitting there, pale and thin, his hands folded in his lap. He wasn't see-through exactly, but the window frame showed faintly through his shoulders.

"I... yes," Ivy whispered. "Who are you?"

"I'm Thomas," he said. "I've been trying to finish my song for a very long time."

Thomas swung his legs off the stool. His feet made no sound when they touched the floor. "I've been here since I was ten," he said. "I was learning this song. My teacher said I had the hands for it. Then I got sick, and I never finished."

Ivy took a step closer. The moonlight didn't fall on him the way it should. It went through him and landed on the wall behind. "How long have you been waiting?" she asked.

"I don't know," Thomas said. He looked at the piano keys. "A long time. I can play most of it. The beginning, the middle. But the ending won't come out. The notes slip away before I can reach them."

"Show me," Ivy said. Her voice came out braver than she felt.

Thomas turned to the piano and set his fingers on the keys. They sank down, and a soft, slow tune began. It climbed like a staircase, then stopped halfway up. One note hung, wobbled, and vanished into the dust.

"There," Thomas said. "That's where it goes."

"Try again," Ivy said.

He did. The same climb, the same stop. The final note thinned out and was gone, like breath on a window. Thomas pressed his hands into his lap. "Every time," he said quietly. "It just goes."

***

Ivy sat down on the bench beside him. The wood was cold through her pajamas. "Maybe you're playing it wrong," she said.

"I've tried everything," Thomas said.

"Then I'll help." Ivy looked at the keys. "What note comes after the last one you can play?"

Thomas hummed it. It was a small, bright note, like a bell. Ivy found a key that matched and pressed it. The note rang out, clear and true. For a moment it stayed. Then it faded, thinner and thinner, until there was nothing left but a tick of dust settling.

"That was it," Thomas said, and his voice cracked. "That was the right one. But it won't stay."

They tried again. And again. Ivy pressed the key; Thomas hummed; the note vanished. Each time it left a little ache behind Ivy's ribs, like a word on the tip of her tongue.

"It's not the piano," she said at last. "It's not your hands. The note wants something."

Thomas looked at her. "What could a note want?"

Ivy thought of her grandmother's house. She thought of the little piano in the sunny front room, and the way her grandmother's hands had found the keys without looking. Something warm flickered in her chest.

"I think I know," she said. "But we have to try something strange."

Ivy pulled the stool close to the old piano and sat down. Thomas floated beside her, dim as a candle at noon.

"When I press a key," she said, "you have to think of something you love. Something warm. Hold it while the note rings."

"I don't remember anything warm," Thomas said.

"Then borrow mine." Ivy set her fingers on the keys. "Think of a kitchen. Yellow curtains. My grandmother at her piano, playing while the bread went brown."

She pressed the key. Thomas hummed. This time the note held a heartbeat longer before it thinned away, and in the fading Ivy heard something else — a small laugh, an old woman's laugh, tucked inside the sound like a seed in a pear.

"Did you hear that?" Thomas whispered.

"I heard it." Ivy's eyes were wide. "That was my grandma. That's where the notes are going. They're hiding in my memories."

"In you?"

"In everything she taught me." Ivy pressed another key, and remembered the two of them playing chopsticks, elbows knocking. The note rang and stayed, humming softly, and a second note joined it from nowhere.

"Keep going," Thomas said. His voice had a shine to it now. "Play them all."

So Ivy played. She remembered her grandmother's humming while she dusted, and the note held. She remembered a rainy afternoon and a song about a boat, and the note held. Each memory she gave, the piano gave back a little more music, until the dusty room was full of it, and Thomas was glowing like a window at dusk.

***

"There's one left," Ivy said. Her fingers hovered over the last key.

Thomas drifted close. "What are you remembering?"

"The last day." Ivy's voice went small. "Grandma was tired. She couldn't play. She just held my hand on the keys and said the music would keep going, even when she couldn't."

"That's the one," Thomas said softly. "That's the note I couldn't find."

Ivy pressed the key. The note rose up bright and full, and Thomas hummed along, and the whole song came together at last — all the notes, all the memories, ringing like a room full of bells. The dust lifted off the piano and turned to sparkles. Thomas closed his eyes and smiled.

"Thank you," he said, and his voice was clear as the note. "I can finish it now."

He faded slowly, like breath off a window, and the last of him was a small warm laugh that sounded a little like her grandmother's. Then the house was just a house, and the piano was just a piano, and Ivy was alone with her heart going fast.

The walk home was short. The moon was out. Ivy climbed back through her window and lay in bed, and she did not feel afraid. In the morning she sat down at her own small piano and played the song from the start. It came out whole, every note of it, and her mother stopped in the doorway and said, "Where did you learn that?"

"From a friend," Ivy said, and kept playing.
