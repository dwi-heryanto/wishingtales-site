Mara stood on the lighthouse steps and watched the fog come in. It rolled over the water like a grey tide, swallowing the waves one by one. Her father climbed past her with the oil can, his boots loud on the iron stairs.

"Hand me the matchbox, love," he said. "This fog is thick as porridge."

Mara dug the matches from her coat pocket and passed them up. The lamp room smelled of oil and salt. Her father struck a match and touched it to the wick. The flame caught, small at first, then tall and yellow. It pushed the dark back into the corners.

"There," he said. "Now the boats can find us."

Mara pressed her nose to the glass. Below, the cliff path curved like a grey ribbon. Something moved on it. She squinted. A dog, thin and brown, trotting along the edge where the grass stopped and the drop began.

"Dad, there's a dog out there."

Her father leaned over her shoulder. "Poor thing. Probably lost. The fog will confuse it."

The dog stopped and looked up at the light. Its ears lifted. Then it sat down on the path, as if it had decided to wait.

"Can we go get it?" Mara asked.

"After the lamp is steady," her father said. "The boats come first. Always."

Mara nodded, but she kept watching the dog. It was still there, a small brown dot against the grey. The fog curled around it, but it did not move.

***

The fog grew thicker. It pressed against the lamp room glass like a wet hand. The light that had been so bright a minute ago now looked weak and small, a candle in a bucket.

Mara's father frowned at the lamp. "The generator's running rough," he said. "I need to check it. Stay inside."

"But the dog—"

"Stay inside, Mara. I mean it. The cliff is dangerous in this fog."

He pulled on his coat and clattered down the stairs. The door banged shut. Mara was alone with the lamp and the fog and the dark.

She counted to sixty. Then she counted again. The light flickered. Outside, the fog was so thick she could not see the cliff path at all. The dog was gone from sight.

A horn sounded, low and long, from somewhere out on the water. Another answered it. The fishing boats were coming home.

Mara pressed her hands to the glass. The lamp glowed, but the fog swallowed it whole. The boats would not see it. They would sail past the point and into the rocks.

She ran to the window on the seaward side. Nothing but grey. The horns sounded again, closer now, and frightened.

"Dad?" she called down the stairs. No answer. Only the wind.

Mara's heart thumped. She thought of the boats, and the men on them, and the rocks. Then she thought of the dog on the path, waiting in the fog.

She grabbed her coat.

Mara pushed open the heavy door and stepped into the fog. It pressed cold and wet against her face. She could not see the cliff edge, but she knew it was there. The horns sounded again, two of them, one high and one low, calling to each other across the water.

"They can't see the light," she whispered. "They're going to hit the rocks."

Her boots crunched on the path. She kept one hand on the lighthouse wall and felt her way toward the shed. The shed door was stuck. She pulled hard, and it opened with a groan. Inside, it was dark and smelled of old rope and salt. She felt along the shelf. A coil of rope. A can of paint. Then her fingers touched something cold and round. A bell. An old brass bell, heavy as a bucket of stones. It had a wooden handle and a rope tied to its top.

"A bell," she said. "If they can't see, maybe they can hear."

The dog appeared at the door. It was wet and shivering, but its tail gave a small wag. Mara grabbed the bell by its handle. It was too heavy to lift with one hand. She had to drag it. The bottom scraped along the floor. She pulled it out of the shed and onto the path.

"I need help," she told the dog. "Can you help me?"

The dog stepped forward and took the rope in its teeth. It pulled, and Mara pulled, and together they dragged the bell along the cliff path. The fog was so thick Mara could not see her own feet. She counted her steps. Ten. Twenty. The bell bumped over a rock. She stopped when she felt the ground grow softer, grassier. The cliff edge was close. She could hear the waves far below, hissing like a crowd.

"Here," she said. "This is far enough."

***

Mara set the bell down and picked up the rope. It was frayed and old. She wrapped the end around her hand and pulled. The bell swung. It made a dull clang, not loud enough. She pulled again, harder. The clang rang out across the water. The dog barked.

"Again," she said. She pulled and pulled. The bell rang, and rang, and rang. Each time it swung, the rope scraped against the wooden handle. Then, with a snap, the rope broke. The bell fell on its side. The clapper rolled out and disappeared into the fog.

"No!" Mara dropped to her knees. The boats' horns were closer now, and she could hear voices shouting. She felt around in the grass. Her fingers found the clapper. It was a small metal ball on a short chain. She could not ring the bell without the rope.

She looked up at the bell. Its top had a metal loop, the size of her two fists. The loop was attached to a wooden frame that stood as tall as her father. If she climbed the frame, she could reach the loop and swing the whole bell with her weight. It was high, but not too high.

"I can do it," she said. "I have to."

The dog whined and pushed its nose against her leg. Mara put her foot on a crossbar. The wood was wet and cold. She pulled herself up. The frame wobbled. She grabbed the top bar and held on. Below her, the dog circled and barked. The fog swirled around her knees.

"Okay," she said, breathless. "Okay."

The wood creaked under her hands. Mara pulled her chin up level with the top bar. The bell hung just below her, huge and dark. Its metal loop was right there, cold as a river stone.

"Stay," she told the dog. It sat in the grass and watched her.

She hooked one arm through the loop and leaned back. The bell did not move. She leaned harder. Nothing happened.

"Come on," she said. "Please."

She kicked her legs out and let her whole weight hang from the loop. The bell tilted. A low groan came out of the frame, and then the bell swung once and knocked against the wooden post.

Clang.

The dog barked at the sound. Mara pulled her knees up and swung again. Clang. The bell rocked back and forth, and each swing hit the post like a hammer on a nail.

Down in the fog she heard a horn answer. Long, then short. Then another horn, farther out.

"They heard!" Mara shouted. "They heard it!"

Her hands were slipping. She wrapped her legs around the frame and hugged the loop with both arms. Every time the bell struck, the whole frame shook. Her teeth rattled. Fog beaded on her face and dripped off her chin.

Then her foot slipped off the crossbar. She hung by her arms, swinging, and the ground looked very far down.

Below her, the dog jumped up and caught the hem of her coat in its teeth. It held on, growling, its paws braced in the wet grass. Mara got her foot back on the bar.

"Good dog," she gasped. "Good dog."

She swung the bell again. Clang, clang, clang. She did not stop. She kept her eyes on the loop and her arms tight, and she rang that bell until the horns were close and the shouting was close and the fog turned the colour of lamplight.

***

The first boat came out of the fog like a slow grey bird. Mara saw its bow, then its mast, then the men on the deck, all looking up at her.

"There she is!" a voice called. "On the frame!"

"Keep ringing, girl!" another voice called. "The others are behind us!"

Mara rang the bell three more times. Two more boats slid past below, their lanterns glowing in the mist. Men threw ropes onto the rocks and tied their boats to iron rings. Nobody was lost. Nobody was even hurt.

Her arms gave out. She slid down the frame and landed on the grass, and the dog was on her at once, licking her face.

"Stop," she laughed. "Stop, that tickles."

"Mara!"

Her father came running out of the fog with a lantern swinging in his hand. He fell to his knees beside her and caught her shoulders.

"Are you hurt?" he said. "Are you hurt?"

"No," she said. "I rang the bell. The boats heard."

He looked up at the bell, then at the boats, then at the dog standing over her like a guard. For a moment he said nothing at all. Then he pulled her into his coat.

"You rang the bell," he said into her hair.

"The rope snapped," she said. "So I climbed the frame. The dog helped me."

Her father looked at the dog. The dog looked back and thumped its tail on the ground.

"Then it had better come home," he said. "Both of you."

They walked up the path with the lantern between them. Behind them the bell hung still, and the boats sat safe in the harbour, and the fog began to lift, and the light on the tower turned and turned and turned.
