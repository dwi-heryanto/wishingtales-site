The river had never come up this fast. By midnight it was licking the bottom step of the ferryman’s house, and by the second hour after, it had swallowed the path entirely. Old Alder, the village elder, stood on the stone bridge with a lantern shaking in his hand. He was seventy years past his first crossing and had never seen the water so high.

“You must go,” Alder said, shouting over the roar. “One last crossing.”

The ferryman—whose name was Corin—stood beside him, rain running off his hood. “There’s no one waiting on the far bank,” he said. “I know every soul in this valley. They’re all on this side.”

“Not everyone.” Alder pointed to the far shore, where a single figure stood at the water’s edge. Grey cloak, motionless, as if the storm had no say over her. “She came down the hill an hour ago. Didn’t speak. Just stood there.”

“She can wait till morning.”

“The river will take the bridge by dawn,” Alder said. “And her with it. You’re the ferryman. You ferry.”

Corin looked at the water. It ran black and quick, carrying branches and the broken door of someone’s barn. He had crossed this river a thousand times, but never like this. His boat, the *Lark*, was tied to a post behind his house, bobbing like a cork.

“The *Lark* won’t survive that current,” he said.

“Then take the small skiff. The one you use for fishing.”

“That’s not a ferry.”

“Tonight it is.” Alder turned to him, and his old eyes were hard. “I’ve known you since you were a boy, Corin. You’ve never turned away a passenger. Don’t start now.”

Corin said nothing. Rain hammered the stones. Somewhere downriver a tree cracked and fell, and the sound rolled up the valley like thunder.

“What’s her name?” he asked.

“She didn’t give one.”

“Where’s she going?”

“She didn’t say.”

Corin pulled his hood tighter. “Then how do I know she’ll pay?”

Alder almost smiled. “You don’t. But you’ll take her anyway.”

He did. He untied the skiff from its mooring, and the little boat pitched hard as he stepped in. The oars were cold and slick. He rowed against the current, angling upstream to reach the far bank, and the river fought him every stroke. The grey woman stood waiting, and as he drew near she did not move or call out. She simply watched.

***

She was younger than he had expected. Not a girl, but not old either. Her face was calm in a way that made Corin uneasy, as if the flood were a thing she had already counted and dismissed. She wore a grey cloak that shed water like a duck’s back, and beneath it a plain dress the color of wet stone.

“You’re the ferryman,” she said. It was not a question.

“I am.” He held the skiff steady against the bank. “Where to?”

“The far side.”

“That’s where you’re standing now,” he said. “The far side is where I came from.”

She looked at him then, and her eyes were the same grey as the river. “I mean the other far side. Downstream. Past the bend.”

Corin shook his head. “There’s nothing past the bend but rocks and deep water.”

“I know.”

“You’ll drown.”

“I won’t.”

He should have refused. He knew that even then, with the rain coming sideways and the skiff already half full of water. But Alder’s words sat in him like a stone, and the woman’s stillness was worse than any storm. He held out his hand.

She took it. Her grip was cold, but not wet. Not even damp. He helped her into the skiff, and the little boat sank lower, as if she weighed far more than she looked.

“Sit in the middle,” he said. “Hold the gunwale. Don’t stand, whatever happens.”

“Whatever happens,” she repeated, and there was something almost like amusement in her voice.

Corin pushed off from the bank. The current caught them at once, sweeping the skiff sideways. He dug in with the oars, turning the bow downstream, and they shot out into the black. The river was everywhere—under them, around them, a living thing with a thousand hands. It pulled at the oars, at the hull, at the lantern hooked to the prow.

“You’ve done this before,” the woman said.

“Not in a flood.”

“But you’ve crossed in the dark.”

“Many times.” He grunted as an oar caught a submerged branch and nearly tore free. “Why do you ask?”

“I want to know what kind of man takes a stranger across a drowning river.”

“The kind who was told to.”

She was quiet for a moment. Rain dripped from the hood of her cloak. “And if no one had told you?”

Corin looked at her. The lantern light caught her face, and for a heartbeat he thought he saw something move beneath her skin—a current, a ripple, like water under ice. Then it was gone.

“I’d still have come,” he said. “It’s what I do.”

The river roared. The skiff climbed a swell and dropped hard, and the woman did not flinch. Corin rowed on, toward the bend, toward the dark.

The bend came up faster than it should have. Corin felt the river shift beneath the hull, a long slow suck like a breath drawn through teeth. The current had been pushing them downstream; now it pulled sideways, toward the outer bank where the water boiled over drowned willows. He leaned into the oars. The skiff groaned and slid sideways anyway.

“Hold on,” he said.

The woman gripped the gunwale. Her hands were pale, and the water streaming off her sleeves seemed to hesitate before it fell. Corin had no time to think about that. A wave stood up amidships, taller than the boat, and broke. The lantern tore free of its hook. It swung out over the black water on its rope, trailing sparks, and the river reached for it.

Corin had one oar shipped and one hand free. The lantern was the only light on the whole flooded valley. Without it they would be rowing blind into the bend, into the willows, into whatever the flood had torn loose and hidden. He could grab the rope. He could haul it back and let the wave take the woman, who sat still as a stone and made no sound at all.

“Take it,” she said. “The light. Take it.”

He did not.

He dropped the oar and lunged for her instead, caught her cloak in his fist, and dragged her down into the bottom of the boat as the wave came over them. Water filled the skiff to the thwarts. The lantern rope snapped. He heard the glass break somewhere out in the dark, a small bright sound swallowed whole.

For a moment there was nothing but cold and noise. Then the skiff rose, heavy and half-swamped, and Corin found the oar again by feel. He rowed. The woman lay in the water at his feet, and she was not breathing hard, and she was not shivering, and when she sat up her grey cloak was dry.

“You chose me,” she said.

“You were closer.”

“You could have saved the light.”

“A light’s no good to a drowned woman.”

She looked at him a long time. The river pulled at the oars, gentler now, almost curious. “Row to the far bank,” she said. “I’ll tell you what I am before we land.”

***

Corin rowed. The flooded river had gone quiet in a way that made the hair rise on his arms. The rain thinned to a mist. No lantern now, only the grey nothing of a night with no moon, and still he could see the woman clearly, as if she carried her own small light under her skin.

“My father is the river,” she said. “The one you call the Sorrow. He has run this valley since before the village had a name, and every year he takes what he is owed. A life for the crossing. That is the old bargain, older than the ferry, older than the stone bridge upriver that fell in the spring flood.”

Corin kept rowing. His hands were steady. “And you’re his collector.”

“I am his daughter. I come when the water rises. I walk among the houses and I choose.” She paused. “Tonight I chose you.”

The oars caught, and he let them trail. The skiff drifted. “You sat in my boat and let me row you out here to drown me.”

“I let you row me out here to see what kind of man you were. My father does not take the unwilling. He takes the willing, or the careless, or the proud. You are none of those. That is a problem.”

“Then take someone else.”

“The bargain is the bargain. A life for the crossing, or the river takes the whole village instead.” Her voice was calm and terrible. “He has done it before. Ask your elder why the old church stands empty on the hill.”

Corin thought of the elder’s face at his door, the way she had not met his eyes. He thought of the village, asleep behind its wall of sandbags, and of the children in the upper rooms, and of the bell that would ring if the water came over.

“There’s no one else out here,” he said. “Just me.”

“Yes,” she said. “Just you.”

He shipped the oars. The skiff rocked, empty and patient, in the middle of the drowned world. Corin stood up carefully, and the woman watched him, and the river watched him, and he understood that both of them had been waiting for this since the moment the elder knocked on his door.

“Then take me,” he said. “And let them keep the light.”

She rose to her feet. For the first time she smiled, and it was not cruel, and it was not kind. It was the smile of water closing over a stone. “That,” she said, “is the first time anyone has offered.”

The river surged beneath the hull. Corin felt it rise to meet him, cold and certain, and he did not step back. “Tell the elder,” he said. “Tell her the crossing is paid.”

“She will know,” the woman said. “They always know.”

And the water took him.

The cold did not shock him. It closed over his head like a hand, and the river took him the way a mother takes a sleeping child, without hurry and without malice. He felt the current comb through his hair and pull at his boots, and he did not fight it. Above him the lantern light broke into pieces on the moving surface, and then the pieces drifted apart, and the dark came in around him, soft as wool.

The woman in grey stood in the skiff and watched the last bubbles rise. The river went quiet under her, the way a dog goes quiet when its master comes home. She reached down and touched the water with two fingers, and the water touched her back.

“You were long in coming,” the river said. Its voice was not loud. It came up through the boards of the boat and through the soles of her feet, and it sounded like every stream she had ever crossed.

“The crossing had to be paid,” she said. “You said so yourself.”

“I have said it many times. No one has ever paid with their own coin.”

She looked down at the place where the ferryman had stood. The skiff still rocked a little, as if someone had just stepped off it. “He was a stubborn man,” she said. “He would not let the village lose its light.”

“And you?” the river asked. “What will you do now?”

She did not answer at once. She took up the oars, which were heavier than she expected, and set them in the locks. The far bank was a dark line under a sky that had begun to thin toward morning. Somewhere on the other side, a rooster would soon be confused by the hour, and a woman in a stone house would wake and know, without being told, that the debt had been settled.

“I will go ashore,” she said. “I have been in the water a long time.”

The river made a sound that might have been a sigh. “Then go. But leave the boat where it is. It belongs to the river now.”

She rowed. The current fought her at first, then remembered the bargain and let her pass, and the skiff slid over the drowned fields with a soft, patient sound. When the keel touched mud, she stepped out into the shallows and pulled the boat up just far enough that it would not drift. Then she stood on the bank, barefoot in the wet grass, and looked back the way she had come.

The middle of the river was empty. No light burned there. The lantern lay somewhere below, its flame long out, and the water moved over it as if it had never been.

“Thank you,” she said, to no one and to everyone.

A heron lifted from the reeds and beat away upstream, and the sky in the east turned the colour of a bruise healing.

***

At dawn the water began to fall. It went down the way it had come up, without apology, leaving a line of mud and torn grass along the lower street and a smell of wet stone in every house. The bell in the square had not rung. The elder stood on the step of her door and watched the river shrink back into its bed, and she did not go inside.

People came out one by one. They found their doors swollen in their frames and their gardens flattened. They found a chair halfway down the lane and a chicken on a roof. They stood in small groups and spoke in low voices, and every few minutes someone looked toward the landing.

The skiff was gone from its mooring.

“He would have taken it out,” said a man with a net over his shoulder. “He would not have let it drift.”

“Then where is he?” said another.

The elder said nothing. She walked down to the water’s edge, and the others followed her, because it was easier to follow than to stand still. The river was brown and quick and ordinary. It did not look like a thing that had eaten a man.

Then a boy pointed across the water. “Look,” he said. “On the far bank.”

They looked. The far bank was a low green line they had all seen a thousand times, and none of them had ever been able to name what grew there. But this morning there was something new. A small light burned low among the reeds, steady and yellow, the colour of a lamp left on for someone who has not come home.

“That’s his lantern,” the elder said. “He kept it under the seat.”

“Then he’s over there,” said the man with the net. “He swam for it.”

“No one swims that river,” said the elder.

They waited for the light to go out. It did not. It burned through the grey morning and into the brighter part, and when the sun came up over the hill it was still there, small and certain, a pinprick of fire on the wrong side of the water.

A woman brought a boat hook and stood at the edge, but there was no boat to hook. The river ran past, taking leaves and sticks and the last of the flood, and it made no sound that anyone could call a voice.

“We should go across,” the boy said.

“There is no ferry,” said the elder. “There is only the river.”

She turned and walked back up the lane, and after a while the others followed, because the day was beginning and there was work to do. The light stayed on the far bank. It stayed all day, and the next night, and the night after that, and people began to leave their shutters open a little so they could see it from their beds. It was not much. It was enough. Someone had paid for it, and no one said aloud who, and the village kept the light the way it kept its dead, quietly, in the corner of the eye.
