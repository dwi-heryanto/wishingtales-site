The morning before the harvest festival, Leo found the cellar door hanging open. His stomach dropped like a stone into a well.

He had come down early to count the wheels. It was his job, and he was proud of it. Twelve rounds of goat cheese sat on the shelves every year. Each one was wrapped in waxed cloth and stamped with the little cheese-shaped seal the town had used for a hundred years. The festival needed all twelve for the tasting table. The grand prize was already engraved with the town's name.

Now the shelf stood empty. Not a crumb, not a rind. Just the smell of cold stone and something faintly sour.

"Cheese," Leo said, very quietly. "Please tell me that's not you."

Behind him, his goat burped.

Cheese was a large, spotted, self-satisfied animal with one bent horn and an opinion about everything. He stood in the doorway with a wisp of something white caught in his beard. He chewed with the slow dignity of a creature who has never once been sorry.

Leo knelt and pulled the wisp free. It was waxed cloth. The edge of a stamp was still visible on it.

"That's the seal," Leo whispered. "Cheese. That is the seal."

Cheese swallowed and looked at him with the flat golden calm of a goat who believes all evidence is circumstantial.

Footsteps crossed the square above, quick and many. Leo shoved the scrap of cloth into his pocket without knowing why. Then he heard the mayor's voice ring out like a dropped bucket.

"The cellar's open! Someone fetch the boy!"

***

By the time Leo climbed the cellar steps, half the town had gathered in the square. The mayor stood with her hands on her hips, her festival sash already pinned in place. Her face was the color of an unripe plum.

"Twelve wheels," she announced to the crowd. "Twelve. Gone. On the night before the one morning this town has anything to show for itself." She turned. "Leo. You had the key."

"I had the key," Leo agreed. His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "But I locked it. Last night, right after the lanterns went up. I turned the key myself, and I heard the bolt."

"And who else was with you?"

Leo's ears went hot. "Cheese was with me. But he was outside. On the rope."

A woman near the front cupped her hands around her mouth. "That goat's been eyeing those wheels since spring!"

"He's a goat," Leo said. "He eyes everything. He eyed a bucket once. He didn't eat the bucket."

"Then how do you explain the wax on his chin?"

The crowd turned as one. Cheese, oblivious and majestic, was still chewing.

Leo could feel the whole square deciding at once. He knew that feeling. It was the same one he'd had in school when the teacher asked who had drawn the goat on the blackboard, and everyone turned to look at the boy who kept goats.

The mayor stepped forward until her shadow fell across Leo's boots. "That animal is in my custody until this is settled. And you, boy, had better hope the cheese comes back. Otherwise the festival gets cancelled, and this town remembers your name for a long time."

"Give me until the sun's overhead," Leo said.

The mayor's eyebrows went up. "You'll be gone by noon?"

"I'll be gone by noon," Leo said, "with the cheese. And with proof."

It was a stupid thing to promise. He heard how stupid it was as soon as it left his mouth. But Cheese, who had understood none of it, leaned his warm head against Leo's arm and let out a small, unbothered bleat. Somehow that made the promise feel less like a lie and more like a dare.

"Tie him to the post," the mayor said, and two men stepped forward.

Leo caught the rope himself. "I'll tie him," he said. "He'll stand better for me." He looped the rope around the post. While his hands worked, he leaned close and whispered into the bent ear. "You and me, we're going to find out who did this. And then you're going to look very noble. Understand?"

Cheese chewed. Somewhere in his beard, a fleck of wax cloth glinted in the sun.

Leo straightened. For the first time he looked at the ground between the cellar door and the square. Half-trampled in the dust was a line of pale crumbs going somewhere.

***

The crumbs were not scattered. That was the first thing Leo noticed. They lay in a line, patient as a row of stitches, running from the cellar door across the square. They led into the shadow of the narrow street that went down toward the bakery.

"Someone carried it," Leo said. "Carried it, and it leaked. That's not a goat. A goat eats the evidence. A goat doesn't leave a trail."

Cheese, ambling at the end of his rope, made a low opinion of this. He had already eaten two crumbs and was looking for a third.

"Not you," Leo said. "Not now. You're being noble, remember?"

The street narrowed. Above them the houses leaned together like old women sharing a secret. The smell of the bakery came out to meet them, warm and stubborn. The crumbs ended at a back door set in a wall of grey stone. Beside the door stood a stack of crates, and on the crates sat a wheel of cheese.

Leo knew that wheel. Everyone in the town knew it. It was wrapped in wax cloth stamped with the festival seal, and the seal was broken.

"That's the prize cheese," he whispered. "That's the one they lock in the cellar. That's the one they—"

He stopped. Through the little window beside the door he could see into the bakery. On the long floury table stood a row of wheels. One, two, three, four, five, six of them. All wrapped. All sealed. All exactly the size of the ones that had gone missing.

"Six," Leo said. "Six of them." He looked at Cheese. "The cellar had six."

Cheese was not listening. Cheese had found a smear of soft cheese on the doorframe and was applying himself to it with the focus of a scholar.

Leo pulled him back. "We need to be quiet. We need to be clever. We need to—"

The door swung open.

***

The baker was a broad woman named Marta, and she did not look surprised to see a boy and a goat in her yard. She looked tired. She wiped her hands on her apron, and the flour came off in a pale cloud.

"If you've come to buy," she said, "I'm closed. If you've come to accuse, get in line."

"That's the festival cheese," Leo said. "On your crates. With the seal broken."

"It is."

"You stole it."

Marta looked at him for a long moment. Then she sighed, and the sigh came from somewhere below her boots. "I took it. I'll not deny it, and I'll not be proud of it either. But I didn't want it."

"You took six wheels of prize cheese because you didn't want to?"

"I took it because I was told to." She jerked her chin at the row on the table. "And before you ask who told me, it was a figure in a black cloak. No, I didn't see a face. Yes, I know how that sounds."

Leo stared at her. Cheese, bored, sat down heavily on Leo's foot.

"Two nights ago," Marta went on, "a knock at the back door. This one, here. I opened it and there was someone in a cloak, hood up, voice low, like a man doing a bad impression of a man. Said if I didn't take the cheese from the cellar and keep it hidden, my oven would go cold. My oven, boy. The only thing I own."

"So you believed them?"

"I believed them enough to try." She gestured at the wheels. "And I couldn't do it. Couldn't get the cellar open, could I? They had to do that part themselves. Whoever it was came back the next night with the wheels already out and left them here in the yard. Told me to hide them till the festival was over. Told me the town would blame the goat."

Leo felt his stomach go tight. "The goat. My goat."

"That's what they said." Marta looked at Cheese, who was chewing the corner of her apron with great serenity. "Though looking at him, I'd say the goat had nothing to do with it."

"No," Leo agreed. "He didn't." Then, carefully, because his heart was beating fast now, "You said the cloaked one came back. Did you see where they went?"

Marta was quiet. "Down the alley. Toward the mayor's house."

Leo's mouth opened. "The mayor's house?"

"I'm not saying it was the mayor," Marta said. "I'm saying it was toward the mayor's house. There's a difference, and I'd thank you not to make it smaller than it is."

Cheese bleated, softly, as if to agree.

***

Leo didn't sleep much. At dawn he was already in the barn, and Cheese was already awake, watching him with that flat, patient goat stare that meant he expected breakfast and would wait exactly four seconds for it.

"We need a decoy," Leo said. He dragged an old wheel of hard rind from the back of the barn — the one nobody ate, the one that tasted like a door. It was about the right size, and in the dark it would pass. "We set it out. We wait. We see who comes."

Cheese chewed a strand of hay and looked unimpressed.

"I know," Leo said. "It's not much of a plan. But it's the only one I've got."

All day he pretended to be busy. He helped string paper lanterns across the square. He carried crates of apples. Every time someone mentioned the stolen cheese he shrugged and said, "Terrible," in a voice that sounded wrong even to him. The mayor passed him twice. Both times she looked at him with that soft, sad, I'm-sorry-for-your-family expression that made Leo want to bite something.

By dusk the festival was nearly ready. Ribbons, lamps, a stage for the band. In the cellar yard, behind the low stone wall, Leo set the fake wheel on a barrel in plain moonlight. He tied Cheese to a post a little way off — not too close, not too far — and crouched in the shadow of the wall with a sack over his knees for warmth.

The night got cold. The moon climbed. Cheese fell asleep standing up, the way goats do, his chin on the post. Leo's legs went numb. He was starting to think he'd been wrong about everything when he heard it: a soft tick, tick, tick on the cobbles, like little claws trying to be quiet.

A shape slipped through the gap in the wall. Small. Low. Four legs.

Leo held his breath. The shape circled the barrel once, twice. Then it rose up on its back legs and hooked its front paws over the rim of the fake wheel.

The moon came out from behind a cloud, and Leo saw the mayor's cat.

"You," he whispered.

The cat froze, one paw on the cheese, eyes like two lamps. Then it did the worst thing possible: it sat down and began to wash its face, as if Leo were the one behaving strangely.

***

Leo lit a lantern and held it up. The cat blinked at him and went on washing.

"It was you the whole time," Leo said. "You took the cheese. You made Marta hide it. You — " He stopped. "How does a cat make anyone do anything?"

"I didn't say it was a cat," said a voice behind him.

Marta stood in the yard gate with a lantern of her own and a face like a closed shop. She looked at the cat. The cat looked back at her, and Leo had the strange feeling they were having a conversation he wasn't invited to.

"The cloak," Marta said at last. "It was hanging on the line. The figure took it down and put it on. I never saw a face. But I did see a tail, and I told myself it was the wind."

"A cat in a cloak," Leo said.

"A very large cat," Marta said. "And a very dark night."

Leo turned the lantern on the wall behind the barrel. There, tucked into a corner where the stones had come loose, was a gap he'd never noticed. He knelt and reached in. One by one, like a magician doing a very slow trick, he pulled out the missing wheels. All four of them. Wrapped in a black cloak that smelled faintly of fish.

The mayor's cat watched him with the calm of a creature who had never once been caught at anything.

"Why?" Leo said. "Why would a cat steal a fortune in cheese?"

Marta crouched and looked into the gap. "Because it's warm and dry and nobody goes there," she said. "And because a cat with a hoard is a cat who never has to ask." She straightened. "Though I'll grant you, it's the strangest thing I've seen in forty years of baking."

Cheese, who had woken up at last, strained at his rope and bleated once. It was a short, sharp sound that somehow managed to be both an accusation and a request.

"He says he told us so," Leo said.

"He did not say that," Marta said. "He said 'let me at that barrel.'"

"Same thing," said Leo, and for the first time all day he laughed.

The cat, deciding the conversation had gone on long enough, strolled over to Cheese. It sat down and curled its tail around its paws. Cheese looked at it. The cat looked at Cheese. Then they both turned their faces toward the cheese, in perfect unison, like two small statues of appetite.

"We'll take the wheels back now," Marta said. "Before those two decide to make a night of it."

By the time the sun came up, the cellar was full again and the festival had its cheese. The mayor made a speech nobody listened to. Marta sold out by noon and had to send home for more. Cheese had been officially declared a hero by a show of hands in the square. He spent the afternoon being fed scraps by every child in town and looking unbearably pleased about it.

The mayor's cat was not punished. Leo made sure of that. He put a saucer of milk by the cellar door every evening, and the cat came to drink it, and after that the cheese stayed where it was put. Mostly.

At the end of the day, Leo found Cheese behind the stall with his nose in the last wheel of the prize-winning cheese. The goat was working through the rind with single-minded devotion.

"You're supposed to be a hero," Leo said.

Cheese did not look up.

"The evidence," Leo said. "You're eating the evidence."

A tail flicked. A crumb fell. Marta came around the corner, saw the wheel, saw the goat, and sighed the sigh of a woman who had made her peace with the world a long time ago.

"Well," she said. "He did find the thief."

"He didn't find anything," Leo said. "He ate the apron."

"Then he ate the apron and found the thief," Marta said. "Let him have his cheese, boy. Heroes get paid."

So Leo sat down in the last of the light and watched his goat eat a small fortune in prize-winning cheese. The town went on celebrating around them. The mayor's cat watched from the wall, its tail wrapped neatly over its toes. Nobody in the whole square looked happier than Cheese.
