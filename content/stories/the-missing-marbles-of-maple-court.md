Cora kept her marbles in a tin box under her bed. On Saturday morning, she pulled it out and found nothing inside but a smell of dust. Her breath went out of her. Twenty-seven marbles, gone. Every shooter, every swirl, every cat's-eye. She turned the box upside down and shook it. One marble rolled out. A blue one, deep as a summer sky, and it wasn't hers. It sat on her windowsill, catching the light.

"Sam!" she called. "Sam, come here!"

Her brother stuck his head around the door. "What?"

"My marbles. All of them. Gone."

Sam came in and looked at the empty box. "Are you sure you didn't lose them?"

"I'm sure. I counted them last night. And this was on the sill." She held up the blue marble. "It's not one of mine."

Sam took it and turned it over. "Weird. Maybe a thief left it by mistake?"

"A marble thief?" Cora said. "Who steals marbles and leaves one behind?"

"Let's ask the neighbors," Sam said. "If someone's been in our house, they might have been in theirs."

Cora put the blue marble in her pocket. It felt cool and smooth, like a secret.

***

Mrs. Gable lived next door and kept her marbles in a glass jar on the porch. When Cora and Sam knocked, she was already holding the jar. "Look at this," she said. "My whole jar is empty except for one green marble. I found it on my doormat."

Cora showed her the blue one. "Same thing at our house."

Mrs. Gable frowned. "Who would do such a thing?"

They went to the Pattersons' house across the street. Mr. Patterson was on his knees in the garden, peering under a bush. "I'm looking for my son's marbles," he said. "Every last one is gone, and there's a red marble on the birdbath that doesn't belong to us."

"Did you hear anything last night?" Sam asked.

"Only a flapping sound," Mr. Patterson said. "Like a big bird. I thought it was the wind."

Cora and Sam traded a look. They knocked on three more doors. Each neighbor had the same story: marbles vanished in the night, one strange marble left behind. A yellow one on a fence post. A white one on a windowsill. A black one in a shoe.

"Every house on the street," Cora said. "And each one has a leftover marble."

"It's a trail," Sam said. "Like breadcrumbs."

Cora pulled the blue marble from her pocket and held it up. "Then let's follow it."

The trail led past three more houses. A green marble sat on a mailbox. A striped one balanced on a gate latch. Cora picked each up and turned it in her fingers. "These aren't from our street," she said. "Look, this one has a tiny star inside. I've never seen it before."

Sam pointed at the ground. "Here's another clue." A line of small scratch marks ran along the bottom of a wooden fence. They were thin and sharp, like someone had dragged a fork through the paint. Cora crouched low. "Bird feet," she said. "Three toes forward, one back. I saw tracks like this in the mud by the pond."

The dust under the fence held a strange pattern too. Little circles, spaced out in a curve. Sam pulled out his notebook and copied them. "They look like the marbles," he said. "As if something rolled them along."

"Something that flies," Cora said. "Mr. Patterson heard flapping." She stood and brushed off her knees. "And something that collects shiny things. Magpies do that."

Sam's eyes went wide. "A magpie stealing marbles?"

"It's not stealing to a magpie," Cora said. "It's treasure. Come on. We need to find its nest."

***

That night, Cora and Sam hid behind a hedge with a borrowed flashlight. The street was dark and quiet. An owl called somewhere far off. Cora's heart thumped hard against her ribs.

"What if it doesn't come?" Sam whispered.

"It will," Cora said. "The marbles are still missing. It's probably hungry for more."

They waited. Then came a rustle in the oak tree. A black and white bird swooped down and landed on the fence. It hopped along the top rail, head cocked. In its beak was a bright red marble.

"There," Cora breathed. "Don't move."

The magpie dropped the marble into a gap in the fence, then flew back up. It disappeared into a nest high in the oak tree. Cora shone the flashlight. The beam caught the edge of the nest, and something glinted inside. Marbles. Dozens of them.

Sam grabbed her arm. "We found it. We found the marble thief."

Cora smiled in the dark. "Now we just have to get them back."

Morning came grey and damp, and Cora was already on the back step with a basket and a spoon. Sam arrived with a coil of string and a bag of raisins. "That's not a trap," he said, looking at the spoon. "That's breakfast."

"It's bait," Cora said. "Magpies love shiny things. My dad says so." She propped the basket upside down on a stick and tied the string to the stick, then set the spoon beneath it, face up to catch the light. Raisins went around the rim like a little fence.

They hid behind the woodpile and waited. The street woke up slowly. A gate clicked. Somewhere a kettle sang through an open window. Cora's knees went stiff and her nose went cold.

"Maybe it's gone for good," Sam said.

Cora shook her head. "Look. The fence gap." A blue-black shape was bobbing along the rail, tail flicking. The magpie landed beside the basket and cocked its head at the spoon. It hopped closer. Then it seized the spoon and tugged.

Cora pulled the string. The basket dropped, but the bird was faster. It flapped up onto the fence with the spoon in its beak and scolded them loudly.

"It's laughing at us," Sam said.

"No," Cora said slowly. "It's showing us." The magpie hopped three times along the fence, then flew low toward the far end of the garden, where an old hollow stump stood. It dropped the spoon into the hollow and waited.

They ran over. Inside the stump, packed in a nest of grass and string and bottle caps, were the marbles. Dozens of them. Glassy, milky, striped, all of them winking in the morning light.

***

One by one, Cora carried the marbles home. She made a list in her notebook and ticked it off as she went. The Hendersons got back their green cat's-eyes. The little boy at number nine got back his twelve milkies. Mrs. Okafor held her cracked blue marble to the light and laughed out loud.

"My grandmother's," she said. "I thought I'd lost it forever. How did you ever find it?"

"There was a thief," Cora said. "But it wasn't a mean one. It just liked the way they shone."

Mrs. Okafor looked at her for a long moment. "Then you'd better give the thief something else to like," she said, and handed Cora a small tin of shiny buttons.

That afternoon, Cora climbed the oak tree with the tin and set it on a branch near the nest. The magpie watched her from the fence, head tipping side to side. When she climbed down, it flew straight to the buttons and rattled them happily.

Back in her room, Cora opened her marble jar. It was almost empty now, but one blue marble sat in the bottom, the one from the windowsill. She rolled it in her palm. It was hers now, a thank-you from a thief.

Sam knocked on the door frame. "You could start a new collection, you know."

"I know," Cora said. She set the blue marble on the windowsill, right where she'd found it, and smiled. "But this one stays out. So the magpie knows where to find me."
