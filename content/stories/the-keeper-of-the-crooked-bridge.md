Maren reached the bridge before the light did. The river ran below it, wide and brown and silent, and that silence was wrong. On the first night of flood season, the water should have been shouting. She set her lantern on the stone post and knelt to check the ropes that held the railing. Her family had kept this bridge for six generations, since before the village had a name, and every keeper knew the river's moods. This mood was new.

The bridge itself was crooked. It had been built that way, or it had fallen that way, and nobody alive could say which. The planks rose and fell like a held breath, and the arches leaned against each other for company. Maren's grandmother had said the bridge was crooked because the river was. Her mother had said the bridge was crooked because it was listening. Maren had never known what that meant until now, standing in the dark with her hands on the wet rope, feeling the stillness under her feet.

She walked the planks from end to end, counting. Forty-two planks. Three loose. One that always groaned. The lantern threw a small yellow circle that did not reach the far bank. Beyond it, the water lay flat as a table, and the reeds stood straight, and no frog spoke.

"You're quiet tonight," Maren said to the river. Her voice sounded thin. She cleared her throat. "I'm not complaining. Quiet is easier to keep."

The river did not answer. It never did, not in words. But it always answered in some way, a ripple or a slap against the pilings, and tonight there was nothing. Maren crouched by the post and warmed her hands on the lantern glass. The flame inside was small and steady, and it had been her mother's flame, lit from the old wick before the fever took her. Maren had kept it burning for four years. It was the one thing she had not let go.

A heron stood in the shallows, not fishing, not sleeping, just standing. It did not turn when she passed. The whole world, it seemed, was holding its breath.

"All right," Maren said. "I'm here. Whatever it is, I'm here."

She sat on the post and waited for dawn, as keepers did on the first night. The ledger lay in her satchel, and she did not open it. There was nothing to write yet. But her hand stayed on the strap, and she watched the flat water, and she did not blink more than she had to.

***

Before dawn, an old woman came out of the mist on the far bank. She walked without a lantern, and the mist parted for her, and her feet made no sound on the planks. Maren stood up. The heron lifted its head at last.

"You're early," Maren said. "The village doesn't cross till sunup."

"I'm not from the village." The old woman stopped at the middle of the bridge, where the planks dipped lowest. Her coat was grey, and her hair was grey, and her eyes were the brown of the river. "You're the keeper."

"I am."

"Then you know the pact."

Maren's hand tightened on the lantern. "I know the words. My mother said them to me. Keep the bridge, keep the silence, keep the flame."

"And do you know what sleeps under it?"

The old woman pointed down through the planks, and Maren looked, and the water below was so clear she could see the bottom stones, and among the stones something long and pale shifted once and was still.

"The serpent," Maren said. Her mouth was dry.

"It wakes every hundred years. Tonight it rises." The old woman's voice was not unkind, but it was flat, like a stone skipped once and sunk. "It will ask for passage. The pact says a keeper must not speak. Not one word, not one breath of a word, until it has gone again. If you speak, the bridge falls."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the bridge stands, and the serpent takes its toll."

Maren looked at the water. The pale shape had not moved again. "What toll?"

"The ledger will tell you." The old woman turned to go, then stopped. "Your mother kept the silence. So did her mother. It can be done." She looked back, and for a moment her grey face was almost gentle. "But it is not a small thing, child. Make sure you know what you are keeping."

She walked into the mist, and the mist closed behind her, and the heron shook its wings and flew. Maren stood alone on the crooked bridge with the lantern in her hand, and the river lay still as a held breath, and dawn was still a long way off.

She opened the ledger.

The ledger was bound in river-leather, soft as a cheek and dark as wet slate. Maren turned the pages by lantern light, and the keepers spoke to her in their cramped, careful hands. Year after year: the river high, the bridge held, the keeper silent. Some entries were only a date and a single word. Held.

Then she found the page where the writing changed. The hand was older, the ink browner, and a note ran down the margin like a scar.

Every hundred years the serpent rises and asks passage. The keeper must not speak. If the keeper speaks, the bridge falls. If the keeper keeps silence, the serpent takes its toll. The toll is one thing the village loves. It chooses. It is always fair. It is never kind.

Maren turned the page. The next entry was a list.

A hundred years past: the mill on the south bank. The village woke to empty air and a millstone turning in the current like a slow, drowned wheel.

A hundred years before that: the well in the square. Children cried for a week. The water never came back sweet.

Before that: the oak at the crossroads, and the swing that hung from it, and the shade where old men played at stones.

Every hundred years, something the village loved, gone by morning. The ledger did not say where the serpent put them. Maren did not want to know.

She turned to the last written page, and her mother's hand was there, small and slanting.

Toll taken: the east field. The one with the good soil. My mother wept. I did not speak.

Beneath it, in a different ink, newer, Maren's own name was written in the margin. Not by her mother. By someone else. The old woman, perhaps. Or the river itself.

Next toll due: the bridge.

Maren sat down hard on the cold stone. The bridge. The crooked bridge her family had tended for nine generations. The only road to the north farms, the only crossing before the water widened and ran too deep. If the bridge fell, the village would be cut off until spring. The north farms would be stranded. The children who crossed to school would wade the ford and some would not make it.

She read the words again, hoping they would change. They did not.

"You knew," she said to the empty bridge. Her voice sounded thin and childish in the dark. "You all knew, and you kept the silence anyway."

But the ledger had no answer. Only her mother's neat hand, and the toll, and the bridge.

The lantern guttered. Down in the water, the pale shape had begun to move again, slow and certain, the way a thought moves when it has decided.

***

Midnight came the way it always comes on the river: all at once, and without permission.

The water did not churn. It lifted. A coil the width of a boat rose out of the black current, shedding silver, and then another coil, and another, until the bridge was ringed by a rising wall of scales. The serpent's head came last. It was not huge. That was the worst of it. It was the size of a horse, and its eyes were the colour of old coins, and it looked at Maren the way a cat looks at a bird it has already caught.

It did not speak. It did not need to. It settled its chin on the parapet and waited, patient as winter, and Maren understood that the silence was not a rule for her alone.

Her mouth was dry. The lantern shook in her hand. One word, the old woman had said. Not one breath of a word.

She thought of the north farms. She thought of the mill and the well and the oak, and how the village had mourned each one and then gone on, because going on was what a village did. She thought of the bridge, crooked and old and hers, and of the cold clean water running under it.

The serpent's eyes narrowed. It was waiting for her to speak. It wanted her to speak, she realised. That way the bridge would fall, and the serpent would not have to take it. It could simply let gravity do the work.

So the silence was a trap, and the word was a trap, and the bridge was going to fall either way.

Unless.

Maren lifted the lantern. It was her mother's lantern, the one she had carried every flood night since she was nine years old, the one with the hairline crack in the glass and the dented brass base. She had never lit another. She had never wanted another.

"I don't offer you the bridge," she said, and the words came out steady, though her hands were not. "I offer you something the village loves. Something I love. That's the pact, isn't it? One thing. Fair. Never kind."

The serpent went very still. The coins of its eyes turned to her, and something in them shifted, like a door opening in a dark house.

Maren held out the lantern, and the flame leaned toward the water as if it had been waiting all along.

"Take it," she said. "And let the bridge stand."

The river rose to meet her hand.

The water was cold. It closed over her wrist like a hand, and for a moment Maren felt the serpent's whole length through it — a current that went down and down and did not stop. The lantern did not float. It sank, slowly, the flame still burning, a small gold eye going under. She did not let go. Her fingers stayed open until the last of the brass slipped free, and then she pulled her hand back, wet to the elbow, and held it against her chest.

"There," she said. Her voice cracked. "One thing. Taken."

The serpent's head sank until its chin touched the water. The coins of its eyes did not blink. "You loved it," it said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"And you gave it anyway." The serpent's voice had changed. It was not gentler, exactly. It was the way a person speaks when they are thinking and not reciting. "The others gave what they could spare. A goat. A ring. A name they did not use. You gave the thing you carried every night."

"The pact says one thing the village loves. It didn't say it had to be easy."

Something moved under the water — a long coil turning, the river bending around it. Maren braced her feet on the wet stone and did not step back. The bridge groaned above her, one beam shifting, and she heard grit fall into the dark.

"The bridge is old," the serpent said. "It would have fallen in a hundred years without me. You have bought it a hundred more."

"Then it's a fair trade."

"It is a fair trade." The serpent's head rose again, and now it was close enough that she could see the fine scales around its jaw, each one ringed like a tree. "But I will tell you what I told your mother's mother, and her mother before. A keeper who gives everything has nothing left to keep. Do you understand me?"

Maren's throat was tight. "I understand."

"No," said the serpent. "You will." And then it went down. The water folded over its head without a sound, and the river ran on, and the coins of its eyes were gone.

Maren stood in the shallows until her feet went numb. Then she climbed the bank and sat on the cold grass and looked at her empty hands.

***

Dawn came grey and slow. The rain had stopped sometime in the night, and the river was back inside its banks, brown and busy and ordinary. The bridge stood. Maren walked its length twice, once in each direction, and found every beam where it should be. The crack in the third railing was still there. The stone under the first arch was still worn smooth by a hundred years of feet.

Villagers came down the road with carts and buckets and questions. Old Renna from the mill asked if the water had taken anything, and Maren said no. A boy named Tobin asked if she had seen the serpent, and she said she had. He waited for more. She gave him nothing, and after a while he went away.

By midmorning the road was full of people pretending the night had been ordinary. Maren climbed to the keeper's hut on the bank, where the ledger sat open on the table, and turned to the last page. Her hand was steady when she wrote the date. Beside it she wrote: one lantern, brass, cracked glass. Taken. And beneath that, after a moment, she wrote: bridge stands.

Then she went to the shelf where the spare lanterns waited — four of them, dusty, unlit, the ones her mother had kept for the nights the first one failed. She took down the smallest, a plain tin thing with a wire handle, and set it on the table.

She had no flame. The old lantern was gone, and no fire in the hut would light a keeper's lamp; that was the first thing her mother had taught her. A keeper's light had to come from a keeper's light.

Maren sat and looked at the cold wick for a long time. Then she took the ledger and turned back through the pages, past her own years, past her mother's, until she found the entry in her grandmother's hand: a new lantern lit from the old, the night the old one broke. In the margin, small and cramped, her grandmother had written: The flame does not belong to the lantern. It belongs to the keeping.

Maren closed the book. She carried the tin lantern down to the bridge and set it on the stone at the water's edge, where the river ran close and clear.

"I gave you the thing I loved," she said to the water, and her voice did not shake this time. "I'm not asking for it back. I'm asking for what a keeper asks."

The river moved. A thread of gold came up through the brown — thin as a hair, then brighter — and it touched the wick and caught. The flame stood up small and steady in the tin lantern, and Maren lifted it, and the light fell on the wet stones and the worn railing and the long road home.

She hung it on its hook by the hut door, where it had always hung. Then she went down to the bridge to meet the morning, because the flood season was not over, and the keeping had only begun again.
