Ana had lived in the same house her whole life, so she knew every sound it made. The stairs complained on the third step. The radiator in the hall ticked like a slow clock. The kitchen tap dripped one drop, then waited, then dripped again.

But on Tuesday night, when she got up for a glass of water, the hallway outside her room had gone quiet. Not the good kind of quiet, like snow. This was the kind that felt held.

She stood in her doorway and listened. Her bedroom clock ticked behind her. The floorboards creaked under her toes. Then she stepped onto the hall carpet, and the sounds stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, like a hand over a mouth.

Ana pulled her foot back into her room. Tick, tick, tick. The clock came back. She put her foot out again. Silence, thick as a blanket.

"Hello?" she whispered.

Her voice went out and didn't come back. It was like shouting into a cupboard full of coats.

The air along the hallway felt warm and close, as if someone had breathed in and hadn't let it out. Ana held her own breath without meaning to, then made herself let it go.

At the far end, the dark doorway of the spare room waited. Downstairs, a light was still on. That thin yellow line under the kitchen door looked very far away.

She reached out one hand. The air near the wall felt the same as the air in her room, but it hummed, just a little, like a jar with a fly in it.

From her parents' room, Dad snored. So the house wasn't broken, not all of it. Something was wrong with the hallway only. Something was in it, or waiting in it, or holding its breath the same way she was.

Ana stepped back onto her bedroom rug and pulled her door until it was open just a crack. The ticking clock was the best sound she had ever heard.

***

In the morning, Teo found her sitting on the top stair with her chin on her knees. Teo was six, and he still believed Ana knew everything worth knowing.

"Why are you sitting there?" he asked.

"Because I'm not allowed to sit on the ceiling," Ana said. "Listen. Put one foot in the hall and then take it back."

Teo wrinkled his nose, but he did it. Foot out, foot back. His eyes went round.

"It's quiet," he said. "It's quiet like a library. But there's no librarian."

"You hear it too?" said Ana, and her chest loosened. She hadn't known how much she needed him to hear it.

They tried a marble. Teo rolled it down the hall carpet, and it stopped rolling halfway, as if it had run into a crowd of invisible people. It didn't bounce. It didn't click. It just lay there, like a word stuck in a throat.

"That's not right," Teo said, very seriously. "Marbles are always loud."

"Say something into the hall," Ana told him. "Something short."

Teo leaned forward and shouted, "MARMALADE!" His shout went one step into the hallway and then vanished, thin as a thread.

He looked at Ana. "Did it go somewhere?"

"I don't know," she said. "Maybe the house is keeping it."

They sat together on the top stair, and the silence leaned on the doorframe like a guest who wouldn't leave. Teo slid his hand into hers.

"Maybe it's just tired," he whispered.

Ana looked down the long hall to the dark end. "Maybe it's holding something in," she said. "And maybe we should find out what."

Their mother came up the stairs with a basket of laundry, humming. Ana held her breath. Mom walked straight into the hallway, past the marble lying still on the carpet, past the place where Teo's shout had gone missing.

"Mom," said Ana. "Does the hall feel strange to you?"

Mom shifted the basket onto her hip and looked down the long corridor. "Strange how?"

"Quiet," said Teo. "Quieter than quiet."

Mom smiled. "It's a hallway. Hallways are quiet." She stepped into her bedroom and set the basket down, and her footsteps landed the way footsteps always had. Then she came back out and touched the light switch. The lamp at the dark end of the hall clicked on, cheerful and yellow. "See? Nothing spooky. Go brush your teeth."

When her door clicked shut, Ana and Teo looked at each other.

"She didn't hear it," Teo said.

"She walked right through it," Ana said. "Like it wasn't there at all."

They stood side by side at the top of the stairs. Ordinary evening sounds drifted up from the kitchen: the tap running, a pot lid going down. But one step past the top stair, the sounds thinned out. The hallway swallowed them the way a blanket swallows a coin.

"Why only us?" Teo asked.

Ana thought about it. "Because we're quiet enough to hear it. Grown-ups are always busy being loud inside their heads." She wasn't sure that was true, but it sounded right, and Teo nodded as if she'd handed him something solid. The held breath pressed at them, patient and enormous. "Get your torch," she said. "We're going to find out where it's coming from."

***

They went down the hallway slowly, Teo in front with the torch, Ana's hand on his shoulder. Halfway along, a thin coldness slid over Ana's bare feet, like water from a fridge.

"Feel that?" she whispered.

The cold came in little drafts, each one weaker than the last. Ana crouched and held out her palm until the next one brushed her fingers. "It's coming from down there," she said. "By the skirting board."

They crept to the dark end of the hall, where the lamp's yellow glow gave up and the shadows leaned close. Teo swung the torch beam along the wall. Nothing. He swung it lower. There, where the board met the floor, a crack ran under the baseboard, thin as a pencil line and long as Ana's arm.

The draft breathed out of it, soft and steady. Ana put her ear close. From inside the wall came the faintest sound, far away and pressed down, like someone humming with a hand over their mouth.

"Teo," she said. "It's not just air. There's something in there."

Teo knelt beside her. "Is it a mouse?"

"Mice don't hum." She sat back on her heels. All her life this house had creaked and sighed and settled. She had never once wondered what it was saying. "I think the house is holding something in," she said. "And it's been holding it a long time."

The crack seemed to wait. Teo aimed the torch and the light did not go in. It stopped at the edge and pooled there, as if the dark inside were something you could touch. "What do we do?" he whispered.

Ana looked at the thin line in the wall. "We let it out," she said, and her voice was steadier than she felt.

Ana knelt in front of the crack, her hands flat on the floor. The cold draft lifted the fine hairs on her arms. Teo crouched beside her, the torch beam trembling against the wall. "What if it's angry?" he said. "What if it's been stuck in there so long it forgot how to be nice?"

Ana bit her lip. All she had to do was stay quiet. The hallway would go on holding its breath, and everything would stay exactly as it was. But the humming from inside the wall had sounded so tired. "It's not angry," she said. "It's waiting. Waiting to be heard."

She leaned close to the thin dark line. The floorboards felt warm under her knees, warmer than wood should be. "Hello," she said. Her voice came out small, and the crack swallowed it whole. Teo grabbed her sleeve. "Say more," he whispered. "Say it properly."

Ana took a breath. "Hello," she said again, louder. "It's Ana. My brother Teo is here too. We live here. We're not going anywhere. You can let it out now."

For a moment nothing happened. Then the crack sighed. It was a long, slow sound, like a door opening after years of being shut. A puff of warm air brushed Ana's face, and with it came a smell she knew: dust and rain and the faint sweetness of her mother's tea, all mixed together. The hallway gave a great creak, and the sound travelled all the way down to the dark end and back.

***

All at once the hallway was full of noise. The clock in the kitchen ticked, and the fridge hummed, and somewhere a door clicked shut. The sounds came rushing in like water into a dry riverbed, and the air felt light and loose, no longer held. Teo let out a breath he must have been keeping for ages. "It worked," he said. "Ana, it worked."

Ana sat back on her heels. The hallway looked the same, but it didn't feel the same. The shadows at the dark end seemed softer now, and the creak of the floorboards had a new tune. She stood up and walked toward the end of the hall, past the crack, past the place where the air used to stop. Teo followed close behind.

"You're not scared?" he asked.

Ana tilted her head. The dark end of the hallway was just a dark end, a place where the lamp didn't quite reach. "No," she said. "I'm just wondering what else the house might say. If we listen."

From under the baseboard came a small, contented sound, like a cat settling into a sunny spot. Ana smiled. She took Teo's hand, and together they walked back to their room, leaving the hallway to its own quiet conversation.
