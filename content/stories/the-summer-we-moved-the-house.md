Ruth found the letter on a Tuesday, wedged between the screen door and the frame like a scrap of dead skin. The bank's logo was a blue diamond, and the words inside were small and certain: the house on Alder Street would be demolished in ninety days. She read it twice on the porch steps, while the afternoon went gold and the neighbor's dog barked at nothing.

Inside, Toby was eating cereal for dinner. He was twelve, four years younger than Ruth, and he still believed that if you ignored a thing hard enough, it would go away. Ruth had stopped believing that when their mother died and the bank had started calling about the mortgage.

"We're not moving," Toby said, milk on his chin.

"We are," Ruth said. "Just not the way they think."

She spread the letter on the kitchen table and put a pencil in her fist. The house was old, built from oak and pine and a kind of stubbornness that had outlasted three generations. It had a porch that sagged in the middle, a staircase that groaned on the fourth step, and a kitchen window that caught the evening sun like a held breath. Ruth loved it the way you love a person who has been kind to you without being asked.

"We take it apart," she said. "Board by board. Number every piece. Move it to the lot out past the fairground. Rebuild it."

Toby stared. "That's insane."

"It's ninety days."

"It's insane and it's ninety days."

Ruth sharpened her pencil with a paring knife. "Then we start tomorrow."

***

The first board came off the porch with a sound like a tooth being pulled. Ruth worked the pry bar while Toby held the nail set, and the morning smelled of sawdust and cold dirt. By noon they had a neat stack of planks on the lawn, each one labeled in Ruth's careful hand.

Mr. Grieves from next door watched from his own porch, arms crossed. He was seventy and had lived on Alder Street since before Ruth was born. He had a shed full of tools and a habit of pretending not to care.

"You're going to ruin that house," he called.

"It's already ruined," Ruth called back. "I'm just making it movable."

He came over at one o'clock, after the wind had picked up and Toby was struggling with a window frame that had swollen shut. Mr. Grieves took the crowbar from Ruth's hands without asking and wedged it under the sill. The frame popped free with a crack.

"You number them on the inside," he said. "Where the weather won't take the ink."

Ruth nodded. "Thanks."

"Don't thank me. I'm only here because your mother used to bring me soup when I had the flu." He wiped his forehead with the back of his wrist. "Debt's a debt."

They worked until the light went blue. Toby carried the smaller pieces to the stack and wrote the numbers where Mr. Grieves told him, and Ruth measured and pried and did not let herself think about the bank or the deadline or the way the house looked with its skin peeled back, like something that trusted her and had been wrong to.

At dusk, Mr. Grieves stood on the lawn with his hands in his pockets. "You know this is a fool's errand," he said.

"I know," Ruth said.

"Good." He turned toward his own door. "Fools get more done than sensible people. Always have."

***

The storm came in from the west on Thursday, fast and low, and Ruth heard the tarp snapping before she heard the thunder. She was out of bed and down the stairs with her boots half on. Toby was already at the back door, holding his jacket closed with one fist.

"Get the bricks," Ruth said. "Weight the edges down."

The wind took the first corner of the tarp and flung it back like a sheet. Rain hit the stacked boards and the numbered window frames and the long crate of hinges and nails. Ruth dragged the tarp across and knelt on it while Toby hauled bricks from the garden wall. She felt the rain soak through her knees, cold and steady.

"The frames!" she shouted. "Get the frames under the porch!"

Toby ran, arms full, and came back for more. A gust took the whole tarp off the stack and slapped it flat in the mud. Ruth stood in the yard and watched the water stream down the fresh-cut wood. Shapes she could name in the dark. Shapes she had spent three days saving.

By morning the tarp lay in a heap, and the boards had swollen and warped and grayed at the edges. The window frames had taken water overnight. Ruth lifted one and the paint flaked off in her hand. She set it down and sat on the wet grass.

"Ruth." Toby crouched beside her. "We can sand it. We can sand it back."

"It won't fit," she said. "Swollen wood doesn't go back together. It's not a line on a drawing. It's just wet wood now." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I was so careful. I numbered everything."

Mr. Grieves came across the lawn in his bathrobe, carrying two cups of coffee he clearly did not intend to drink himself. He stood over the damaged stack for a long moment and said nothing at all, which was somehow worse than anything he could have said.

"The bank doesn't care," Ruth said. "They'll knock it down and I'll have a pile of wet wood and a lot of numbers."

"Probably," Mr. Grieves said. He held out a cup. "Drink it. You look like a drowned cat."

She drank it. It was terrible coffee and she drank all of it, and then she went and got the sandpaper.

***

They spent the day sanding and drying and sorting what could be saved from what could not. It was slow work and it made Ruth's shoulders ache, and by four in the afternoon she had a stack of good boards and a stack of firewood and a growing certainty that the firewood stack was winning.

Toby worked the high shelf over the porch, where the last of the trim boards were still nailed. Ruth was underneath with the crowbar. "Careful," she said. "That one's holding the others."

"I've got it," Toby said.

The board came loose sooner than either of them expected. It swung free, and the shelf above it tipped, and the heavy crate of numbered slats slid off the edge and came down.

Toby twisted away and the crate caught the ladder instead of his head. The ladder went sideways. Toby went with it, and his leg folded under him when he hit the ground, and the sound he made was small and bright and awful.

Ruth was beside him before she remembered moving. His shin was already going dark, and his face was white and sweating.

"Don't touch it," he said through his teeth. "Don't. Ruth. Don't touch it."

Mr. Grieves called from his porch, and then he was there too, kneeling, his hands steady and his voice flat and calm, telling Toby to breathe, telling Ruth to get the car keys and the blanket off his own back seat. Ruth ran. She ran past the good boards and the firewood and the numbered slats scattered across the grass, and none of it looked like a house anymore. It looked like a yard full of broken things.

The hospital was forty minutes away. Somewhere between the turn onto the highway and the second traffic light, Ruth understood that she had been choosing wrong for a week. She had been counting boards when she should have been counting her brother.

"I'm sorry," she said, at a red light, to nobody in particular.

Toby, sweating in the back seat, said, "The house isn't going anywhere."

"Yes it is," Ruth said. "That's the whole problem."

***

Ruth sat in the hospital corridor with her hands wrapped around a paper cup of tea she had not drunk. Toby's leg was in a cast from ankle to knee, a clean break, the doctor said, and he would be fine. The relief came and went in waves, leaving a flat, tired feeling behind. Mr. Grieves sat two chairs down, leafing through a fishing magazine he had found somewhere.

"You should go home," Ruth said. "It's late."

"I will," Mr. Grieves said. "When you do."

She looked at him. His jaw was set in that way it got when he had already made up his mind. "Why are you helping us?" she asked. "You didn't want to."

He turned a page. "I didn't want to watch you do something foolish alone. There's a difference." He closed the magazine. "The deadline is Friday. That's four days. How much is left?"

"Too much," Ruth said. "The storm ruined half the boards. The windows cracked. And now I can't lift anything because I have to look after Toby."

Mr. Grieves was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "You know, there's a whole street of people who owe your mother favors. She watched half their kids when they were small. She drove Mrs. Alcott to her treatments every week for a year." He stood up and tucked the magazine under his arm. "You don't have to do it alone. You just have to ask."

***

The next morning, Ruth stood on the porch of the house with a clipboard and a list. Toby was propped on the sofa with his leg up, directing operations from a distance, and the empty rooms behind her still smelled of the kitchen things she had packed away in crates the week before.

Mrs. Alcott arrived first, wearing gardening gloves and carrying a toolbox. "I hear we're moving a house," she said. "I've moved three in my time. Well, I've moved furniture. It's the same principle."

Then came the Hendersons from number twelve, and their two teenage sons, who looked bored until Ruth showed them the crowbar. Then came Mr. Okafor with his truck, and the woman from the corner shop with a thermos of coffee and a box of sandwiches. By nine o'clock, there were eleven people in the yard, and the work went faster than Ruth had ever seen it go.

"I don't understand," Ruth said to Mr. Grieves, who was carefully labeling a stack of floorboards with a piece of chalk. "Why did they all come?"

"Because you asked," he said. "And because your mother was the kind of person who showed up. People remember that."

Toby watched from the window, his cast propped on a cushion. "You're doing it wrong," he called out to the Hendersons' youngest son. "The nails go the other way."

"You want to come out here and do it yourself?" the boy called back.

"I would if I could," Toby said, and grinned. "But I'm supervising."

By Thursday night, the house was a flat stack of numbered boards and beams, bundled and tied and loaded onto Mr. Okafor's truck and a borrowed trailer. Ruth stood in the empty lot where the house had been. The grass was flattened, and there was a square of bare earth where the foundation had been. It looked like nothing. It looked like a place where something had been taken away.

***

The new lot was on the other side of town, a narrow strip of land between a church and a row of garages. It was not the same. The soil was harder, and the light fell differently. But it was theirs.

On Saturday morning, the same eleven people came back. Mrs. Alcott brought her toolbox again, and the Hendersons brought their sons, and Mr. Okafor brought his truck. Mr. Grieves brought a level and a ball of string and a patience Ruth had not known he had.

"Corner first," he said. "Then the frame. Then the floors. You don't rush a house."

Ruth worked until her hands blistered. She held boards while they were nailed, and she read out the numbers from the chalk marks, and she learned the difference between a joist and a rafter. Toby sat in a folding chair with his leg out, calling out measurements and arguing with Mr. Grieves about the best way to brace a wall.

"You know," Toby said, when the frame was up and the sun was going down, "it doesn't feel like the old house."

Ruth looked at it. The bones were the same, the same boards and beams, but the shape was different in the new light. It was smaller than she remembered. It was quieter. "No," she said. "It doesn't."

"That's okay, though," Toby said. "Right?"

Ruth thought about the empty lot and the flattened grass and the square of bare earth. She thought about the way Mr. Grieves had said you don't rush a house, and the way the neighbors had come when she asked. "Yeah," she said. "That's okay."

They finished the roof on Sunday. The house stood on its new lot, smaller and stranger than the old one, but it stood. Ruth walked through the empty rooms and put her hand on the wall by the door. It was warm from the afternoon sun. She thought of her mother, who had packed the kitchen into crates and never seen the new lot, and she stood there a while with her palm flat against the boards.

"It's not the same house," Toby said from the doorway, leaning on his crutches.

"No," Ruth said. "But it's still home."

She looked out the window at the street, and the church, and the row of garages, and the neighbors who were still there, sitting on the tailgate of Mr. Okafor's truck and eating the last of the sandwiches. "I know," she said.
