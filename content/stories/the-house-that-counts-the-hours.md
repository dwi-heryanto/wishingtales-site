The taxi pulled away, and Mira stood alone on the gravel drive of her grandmother's house. Weeds had pushed through the stones, and the porch sagged like a tired back. She'd never been here without Gran waiting at the door with a glass of lemonade.

Aunt Ruth had said to come straight in. The key was under the mat, where it had always been. Mira let herself in.

The hallway smelled of dust and lavender. A narrow table stood against the wall, and on it lay a thick ledger bound in cracked leather. It was open, and the pages were crowded with dates and neat columns of tally marks.

Mira ran her finger down the page. There were years, months, days, each followed by clusters of four vertical strokes and a diagonal slash. Some pages had dozens of them. Others had only a few.

"Hello?" she called.

No answer. The house settled with a soft creak, as if it had heard her.

She turned the ledger toward the window. The dates went back decades, in several different hands. The earliest entries were faded brown ink. The most recent were in Gran's spidery script, and they stopped three weeks ago, on the day she died.

A chill crept up Mira's arms. She closed the book and slid it back onto the table, then wiped her palms on her jeans.

"Ruth?" she called again, louder.

A door opened at the end of the hall. Her aunt appeared, drying her hands on a tea towel. Ruth had Gran's sharp eyes and none of her softness.

"You made it," Ruth said. "I was starting to think the train swallowed you."

"The taxi took forever," Mira said. She nodded at the ledger. "What is that?"

Ruth glanced at the table, and something flickered across her face. "Gran's book. She kept it out here. Didn't like it in the sitting room."

"It's full of tally marks. What was she counting?"

Ruth folded the tea towel and set it on the table beside the ledger. "Hours," she said. "The house counts the hours."

***

Mira followed her aunt into the kitchen. It was warm and bright, and a kettle was already steaming. Ruth poured two cups without asking.

"What do you mean, the house counts the hours?" Mira said.

"Exactly that." Ruth slid a cup across the table. "Every hour anyone spends living here gets a mark. Gran started it when she moved in. Her mother did it before her. The house has always counted."

Mira wrapped her hands around the cup. "That's not a real thing."

"No?" Ruth raised an eyebrow. "You know how many hours you've spent under this roof? I do. Four hundred and twelve, give or take. Gran wrote them down for you."

"I've only visited a few times."

"Forty-one days, all told. She added them up every night."

Mira stared at the ledger through the doorway. The idea made her skin prickle. "Why would anyone keep a record like that?"

Ruth stirred her tea, slow and deliberate. "Because the house asks for it. Gran used to say the house gets restless if the count falls behind. The marks keep it quiet."

"That's a story to scare kids."

"Maybe." Ruth sipped. "But when I was your age, I stopped writing my marks for a week. The pipes screamed all night. Every door in the house swung open at once. Gran made me fill in every hour I'd missed before morning."

Mira looked at her aunt's face. Ruth wasn't smiling. "You believe it."

"I believe Gran believed it. And I believe this house is old and strange and doesn't like being ignored." Ruth set down her cup. "You're here for the summer. The ledger's in the hall. I'd keep it up, if I were you."

"And if I don't?"

Ruth picked up the tea towel and folded it again. "Then I suppose we'll find out."

That night, Mira lay in Gran's old room and listened. Somewhere below, a floorboard creaked. Then another. A soft, steady rhythm, like someone counting under their breath.

Mira woke to rain ticking the window like a fingernail. The ledger lay where she'd left it, open on the hall table. She'd meant to ignore it. Instead she pulled the chain on the lamp and turned the pages back.

Dates ran in columns, each with a cluster of strokes beside it. Some days had five marks. Some had twenty. The last page was different. The marks there weren't neat. They climbed the margin, pressed so hard the pen had torn the paper in two places. At the bottom, in Gran's slanted hand: *count to the end of the line.*

Mira counted. She counted twice, then a third time with her finger on the page. The marks added up to a date. Tomorrow. She checked the calendar on the wall. Same date.

"Ruth?" she called.

Her aunt came in from the kitchen, wiping her hands. "What is it?"

"The ledger. The marks — they add up to tomorrow. Every stroke on this page, it's all counting down to tomorrow."

Ruth leaned over the book. Her face didn't change, which was worse than if it had. "Gran did that at the end. She said the house wanted a finish. A last entry."

"A last entry for what?"

"She never told me." Ruth straightened. "She wrote something in the back, I think. I never found it."

That night the house began to groan. Not the small creaks of old timber settling. This was deeper, a long low sound that came up through the floor and into Mira's teeth. It stopped. Then it started again, and this time she could hear the shape of it. A rhythm. One, two, three. One, two, three.

The counting.

She lay still and listened until she couldn't stand it. At the top of the stairs she looked down into the dark hall. The ledger was glowing faintly under the lamp, and the groan came from everywhere at once, from the walls and the chimney and the boards under her feet. Mira went back to bed and pulled the blanket over her head, and the house counted on beneath her, patient as a heartbeat.

***

By morning the rain had stopped and the house had not. Mira came downstairs to find every clock in the hall stopped at different times. The tall one by the door said ten past three. The little brass one on the shelf said seven. The kitchen clock had no hands at all.

She didn't touch the ledger. She put on her shoes and picked up her bag.

"Going out?" Ruth asked from the kitchen doorway.

"To Jen's. I told her I'd come over."

Ruth looked at the stopped clocks, then at Mira. "Be careful on the step. It's wet."

Mira pulled the front door. It didn't move. She pulled harder, both hands, heels braced. The handle turned fine, the latch clicked, but the door sat in its frame like it had grown there.

"It's stuck," she said.

Ruth came and tried it herself. She put her shoulder to it. Nothing. "The damp," she said, but her voice had gone thin.

Mira went to the back door. Same thing. The windows wouldn't budge either, though she could see the garden through them, grey and dripping and close enough to touch.

"Ruth. The windows."

Her aunt stood in the hall with her arms folded, and for the first time she looked frightened. "It's counting," she said. "Listen."

Mira listened. The groan was louder now, and faster. One, two, three, four. The floorboards took it up. The pipes in the wall answered. It wasn't under her breath anymore; it was all around her, in the air, in her own chest.

"What does it want?" Mira said.

Ruth shook her head. "Gran knew. She wrote it down somewhere. I've looked for years."

The house counted on. Mira pressed her hands over her ears, and the counting went right through them.

Mira went up to her grandmother's room because it was the only door left that might open. The stairs complained under her feet, but they let her pass. The bedroom smelled of lavender and old paper. She stood in the middle of it and listened to the house count—faster now, a pulse in the walls.

"Gran," she said, "if you left something, I need it."

The room gave nothing back. She pulled open the wardrobe, and the door swung wide with a sigh. Coats, a shoebox of buttons, a hatbox with a dried corsage. She ran her hands along the back panel, and her fingers caught on a seam. The panel lifted out. Behind it was a folded page, torn from the same ledger that lay in the hall.

Mira unfolded it. Her grandmother's handwriting, small and slanting: *The house counts what it is given. Every hour we live here, it writes us down. When we go, it keeps the last entry. It does not want to stop counting. It will not let the last of us leave until the account is closed. One final entry, and it rests. Write a name. Any name. Then it will sleep.*

She read it twice. The house counted through her hands. "Any name," she said, and her voice sounded strange in the empty room. "Gran, you couldn't do it either, could you?"

From below, Ruth called up the stairs. "Mira? What have you found?"

"A note," Mira said. She came down with the page held out. Ruth took it and read it standing in the hall, and her face did something complicated—relief and fear together.

"Any name," Ruth said slowly. "She never told me. She just stopped writing in it, and then she got sick, and then she was gone."

"She couldn't write a name," Mira said. "Because it would be a real person. It would be like giving someone to the house."

Ruth looked at the ledger on the hall table. The tally marks had climbed higher in the last hour. There were more of them now than there were hours left in the day. "Then we run," she said. "We try the door again. We break a window."

"The windows won't break," Mira said. "You know they won't."

The house groaned, and the sound went up through the floor and into her teeth. She thought of the date the marks were adding up to—tomorrow, the day her grandmother had died. The house had been counting down to it for years.

"Write my name," Ruth said suddenly. "I'm older. I've had more of it than you."

"No." Mira said it hard. "That's not how it works. You're the one who stayed. It would keep you."

She stood in the hall with the pen in her hand, and the house counted, and she understood at last what her grandmother had known. The house didn't want a stranger. It wanted the one who was leaving. It wanted to keep the last of them.

***

Mira waited until the clock in the hall showed eleven fifty. The counting had become a roar she could feel in her bones, and Ruth sat on the stairs with her hands over her mouth, watching.

"You don't have to do this," Ruth said.

"I know," Mira said. "That's why I'm doing it."

She opened the ledger to the last page. The tallies crowded the margins, and the date at the top was tomorrow. Her grandmother's note lay beside it. *Write a name. Any name. Then it will sleep.*

Mira thought of running. The doors wouldn't open, but she could try the cellar, the chimney, the gap under the eaves. She could fight the house until morning. But the counting would go on, and Ruth would stay, and next year it would be Ruth's name the house wanted.

She put the pen to the paper and wrote her own name.

For a moment nothing happened. Then the counting stopped. Not faded—stopped, like a held breath. The silence was so complete that Mira heard her own heart and the tick of the clock and Ruth's small gasp. The house settled. The pipes went quiet. The floorboards stilled under her feet.

"Mira," Ruth whispered. "What did you do?"

"I closed the account," Mira said. Her hand was shaking. She looked at her name on the page, and it looked back at her, ordinary and small. "It wanted the last one of us. It didn't want to be alone. So I gave it someone to remember."

The front door clicked. It swung open on the wet grey morning, and cold air came in, and the house breathed out.

Mira picked up her bag. At the threshold she turned and looked back at the hall—the ledger, the clock, her aunt standing in the middle of it with tears on her face. "It'll sleep now," she said. "You can stay. It won't count you anymore."

She stepped out into the rain, and behind her the house was quiet, and it did not call her back.
