Milo woke because his left foot was cold. Not the ordinary cold of a dormitory at six in the morning, with frost on the inside of the window and the radiator clicking like a tired cricket. This was a specific, personal cold. It meant his foot was bare and the blanket had betrayed him.

He sat up and groped under the bunk. His fingers found the right sock first, a woolly grey tube. Then nothing but floorboard. The left one was gone. He checked the other side, the gap between the bed and the wall, the tangle of his school bag strap. Nothing.

"Oi," he said, to nobody in particular. "Who's nicked my sock?"

A snore came from the top bunk. That was Dev, who could sleep through a fire alarm and had once slept through an actual fire alarm. In the next bed, Priya was already awake, looking at her own feet with deep suspicion. One foot was wearing a sock. The other was not.

"Don't say it," Priya said.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say, 'Priya, have you seen my left sock,' in that voice."

Milo swung his legs out of bed. The floor was freezing. "I was going to say, 'Priya, why are you missing a sock?' It's a different question entirely."

Priya pulled her blanket up to her chin. "Because something in this room is collecting them. Look." She pointed at the end of her bed, where a single sock sat neatly folded on the pillow, as if placed there with care. "I didn't fold that. I never fold anything."

Milo looked around the dormitory. It was a long, low room with six beds, a row of lockers, and a window over the frozen hockey pitch. The morning light was grey and thin. Near the door, a trail of small soft shapes led toward the space under Dev's bed. Socks. Left socks, by the look of them, all colours and patterns, lying end to end like stepping stones.

"Dev," Milo said, loudly. "Dev, wake up. There's something under your bed."

Dev's head appeared over the edge of the bunk. His hair stuck up in six directions. "What?"

"Socks. A lot of them. And they're not yours."

Dev looked down. His face went through several expressions in quick succession: confusion, interest, then a slow, delighted grin. "Brilliant," he said. "It's a sock monster. I told you this place was haunted."

"It's not haunted," Priya said. "It's a building. Buildings don't haunt. They just settle and make noises."

"Then explain the socks."

Priya had no answer for that. She got out of bed, pulled on her one sock, and walked over to the trail. Milo followed. Together they crouched at the edge of Dev's bed and peered into the dark underneath.

At first Milo saw nothing but dust and a lost pencil case. Then the pile moved.

It was a heap of socks, dozens of them, heaped up like a small, grumpy hill. The heap had a sort of face, if you squinted: a sock with a hole in it for a mouth, two balled-up ankle socks for eyes, a fringe of loose threads like eyebrows. It was breathing, or at least pulsing gently, in and out.

"Hello," Milo said.

The pile shifted. The mouth-sock opened and closed. A low, gravelly voice came out of it, like someone talking through a mouthful of wool. "You're late," it said. "I've been waiting."

Dev made a squeaking noise. Priya grabbed Milo's arm.

"Waiting for what?" Milo asked.

"For you to notice," said the pile. "You never notice. You just wear your socks and lose them and never think about where they go. Well, I'll tell you where they go. They come to me. All the left ones. Every last one."

***

Priya recovered first. She let go of Milo's arm and folded her arms instead, which was her way of pretending she wasn't scared. "That's theft," she said. "You can't just take people's socks."

"I didn't take them," said the pile, with dignity. "They came. Left socks are lonely. They get separated from their partners and they wander. I give them a home."

Dev had crawled out from under his bunk and now sat on the floor, cross-legged, looking at the pile like a boy who had just found a new favourite thing. "Do you have a name?" he asked.

"I am the Left Sock Collective," said the pile. "But you can call me Colin."

"Colin," Dev repeated, delighted. "Colin the Sock Monster."

"I am not a monster. I am a collective. There's a difference."

Milo looked at the trail of socks, then at the pile, then at his own bare left foot. "Right," he said. "Colin. We need our socks back. The dorm inspection is at nine. If we don't have matching socks, we get a demerit."

"A demerit," said Colin, as if tasting the word. "How frightening. A small mark on a piece of paper. I have been here for thirty years, boy. I have seen demerits come and go. I have seen boys come and go. The socks stay."

"Thirty years?" Priya said. "That's not possible. This dormitory was built in 1987."

"Time moves differently for socks," Colin said, vaguely. "It's all the standing still."

Dev leaned forward. "What do you want?" he asked. "For the socks. What's your price?"

Colin's eyebrow-threads twitched. The pile settled itself more comfortably, as if it had been waiting for this question all along. "A trade," it said. "One object from each of you. Something you love. Something precious. Then I'll release the socks."

Milo looked at Priya. Priya looked at Dev. Dev looked thrilled.

"What sort of object?" Milo asked.

"That's for you to decide," said Colin. "But choose well. I'm not easily impressed."

The pile rippled, and somewhere in its depths a sock sighed. Outside, the bell for first lesson rang across the frozen pitch, and none of them moved.

"First lesson is chemistry," Priya said, not moving. "We have a test."

"You have a test," said Colin. "I have a sock collection and a growing sense of impatience. Choose your objects."

Milo's mind was a sock drawer of its own, rummaged through and mostly empty. The lump under the bed had grown taller than his knees, and it smelled faintly of wet wool and the inside of a shoe. Dev was already digging in his rucksack. He pulled out a comic book in a plastic sleeve.

"Signed first edition," Dev said. "My uncle queued six hours for this."

Colin's eyebrow-threads lowered. "Paper," it said. "Again. Paper."

"It's not just paper, it's—"

"Next." A sock arm snaked out and took the comic anyway, tucking it somewhere deep. Dev's mouth hung open. "You said trade," he said. "That was trading."

"I said choose well," said Colin. "You chose paper."

Priya stepped up. She held her watch in both hands, the one her grandmother had sent from Kerala, its strap worn soft as a ribbon. "This," she said quietly. "It's not paper."

Colin considered it with the solemnity of a customs officer. "Metal and glass," it said. "Acceptable. It ticks. I like ticking things." The watch vanished into the pile, and Priya's wrist looked suddenly bare and thin. She didn't complain, but she folded her arms.

Milo was last. The pile swivelled toward him, and a hundred socks leaned in.

"Well?" said Colin.

"I'm thinking."

"Think faster. The bell's gone, and your chemistry teacher will notice."

Milo emptied his pockets onto the floorboards: a bus ticket, two pencils, a crumpled packet of mints, and a grey stone the size of a plum. The stone had a white band across it like a belt. He'd found it on a beach when he was seven. He had carried it through four schools, two house moves, and one very bad summer. It was, he had always felt, the reason nothing worse had happened to him. He didn't believe in luck, exactly. He just didn't want to find out.

"This," he said, and held out the rock.

Colin took it. The sock tips curled around the stone, and for a moment the pile was still. Then it snorted, a wet, woolly sound.

"A rock," it said.

"It's a lucky rock."

"It is a rock. It is grey. It has a stripe on it. I have socks with stripes on them. I do not need a rock." Colin tossed it back. It bounced off Milo's knee and rolled under the bed. "Try again."

"That's all I've got."

"You have more than that," said Colin. "You have a great deal more than that. I can smell it on you."

Milo's stomach went cold. "I don't know what you mean."

"The furry thing," said Colin. "The one with the tail. The one you feed pilchards to when the matron isn't looking. I can hear it breathing under the third bed. It has been breathing under there all night, and it is very boring, and I would like it to stop."

Priya made a small sound. Dev said, "No. Absolutely no."

"The cat," said Colin, "is an object of value. It is warm. It moves. It would make an excellent addition to the pile." A sock arm extended, pointing at the far corner. "Bring me the cat, and you may have all your socks back. All of them. Even the ones with the holes."

Milo stood very still. Under the third bed, in the dark, something small and orange was purring, entirely unaware that it had become currency.

***

The cat was called Biscuit, though nobody had ever officially named it. It had arrived in October, thin and loud, and had simply refused to leave. Matron said it was against the rules. Matron also left a saucer of milk out on Thursdays, so the rules were flexible.

"You can't," Dev whispered. "Biscuit's not an object. Biscuit's a person. Sort of."

"Biscuit is a cat," said Colin, from the pile. "Cats are objects. I have known many cats. They are all objects, and most of them are smug."

Priya crouched down and looked under the third bed. Two green eyes looked back. "He's not smug," she said. "He's just orange."

"The cat," said Colin, "or the socks. Those are the terms. I am being generous. I am being more than generous. I am being magnanimous, which is a word I learned from a dictionary that got mixed in with the laundry in 1994."

Milo's hands were shaking, so he put them in his pockets. The lucky rock was somewhere under the bed, useless. The bus ticket was useless. The mints were useless, unless Colin wanted mints. Colin did not look like it wanted mints.

"Why a cat?" Milo asked. "Why not something else?"

"Because I am lonely," said Colin, and for a moment its voice lost its grumpy edge and sounded almost like a boy's. "Because I have been under this bed for thirty years, and the socks do not talk back, and the dust is not a conversationalist. The cat would talk back. The cat would be company."

"Biscuit doesn't talk," said Dev.

"He would learn," said Colin. "Everyone learns, eventually. Even socks."

Milo looked at the pile. He looked at Priya, whose bare wrist was still folded against her chest. He looked at Dev, who had lost his comic and was now gripping the bed frame as if it might hold him back.

"No," Milo said.

Colin's eyebrow-threads shot up. "No?"

"No. You can't have Biscuit. He's not mine to give. He's not anyone's. He just lives here."

"Everyone lives somewhere," said Colin. "That is not a legal argument."

"It's the only one I've got," said Milo. "And if you take him, I'll—I'll tell Matron. I'll tell her there's a sock monster under the bed."

"Matron," said Colin, "does not believe in sock monsters. Matron does not believe in anything she cannot iron."

"Then I'll sit here," said Milo, and he did, right down on the floorboards, crossing his legs. "I'll sit here until you give the socks back. I've got nowhere to be. I've got a chemistry test I'm going to fail anyway."

Priya sat down next to him. After a moment, Dev sat down too, though he made a point of sighing about it.

The pile loomed over them, enormous and ridiculous, smelling of wet wool and thirty years of loneliness. Colin stared down at the three of them with its button eyes.

"This," it said at last, "is not how trades work."

The pile waited. It had all the time in the world, apparently, and no intention of being reasonable about it. Milo sat on the cold floorboards and thought about his chemistry test. He thought about the words on the test, and the fact that he would fail it whether or not he ever saw his left sock again. Then, quite suddenly, he thought of something else.

"You know what I've got?" he said. "I've got a show."

Colin's button eyes narrowed. "A show."

"A performance. For you. Since you're lonely. Since you've been under here for thirty years with nothing to do but be a pile." Milo was already pulling off his right sock, the one the creature had left him. "Dev, give me your sock. Priya, the spare in your pocket, the one you keep for luck."

"That is my lucky sock," said Priya.

"It'll be worth it." He held up his hands. "Trust me."

She handed it over. Dev handed his over too, grumbling, and Milo pulled all three socks onto his hands and his left foot and made his fingers into a mouth.

"Good evening," said the sock on his right hand, in a high, wobbly voice. "My name is Gerald. I have come to complain about the weather."

The pile did not move.

"The weather," said the sock on his left hand, in a deeper, stupider voice, "is wet. I am a sock. I am always wet. Nobody has ever asked me how I feel about this."

A small sound came from the pile. It might have been a cough.

"And my colleague," said Milo, waggling his foot, "has only one thing to say about the whole business." He made the foot-sock rear up. "I am not a hat. Stop putting me on your head."

Colin made a noise. It started low in the wet wool and came up through all of it, and it was a laugh, a creaking, rusty, thirty-years-of-no-laughing laugh. The pile shook. A pair of socks fell off the top and landed on the floor.

"Stop," said Colin. "Stop. I am a dignified creature."

"I am a sock," said Milo in the high voice, "and I have been inside a boot for nine hours, and I would like to speak to the manager."

The laugh got bigger. The pile was heaving now, great shuddering waves running through it, and threads were popping loose at the seams, and the button eyes were sliding down its face. Dev was laughing too, in spite of himself, and Priya had her hand over her mouth.

"I am a sock!" Milo shouted, waving both hands. "I am a sock and I am FREE!"

The pile burst. It simply came apart, all at once, and cascaded across the floor in a great harmless avalanche of wool. Socks went everywhere, over the bed, under the wardrobe, into Dev's hair. The button eyes rolled across the boards and stopped against Milo's knee.

***

For a moment nobody said anything. The dormitory looked like the inside of a sheep.

Then Priya stood up, brushed a sock off her shoulder, and began to sort. "Left socks in one pile," she said. "Right socks in another. Dev, stop laughing and help."

"I'm not laughing," said Dev, laughing.

They worked fast. The inspection was at nine, and it was already a quarter past eight, and Matron's footsteps had started up somewhere below, steady as a metronome. Milo matched socks with a kind of frantic joy, and by ten to nine there was a neat row of pairs along the foot of every bed, and not one of them was missing.

Biscuit had not moved from the windowsill the whole time. He watched them work with the calm of a creature who had never doubted the outcome.

Matron came in at nine exactly, with her clipboard and her iron-grey bun, and walked down the row of beds, and looked at the socks, and looked at the socks some more.

"Acceptable," she said, and left.

They stood in the middle of the dormitory in the sudden quiet.

"We are never," said Dev, "speaking of this again."

"Agreed," said Priya.

"Agreed," said Milo.

They shook on it, solemnly, three hands meeting in the air. Then Priya went off to her chemistry revision and Dev went to find breakfast. Milo sat on the edge of his bed and looked at the button on the floorboards, and after a while he picked it up and put it in his pocket.

He was late for the test. He failed it, exactly as predicted. It was, on the whole, a good day.

That night he came back to the dormitory first, and stopped in the doorway, because something was wrong. He could feel it before he could see it. He checked the beds, the floor, the row of pairs.

Every sock was there. Every single one.

But on the end of Dev's bed, where a glove should have been, there was only a small, tidy, empty space, and a single button lying in the middle of it.
