The saucer was the good one, chipped at the rim. Otis set it on the windowsill and filled it with milk until it sat level with the paint.

"You know it's just going to evaporate," Mira said. She was sitting on the floor with her back against his bed, bicycle clips still on her ankles.

"Right," said Otis. "But if it does, we'll know it was the air. That's a test."

He laid the folded page beside the saucer and weighted one corner with a pebble from the lane. On the page he had written, in his best handwriting: *Who is writing in my ledger?*

The lamp went off. The hill stood outside the window, black against a sky that had not decided on its colour yet.

The dark was the ordinary kind, at first. A clock ticked in the next room. Mira's breathing slowed and then sharpened, the way it did when she was thinking something she did not want to say.

"How long?" she said.

"Until midnight. That's when the other lines came."

"And then what, we shake hands with it?"

"We see what it writes."

She pulled her knees up. "I've seen nothing for two nights, Otis. Not one thing. I'm here because you asked, not because I believe you."

He did not argue. He watched the milk instead, the little ring of light on its surface, and the page with his question on it, which looked smaller the longer he looked at it. Outside, the wind leaned on the house and moved on, bored.

***

At midnight the clock in the next room struck, slowly, and Mira sat up as if someone had knocked.

The saucer had not moved. The milk was still level to the paint. The pebble still held its corner, and when Otis lifted the page, his question sat there alone, dry and unanswered.

"Nothing," Mira said. She sounded pleased and disappointed at the same time.

Then Otis saw the ledger. It was open on the desk, and it had not been open. His pen was across it, uncapped, and the page had a line on it that he had not written.

The hand was small and upright. It said: *Wind from the west, falling. No rain. The village will sleep well.*

"That's tomorrow's entry," Otis said.

"That's your handwriting with a headache," said Mira. But she had come closer, and she did not touch it.

A cold came into the room. It was not the cold of the window left open. It came from the page, the way heat comes from a stove, except it was the wrong way round. Otis felt it on the backs of his hands first, then at his neck.

Mira crossed her arms. "Tell me that's the draught."

"The draught is behind you," Otis said.

The lamp would not turn on. He tried the switch twice, and the second time the bulb flickered and gave up. So they stood in the dark with the ledger open and the milk shining on the sill, and far off, over the hill, something moved that was too slow to be weather.

"Right," said Otis, quietly. "So it heard us."

Mira found the torch on the shelf above the coats, and the beam was a thin yellow finger that made the dark look bigger. Otis took out his pen and unscrewed it. "If it writes with ink," he said, "it needs ink."

"Or it's a ghost and it doesn't need anything," said Mira. "I'm just saying what I see."

"You don't see anything."

"I see a cold room and a milk saucer nobody's drunk from." She held the torch steady. "What's the drop for?"

The drop was for measuring. Otis put one bead of blue ink on the corner of the open page, then folded the top half down over it, careful as a surgeon. "Grandad taught me this. If you press a page on a wet mark, it copies. So if anything writes in this book tonight, it has to get past my fold."

"That's not a test," said Mira. "That's a trap for a page."

"It's both," said Otis, and closed the ledger. The cold went out of the room like a held breath let go. The lamp, when he tried it, came on. He turned it off again, because the dark felt more honest now. They sat on the floor with their backs to his bed and did not talk about the hill. At half past eleven Mira's head dropped onto his shoulder. At midnight the torch was still on, and the ledger was open, and neither of them had touched it.

***

Otis unfolded the page with two fingers, the way you open a letter you are afraid of. The ink drop was still there, a small blue sun. No copy had bled into the fold. But under it, in the same upright hand, were two lines, and they were not about the hand at all.

*The hill hears the village the way a cup hears the tap. It is not empty of you.*

*You have asked four times who writes. Ask once what the hill is for.*

"It didn't answer your question," Mira said. Her voice had gone flat and careful. "It answered a question you didn't ask."

Otis read the line again. Four times. He counted on his fingers, and the count came out right, and that was the worst part. It had been keeping count of him. "It's not writing to the book," he said slowly. "It's been listening to me. The whole time."

Outside, the hill was black against a sky the colour of dishwater. He looked at it and the night looked back, patient, and something in his chest went small and cold, like the moment before you step onto ice. "Mira," he said. "Do you ever get the feeling the ground knows your name?"

She was quiet a long time. "No," she said at last. "But I'm getting it now."

Mira had to go home before her mum started calling up the lane for her. Otis heard her wheels on the wet road, then the tick of a gate, then nothing. He sat with the ledger open and the lamp turned down low, and the house made its small night noises around him. The fridge hummed. A slate shifted somewhere above his head.

From the next room came a cough, then a voice, thin as paper.

"Otis. Is that you breathing out there?"

He went in and sat on the edge of the bed. Grandfather's eyes were open, which they were not often, and they were clear the way a window is clear after rain.

"I can't sleep," Otis said. "I keep thinking about the hill."

"Good," said Grandfather. "It's been thinking about you a while."

Otis's mouth went dry. "You know?"

"I wrote in that book for fifty years, boy. Some nights the ink came out of me. Some nights it didn't." Grandfather shifted his hand on the blanket, slow, like moving a heavy thing. "I used to ask it who. Never got anywhere. Then one winter I asked it something else, and it told me."

"What did you ask?"

"What it wanted." He closed his eyes. "It said, keep the record. That's all. Somebody has to remember what the weather did." A breath. "You've been asking it who it is. Ask it what it needs. It's a big old thing and it's lonely, that's my guess. Don't ask me how I know."

Otis sat very still. The lamp ticked. Outside, the hill was a long dark shape against a sky going grey at the edge.

"Right," he said.

***

Back at the window, Otis opened the ledger to the last page. The strange line was still there, patient in its old-fashioned hand. He picked up his pen and turned it over once, the way he did before an entry, and then he wrote the evening's weather as honestly as he could.

*Wind from the west, dropping after dark. No rain. Frost coming, I think, by the feel of the gate. Sky clear enough to see the hill.*

He read it back. It was true. It was the truest thing he had written in weeks, because he had stopped writing what he hoped and started writing what was there. The frost would come. The hill would be under it.

Then, underneath, he wrote his question. One question, the way the rules went. He kept it short so it could not be dodged.

*What does the hill need?*

He put the pen down and did not wait up. Whatever answered would answer in its own time, and he had school in the morning, and Mira would want to know everything, and he would have to tell her he did not know yet. That was fine. He was getting used to not knowing.

He turned off the lamp. The dark came in, soft and ordinary. Through the glass the hill stood up against the last of the grey, and it did not feel like something watching any more. It felt like something waiting, which is a different thing, and warmer.

Otis pulled his blanket to his chin and listened to his grandfather breathing in the next room, in and out, steady as weather. Then he slept.
