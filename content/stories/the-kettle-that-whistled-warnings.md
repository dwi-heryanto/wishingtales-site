Nell woke to a sound like a small train in her bedroom. It was a thin, bright whistle, coming from the kitchen below.

She knew that whistle. It was her grandmother's brass kettle, the one with the dent in its side and the handle tied with string. Gran had brought it from the old house and set it on the shelf, where it had sat for a year, silent and cold.

Nell padded downstairs in her socks. The kitchen was dark except for the moon, which lay across the floor like a dropped scarf. The stove was cold. She touched it to be sure. Cold.

But the kettle was whistling.

"Gran?" Nell whispered.

No answer. Her grandmother slept like a stone at the end of the hall.

The whistle stopped. In the quiet after it, Nell heard the sea, three streets away, breathing in and out. She lifted the kettle off the shelf. It was lighter than it should have been. Something shifted inside it, sliding against the brass.

Nell turned it upside down over the table. A folded paper fell out, soft as an old leaf.

She unfolded it. The writing was small and slanted, in pencil, and it said:

*under the third bollard, where the rope learns to curl*

Nell read it twice. Then she carried it up to her room and put it under her pillow, and lay awake listening to the kettle, which did not whistle again.

***

In the morning, Nell showed Sam the note at the end of the harbor wall. Gulls hung above them, complaining about the weather.

"Where the rope learns to curl," Sam read out loud. "That's not a place. That's a poem."

"It's a clue," said Nell. "Bollards are for tying boats. Rope curls around them. So it's a bollard where rope has been tied a lot."

Sam squinted down the harbor. The bollards stood along the wall in a row, black and round as mushrooms. "Third bollard from what? From here? From the fish shop?"

"From the fish shop," Nell said. "The note was in Gran's kitchen. She always walked down past the fish shop."

They counted as they went. The third bollard was shorter than the others, worn smooth by years of rope. Nell knelt and felt around its base with her fingers. The stone was cold, and then it was not.

Her hand closed on something loopy and rough.

She pulled, and a coil of rope came out from behind the bollard. It was blue, thick as her wrist, and knotted at one end in a way that looked deliberate, like a signature.

"That's boat rope," Sam said. "Good rope. Nobody leaves good rope on a bollard."

Above them, a gull dropped low and screamed. Nell looked at the knot, then at the harbor, then at the note in her hand.

"Somebody did," she said. "Somebody wanted us to find it."

The kettle was whistling again. Nell heard it from the harbor path, a thin high note cutting through the gull cries. She ran with the rope coil banging her hip, Sam half a step behind.

In Gran's kitchen the brass kettle sat on the cold stove, lid rattling, steam curling from the spout. Nell lifted it off with a tea towel. The whistling stopped.

"It's pointing," Sam said.

The spout had swung toward the window. Nell turned the kettle slowly, and the whistle came back, fainter, then louder when she faced the door.

"It wants us to go outside," she said.

They followed the note down the lane, past the fish shop, along the seawall where the tide was sliding out. The whistle pulled them left, toward the old net sheds. Nell stopped.

"The lantern's gone," she said. "Gran's harbor lantern. It hung on that hook by the shed door. I saw it yesterday."

The hook was empty. Sam crouched and pointed at the mud below. A line of small footprints led away, pressed deep, as if whoever made them had been carrying something heavy.

"Somebody took it," Sam whispered.

The footprints ran along the mud to the far end of the harbor, where the old boathouse leaned into the wind. Its windows were dark. Its door hung crooked on one hinge.

Nell held the kettle up. It whistled once, sharp and sure, then went quiet.

"That's the way," she said.

***

The boathouse smelled of tar and wet wood. Nell pushed the door and it swung with a groan. Inside, a small boat lay on its side, its hull patched with odd scraps — a tin lid, a piece of shutter, a strip of blue sailcloth.

A boy stood behind the boat, holding a hammer. He was thin and brown-haired, and his shirt was too big for him.

"Don't tell," he said.

Sam stepped forward. "Tell what?"

The boy swallowed. "I'm Pip. I borrowed things. The rope. The lantern. A few boards." He pointed at the hull. "The boat leaks. My family needs it. My dad's gone to sea and my mum's ill, and the boat is all we have."

Nell looked at the patches. "You took Gran's lantern?"

"To see by," Pip said. "I bring it back before morning. I always bring it back." He set the hammer down. "I didn't want to steal. I just didn't know how to ask."

Sam crouched by the boat. "This patch is loose. The water will come right through."

Pip's face went red. "I know. I ran out of nails."

Nell looked at the kettle in her hands, then at the boy, then at the boat. Outside, the tide was turning. She could hear it.

"We can help," she said. "But we have to be quick."

Sam picked up the hammer. "If we tell the harbormaster, Pip gets in trouble. But if we don't fix this boat, it sinks."

Nell nodded. "So we fix it. Fast." She turned to Pip. "Where's the leak?"

Pip pointed to a seam near the keel. "There. And there." He looked at the floor. "I used the last of the nails."

Sam was already moving. "Nell, your gran's shed has nails. And a pot of tar. I saw them."

"Go," Nell said. "I'll stay with Pip." Sam ran out into the dark.

Pip stared at her. "You're not going to tell?"

"Not if we can fix this," Nell said. "But you have to promise something. No more borrowing without asking."

Pip nodded hard. "I promise." He picked up a loose board. "I'll hold this. You hammer?"

Nell took the hammer. Her first swings were clumsy, but Pip guided her. "There. Now the tar."

Sam came back with a jar and a fistful of nails. "Tide's coming in fast," he said. "We've got maybe an hour."

The three of them worked. Nell held the boards, Sam hammered, and Pip spread tar along the seams. The kettle sat by the door, quiet now.

When the last nail was in, Pip stepped back. "It might hold." He looked at Nell. "Thank you."

"We're not done," Nell said. "We have to put everything back before morning."

***

The lantern went back to Gran's shed first. Then the rope, coiled exactly as they'd found it. Pip carried the boards he hadn't used.

At the harbormaster's office, Nell stopped. "The lantern and rope were reported missing," she said. "We found them."

The harbormaster, a tired woman with a clipboard, raised an eyebrow. "Found them where?"

"In the old boathouse," Sam said. "Someone left them. We brought them back."

It was not a lie, exactly. The harbormaster wrote something down. "Good work. Tell your gran I said hello."

When they got back to Nell's house, Gran was waiting at the kitchen table. The kettle sat in front of her, polished and quiet.

"You found the note," Gran said. It wasn't a question.

Nell told her everything. Gran listened, then nodded slowly. "That kettle whistled for you because it wanted to help. It's been in this family a long time." She looked at Pip, who stood in the doorway. "Your boat's fixed?"

"Yes, ma'am," Pip said. "Thanks to Nell and Sam."

Gran stood up and picked up the kettle. "Then it's yours now." She held it out to Pip. "Kettles are for keeping things safe. Boats, families, secrets. You'll need it."

Pip took the kettle with both hands. "I'll take care of it."

Nell smiled. The kettle had started all this, and now it would keep watch over Pip's boat. That felt right.
