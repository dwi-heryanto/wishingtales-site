The brass lamp sat on the doorstep like it had been waiting for someone to notice. Lucia found it when she went out for the milk, three months to the day since her father's funeral. It was heavy for its size, green with age, and the handle curved into a shape she couldn't name. No note, no box, just the lamp on the concrete steps, cold in the morning air.

She carried it inside, because it seemed wrong to leave it out. "Mamá," she called. "Someone left a lamp."

Elena came from the kitchen with flour on her hands. She looked at the lamp and her face did something complicated. "Put it in the hall," she said. "We'll ask the neighbors later."

But Lucia didn't put it in the hall. She set it on the table by the window, where the afternoon light could reach it. The brass warmed under her fingers, and for a moment she thought she felt it hum, like a cat purring. She told herself it was the house settling.

Her brother Tomás came home from school and dropped his bag. "What's that?"

"A lamp. It was on the step."

"Who leaves a lamp on a step?" He picked it up, turned it over. "There's writing on the bottom. Looks like Spanish, but old."

Lucia leaned in. The letters were worn, but she made out one word: recuerdo. Memory. She didn't tell Tomás. She wasn't sure why.

That night the lamp lit itself.

***

No one saw it happen. They were eating dinner, the four of them at the table (Lucia, Tomás, their mother Elena, and Elena's sister Rosa, who had come to stay for the week), when the lamp on the sideboard began to glow. Not a flame, not exactly. A soft yellow light, steady and warm, as if a candle burned inside the brass.

Elena put down her fork. "Tomás, did you light that?"

"No." He held up his hands. "I didn't touch it."

Rosa leaned back in her chair. "Is this one of your tricks, Lucía?"

"It's not a trick," Lucia said, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

The light pulsed once, and then a voice came out of the lamp. It sounded like a radio tuned to a station just past the edge of hearing.

"I can return one lost thing," it said. "In exchange for one memory."

Lucia's skin went cold. Her mother's chair scraped back.

"What kind of trick is this?" Elena demanded.

"No trick," said the lamp. "A trade. You give me something you remember, and I give you something you lost. Anything. A ring. A letter. A person's laugh."

Tomás stared at it. "You can bring back the dead?"

"I can return what was lost," the lamp said. "Not always in the way you expect."

Elena stood up. Her voice was hard. "We don't want anything from you. Get out of my house."

"I am not in your house," the lamp said. "I am on your sideboard. You brought me in."

Lucia looked at her mother. Elena's jaw was tight, and her eyes were bright with something Lucia hadn't seen in months: fear. Not of the lamp. Of what it offered.

"We trade nothing," Elena said. "Not one memory. Do you understand me?" She looked at Lucia and Tomás. "Both of you. Promise me."

Tomás nodded slowly. Lucia said, "I promise," but her voice sounded thin to her own ears. She was thinking about her blanket, the blue one she'd lost when they moved, the one her father had wrapped her in when she was small.

That night, the lamp's light stayed on. It didn't flicker. It just watched.

***

Lucia waited until the house was quiet. Her mother's door was closed, and Tomás was asleep with his headphones on, the faint tinny beat of music leaking out. Rosa's room was dark too. The lamp sat on the sideboard in the front room, its brass surface dull in the dark. It wasn't glowing now. It looked like an ordinary thing, the kind you'd buy at a yard sale and regret.

She stood in front of it for a long time. Her promise sat in her chest like a stone. But the blanket was real. She remembered it: blue wool, a frayed corner, the smell of her father's jacket when he wrapped it around her on the porch. She had lost it in the move, and she had never stopped missing it.

"You're awake," the lamp said. Its voice was quiet, almost kind.

"I want my blanket," Lucia whispered. "The blue one. I lost it."

"A small thing," the lamp said. "A small memory will do. Something you can spare."

"What memory?"

"His laugh," the lamp said. "The one you heard when he told jokes at dinner. The one that filled the kitchen. You have many memories of him. Give me that one."

Lucia's throat tightened. But the blanket was already in her mind, soft and warm and safe. "Okay," she said.

The lamp glowed. It wasn't a bright light, just a low amber pulse, like a heartbeat. Lucia felt something shift inside her, a small tug behind her eyes. Then the blanket was there, folded on the floor at her feet, blue and worn and exactly as she remembered.

She picked it up and pressed it to her face. It smelled of dust and old wool, not of her father. She tried to remember his laugh, the way his shoulders shook, the sound that made her mother roll her eyes. She could remember that he laughed. She could remember the shape of it. But the sound was gone, a hole where something warm used to be.

She stood there holding the blanket, and she did not cry. She felt too hollow for that.

***

The next morning, Elena found the blanket on Lucia's bed. She stood in the doorway with a cup of coffee in her hand, and her face went very still.

"Where did you get that?" she asked.

Lucia looked up from tying her shoes. "I found it," she said. The lie came out flat.

Elena set the coffee down on the dresser. "Lucia. Where did you get it?"

"The lamp," Lucia said. She couldn't look at her mother. "I traded a memory. Just one. His laugh. I can't hear it anymore."

Elena closed her eyes. For a moment she looked like she might cry, but she didn't. She turned and walked out of the room, and Lucia heard her footsteps go hard down the hall.

She found her mother in the front room, standing in front of the sideboard. The lamp was glowing again, a steady amber light.

"You took from my daughter," Elena said. Her voice was low and shaking. "You had no right."

"She came to me," the lamp said. "She asked. I only trade."

"I want it back. The memory. Give it back."

"I cannot return what was given freely," the lamp said. "But I can offer something else. Something larger. His voice. The way he said your name. The way he said goodnight. For a memory of his face. Just one. The one from your wedding day, perhaps. You have others."

Elena's hand went to her mouth. She stood there, breathing hard, and Lucia watched from the hallway, frozen.

"No," Elena said. "No. I won't trade my husband's face for his voice. I won't cut him into pieces."

"Then I have nothing for you," the lamp said. And its light began to dim, slowly, like a coal cooling in a grate. The amber faded to a dull red, then to almost nothing. The room grew colder, and Lucia pulled her sweater tight around her shoulders.

Elena turned and saw her in the doorway. "Go to your room," she said, but her voice wasn't angry. It was tired. "We'll talk about this later."

Lucia went. Behind her, the lamp sat dark and silent, but she could feel it waiting.

***

The house was colder the next morning. Not the cold of a broken window or a dead furnace, but a cold that lived in the walls and breathed out at odd hours. Lucia felt it on the stairs, on the kitchen tiles, in the air above her bed. She pulled her quilt up to her chin and watched her breath cloud. Her mother had gone to work early, leaving a note on the table: *Back by six. There is soup in the fridge.*

Lucia found the lamp in the living room, dark and silent. She stood over it for a long time, her arms crossed against the chill. Something was wrong with the math of it. Her blanket had come back, but her memory of her father's laugh had not simply gone missing. It had been taken, she realized, and the taking had left a hole that the cold poured into.

She sat on the floor beside the lamp and thought about the blanket. It was soft and familiar, and she had wanted it so badly. But when she held it, she had not felt warm. She had felt like a person standing in a doorway, watching a room she could not enter.

"You didn't want the memory," she said aloud, not expecting an answer. "You wanted the space it left."

The lamp did not speak. But the cold in the room deepened, just slightly, as if it had heard her and was pleased.

Lucia got up and walked through the house, touching the walls. The cold was worse near the lamp, she noticed. It thinned out the farther she went, until in the kitchen it was almost normal. The lamp was not just sitting there. It was eating. Every trade, every memory given up, made it colder and made the house weaker.

She thought of her mother's face when she refused the trade. The way she had said *I won't cut him into pieces.* Her mother had known, somehow, that the lamp's offers were a kind of butchery.

Lucia went back to the living room. "You're feeding on us," she said. "That's what you do. You take our memories and you leave the cold."

"I offer what is asked," the lamp said, its voice low and dry. "I do not take what is not given."

"You make us want to give," Lucia said. "You make the house cold so we'll trade for warmth."

The lamp was silent. The cold pressed in around her ankles.

Lucia looked at the lamp for a long moment. Then she picked it up. It was heavier than it looked, and the brass was cold against her palms. She carried it out to the back step, where the concrete was hard and the ground was frozen. She set it down and went to the shed for the hammer.

***

When she came back, her mother was standing on the step. Elena's coat was still on, her keys in her hand. She looked at the lamp, then at the hammer in Lucia's grip.

"I know," Elena said. "I felt it too. The cold. It's been getting worse."

"It's eating us," Lucia said. "Every time we trade, it gets colder. I traded my memory of his laugh, and now I can't hear it. I can't hear him laugh at all."

Elena's face crumpled, just for a second. Then she steadied. "I know, mija. I know."

"We have to break it."

"Yes," Elena said. "We do."

Lucia raised the hammer. The lamp spoke one last time, its voice quick and thin. "I can give him back. His voice. His laugh. All of it. For one memory. One small memory. You have so many."

Lucia's hands shook. The hammer felt very heavy. She thought of her father's laugh, the one she could no longer hear, and how much she wanted it back. She thought of her mother's wedding day, the memory the lamp had asked for, and how her mother had refused to give it up.

"No," Lucia said. "We keep what we have. Even the parts that hurt."

She brought the hammer down.

The brass shattered. Light burst out of it, not cold and amber but warm and white, like sunlight through a window. For a moment, the light took a shape. A tall man, broad-shouldered, with a familiar tilt to his head. He was laughing, or maybe he was just smiling, and then he was gone.

The cold lifted. It went out of the walls and the floor and the air, and the house felt like itself again. Elena let out a breath she had been holding for months. Lucia dropped the hammer and sat down hard on the step.

"I still can't hear his laugh," she said.

"I know," Elena said, sitting beside her. "But you'll remember that you loved it. That's something the lamp couldn't take."

The back door opened, and Lucia's brother Tomás stuck his head out. "What happened? The house just got warm."

"We broke the lamp," Lucia said.

Tomás looked at the shattered brass on the step. "Good," he said. "I never liked it."

They sat there together as the afternoon light faded, and the house behind them glowed with its own warmth. Lucia thought of her father, of his face and his voice and his laugh, and she held the memories close. Some of them were sharp and some were soft, and she kept them all.
