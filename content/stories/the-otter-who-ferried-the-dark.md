Sedge lay on the flat stone with her paws tucked under her chin and watched the river lantern. It hung from a bent iron pole at the end of the dock, a clay bowl of oil with a wick that had burned without fail for as long as any otter remembered. The flame was small tonight. It guttered when the wind came off the water, leaned sideways, and caught again. Sedge counted the leanings. Nine, then ten.

On the eleventh the flame went out.

"Keeper," she said, not loudly, because the old woman was already coming down the dock with her stick.

The keeper stopped beside the pole and looked up at the dead wick. She was thin as a reed and smelled of fish oil and cold tea. "That's the last of the harbor oil," she said. "I've been expecting it."

"It's just a draft," Sedge said. "We can relight it."

"Not from the lamp oil. There isn't any." The keeper tapped her stick once on the boards. "By morning the dark will take the bend. You know what that means."

Sedge did know. When the lantern went out the river turned strange. The current pulled wrong, the eddies spun the wrong way, and boats that went upriver in the dark came back empty or not at all. The older otters said the dark had a mouth and it chewed on the bend until morning. Sedge had always thought that was a story to frighten pups. Now she looked at the black water beyond the dock and was not so sure.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

"Until the moon sets. Four hours, maybe less." The keeper turned and started back toward her hut. "Come inside. There's something I need to show you."

Sedge followed, her belly low to the boards. The hut smelled of smoke and rope. The keeper lifted a clay cup from a shelf and set it on the table between them. Inside, nested in ash, was a single lump of coal the size of a walnut. It glowed faintly, a dull orange heart.

"The last coal," the keeper said. "Kept from the old fire. The lantern was built to be lit from it, and only from it."

"Then light it," Sedge said.

"It has to go into the bowl at the lantern stone. Upstream. Past the narrows." The keeper's eyes were steady. "I can't carry it. My knees are done, and the path is all roots and slick rock. Someone has to swim it up and climb the stone."

Sedge looked at the coal. It pulsed once, like a slow breath. "I'll take it," she said.

The keeper was quiet for a moment. "It's a long way, and the dark is already in the water. It will talk to you. It will tell you the coal is heavy, and that you should rest, and that no one would blame you."

"I know what the dark says," Sedge said. "I've heard it before. It's just water."

"It's not just water tonight." The keeper tied the cup into a harness of soft leather and fitted it across Sedge's back. The coal sat warm between her shoulder blades. "Go fast. Don't stop for anything that talks."

Sedge slipped into the river. The cold hit her like a wall, and under it, faint as a whisper, something said: heavy, isn't it. She kicked hard for the far bank and did not answer. Behind her the keeper's voice came across the water, thin and clear. "Past the narrows, Sedge. Past the heron. Don't let it take the coal."

The current tugged at her back, and the dark leaned close, and the river ran on toward morning.

***

The far bank was mud and reed stubble, and Sedge hauled herself out with her claws full of it. Water ran off her fur in ropes. The cup sat snug against her spine, warm as a live thing, and when she stood the leather creaked once and settled.

"Heavy, isn't it," said the river.

Sedge shook herself from nose to tail and did not answer. The bank path ran narrow between the water and a wall of black willow roots, and she took it at a lope, four feet finding the ground one after another without needing her to think about it. Above the willows the sky was the color of wet slate. Somewhere ahead an owl asked a question and got no reply.

After a while the warmth on her back began to feel like a stone. Her shoulders ached. The path bent left, and the bend was easier than the straight, and the water at the bend said, very reasonably, *the path by the water is hard. The path up the bank is soft. Rest a moment and you'll go faster after.*

Sedge stopped.

She stood in the mud with her heart going and knew, exactly and precisely, that this was the voice the keeper had warned her about. It did not sound like a monster. It sounded like her own good sense, and that was worse.

"I know what you are," she said aloud, to have something to say it to.

*I'm the river,* said the river. *I'm only being kind.*

"You're being patient. There's a difference."

She started walking again, and the voice followed her the way the current follows a stick, nudging, suggesting, and never once raising itself. Somewhere past the bend a frog went quiet as she passed. The warmth on her back had sunk deep now, into the muscle. It was not the coal that was heavy. It was the morning behind it, all the dark hours still to be got through. It would be so easy to set the cup down on a root. She could take it up again in a moment.

Her claws were on the harness strap before she knew it.

Then she thought of the keeper's face, and the way he had said *go fast*, and how his hands had shaken on the knot. Not fear for himself. He had stood on that bank for years and the light had been his whole work, and he had given it to an otter because there was no one else.

Sedge put her claws back on the ground. "Not yet," she said, and the river said nothing at all, which was somehow worse than the whispering.

***

The narrows came up on her before the light did. The bank fell away into rock, and the river squeezed itself between two shoulders of grey stone, and the sound changed from a murmur to a hard, flat rush. Sedge slowed. Mist hung over the water, and out of the mist, standing in the shallows on one leg, a heron unfolded its neck and looked at her.

It was a big one. Its beak was the length of her whole body, and its eye was yellow and did not blink, and it stood directly across the only dry way through.

"Morning," said the heron.

"Morning," Sedge said. "I need to pass."

"Everyone needs to pass." The heron shifted its weight, and the water slid around its thin legs. "You're carrying something warm."

Sedge did not look at her own back. "I'm carrying nothing that's yours."

"The narrows are mine," the heron said. "I keep them. Nothing goes through that I don't allow, and I allow very little in the dark." It tilted its head, and the yellow eye came down level with hers. "The toll is the coal. Set it on the stone and go on. You'll be lighter, and I'll be satisfied, and the river will still be here in the morning."

"If I give it to you, the lantern stays dark."

"The lantern is a stone with a bowl in it." The heron's voice was flat and patient, a voice that had never once been hurried. "The dark comes to the bend either way. Give me the coal and you live to see it. Keep it and I take it off you anyway, and you'll be slower for it."

Sedge's fur stood up along her spine. She could feel the coal's warmth pressing into her, and the water roaring in the narrows, and the heron's beak, and behind all of it the river's voice, silent now, waiting to see what she would do.

She could fight. Otters fought. She had teeth and she was fast and the heron was all legs and reach and it would be a bad, short, stupid fight on wet rock.

Or she could think.

"You keep the narrows," she said slowly. "You keep the dry way. But the water's yours too, isn't it? All of it?"

The heron said nothing.

"Then you don't need the coal," Sedge said. "You need the bend to stay dark, so the fish run blind and the frogs sit still and the narrows stay yours. But I'm not going to the bend. I'm going under it."

And she turned, and dove, and the cold took her, and the heron's beak came down through the place where she had been and struck stone.

***

Under the surface the river turned her over like a hand turning a stone. Sedge tumbled, righted herself, and struck out downstream with her eyes open and her back arched to keep the cup high. The water here was not the water she knew. It was colder, and it pulled at angles, sideways and down, as if it had opinions. She let it have her, a little, and steered with her tail.

Then the whirlpool took her.

It was not loud. That was the worst of it. One moment she was swimming and the next she was going round, and round, and the river had made itself a bowl and she was in the bottom of it. Sedge braced her forelegs and kicked and got nowhere. The cup shifted on her back. She felt the strap slide, felt the clay go light against her, and then she felt nothing there at all.

She heard it go in. A small flat sound, like a stone dropped in a well, and then the coal was gone down into the dark.

Sedge went after it.

The whirlpool did not want her to. It rolled her and squeezed her and for a terrible stretch she could not tell which way was up, and her lungs began to burn at the edges, and the burn spread, and her legs stopped doing what she asked. Somewhere above her the surface was a pale grey smear she could not reach. She thought, with a strange calm, that this was how the bend would get its dark after all, one small otter at a time.

Then her paw touched clay.

It was the cup, tipped on its side in the silt, and the coal was there beside it, still faintly warm, throwing a thin red line across the mud. Sedge closed her paw around it and kicked once, twice, and the river let her go. She broke the surface with a noise she did not have breath to make and lay there gasping, the coal pressed to her chest, her heart going like a fish on a bank.

The heron was gone.

***

The lantern stone stood where the river bent, grey and worn, with a rusted iron bowl on top of it and a wick as long as her arm. Sedge climbed out onto the rock and lay still for a moment. The bend was darker than the rest of the river. The water there had no shine on it. She could feel the fish hanging deep and the frogs gone quiet, waiting for something to change.

Her legs shook. She got up anyway.

The bowl was taller than she was. Sedge climbed the stone, found a crack for her hind feet, and hauled herself up with the coal clutched against her chest. It had gone cool now, cool and grey, and she was afraid, with a fear that sat low and heavy in her, that she had carried a dead thing all this way. But when she tipped it out into the bowl, the coal caught.

It caught the way a coal does, grudgingly, one edge first, and then the whole of it went gold.

The wick took. Light climbed it and spread out across the bend, and the dark water got its shine back, and the fish moved, and somewhere downstream a frog spoke, once, as if testing whether it was safe. Sedge lay flat on the lantern stone with her fur steaming and watched the light run out across the river in long fingers.

"Well," said the keeper from the bank. "There she is."

The old otter had come down the path with a lamp in her hand, though she did not need it now. She climbed the stone slowly and stood beside Sedge and looked at the bowl.

"You lost the cup," she said.

"I got it back."

"So you did." The keeper was quiet a moment. "The heron?"

"Went under me. I told it the narrows were its own and it could keep them."

"And it believed you?"

"It didn't stop me," Sedge said. "That's near enough."

The keeper laughed, a dry sound, and sat down on the warm stone. Below them the bend glowed, and the light held, and the river went on being a river. "You'll do," she said. "You'll do." Sedge put her head down on her paws and let herself be small for a while, and the coal burned steady, and the dark kept its distance.
