Maya was reaching for the last roll when the kitchen went black. The refrigerator's hum stopped. The little green clock on the stove vanished. For a second nobody moved.

"Nobody panic," said Dad, though his voice went up at the end.

"I'm not panicking," said Maya. "I'm just holding a roll."

Her little brother, Theo, knocked over his cup. Water spread across the table. Maya heard it drip onto the floor.

"Stay put," Dad said. "I know where the flashlight is. I think."

Maya listened to him feel his way along the counter. Something clinked. Something else rolled away and stopped.

"Found it," he said. A beam of light swung across the room. It caught Mom's face, then Theo's, then the spilled water shining on the table.

"Well," said Mom. "That's the whole street, I bet. Look."

Through the window, every house on the block was dark. No porch lights. No glow from the Millers' kitchen. Just the moon, thin and white, over the roofs.

"Can we still eat?" Theo asked.

"We can eat by flashlight," said Mom. "It's practically a picnic."

They ate. The rolls were still warm. Dad told a story about a blackout when he was a boy, and how his whole street had stood outside and talked until midnight. Theo asked if they could do that. Mom said maybe later.

Maya chewed and listened. Under the table, her foot tapped. The house felt bigger in the dark, like the rooms had grown while she wasn't looking.

***

Dad stood up and went to the drawer where Mom kept the candles. Maya heard him open it, then close it, then open it again.

"Wrong drawer," he said.

"Try the one by the sink," said Mom.

He found a jar with a candle stub inside, half used, the wick black and bent. He lit it with the flashlight's help and set it on the table. The flame leaned, then stood up straight.

"Maya," he said. "I need you to do something."

She looked up. "What?"

"Mrs. Patterson next door. She lives alone, and she's older. She might not have a light."

Maya looked at the jar. The flame was small, but it held. "You want me to take it over?"

"Just to the porch," said Dad. "Knock. Ask if she's all right. If she is, come straight back."

"What if she's not?" asked Maya.

"Then come tell me," he said. "And I'll go over. But I have a feeling she'd rather see a friendly face first."

Maya took the jar. The glass was warm against her palms. The night air coming through the screen door smelled like rain and cut grass.

"Take the flashlight too," said Mom.

"I have the candle," said Maya.

"Take both," said Mom, and Maya did.

The screen door squeaked when she pushed it open. Behind her, Theo called, "Bring back a cookie!" and Mom shushed him.

Maya stepped onto the porch. The candle threw a little circle of light around her feet, and the flashlight beam reached further, out to the walk and the dark shape of the hedge.

"Straight there and straight back," said Dad from the doorway.

"I know," said Maya. She didn't feel brave. She felt like somebody had handed her something important and was watching to see what she'd do with it. She started down the steps, one at a time, holding the jar steady.

The street was a long black river with no lights on either bank.  
Maya stopped at the bottom of her steps and listened.  
Somewhere down the block, a man's voice called out, "You all right over there, Ruth?"  
A woman's voice called back, "We're fine! Got the baby and a flashlight!"  

Other sounds floated up the block. A screen door banged. Someone laughed, low and easy.  
A dog whined once, then quieted.  
Maya shifted the jar to her other hand. The candle flame leaned, then straightened.  
She walked past the Hendersons' dark windows, where a single orange glow flickered deep inside.  

At the corner, Mr. Alvarez from number twelve stood on his porch with a battery lantern held high.  
"Need anything, Maya?" he called.  
"Just checking on Mrs. Patterson," she said.  
"Good girl," he said. "We've got water if you need it. And batteries."  

Maya nodded and kept going. The flashlight beam bounced along the sidewalk cracks.  
A cat's eyes flashed green from under a parked car. It blinked and was gone.  
Up and down the block, she heard small kindnesses. Someone sharing a lighter. Someone counting heads.  
The dark wasn't empty after all. It was full of people, quietly taking care.  

Mrs. Patterson's house sat at the end of the street, behind a tangle of roses.  
No light showed in any window.  
Maya climbed the porch steps slowly. The wood creaked under her sneakers.  
She knocked, and the sound was small in the big dark.  

***

No one answered.  
Maya knocked again, harder.  
"Mrs. Patterson?" she called. "It's Maya. From next door."  
A long pause. Then a voice, thin and close, said, "Maya?"  

"Yes," said Maya. "Can I come in?"  
"The door's unlocked," said Mrs. Patterson. "I'm right here. On the sofa."  
Maya pushed the door open. The flashlight beam swept across a tidy room.  
Mrs. Patterson sat on the sofa with her hands folded in her lap.  

"I can't find my candles," she said. "I know they're in the kitchen drawer. But I got turned around."  
"That's okay," said Maya. "I brought one."  
She held up the jar. The candle flame made a warm circle on the wall.  
Mrs. Patterson let out a breath. "Oh," she said. "Oh, that's nice."  

Maya set the jar on the coffee table. Then she sat down beside Mrs. Patterson.  
"Are you scared?" asked Maya.  
"A little," said Mrs. Patterson. "The dark makes me think of being small. And alone."  
"Me too," said Maya. "But my dad said you'd rather see a friendly face."  

Mrs. Patterson smiled. "Your dad is a smart man."  
They sat together. The candle flickered between them.  
Outside, someone down the street started singing a song Maya's grandma used to sing.  
Mrs. Patterson hummed along, soft and off-key.  

"I used to know all the words," she said.  
"Me too," said Maya. "My grandma taught me."  
"Then we'll hum it together," said Mrs. Patterson.  
And they did. The dark around them felt smaller and smaller.

They hummed until the song ran out. Then the quiet came back, but it wasn't the heavy kind anymore. Mrs. Patterson reached over and touched the jar. "My mother had a candle like this," she said. "She lit it every night during the war. Said it kept the dark from getting ideas."

"Did it work?" asked Maya.

"Most nights," said Mrs. Patterson. "Some nights the dark got ideas anyway. But she'd light it again the next night."

Maya thought about that. "My dad says courage isn't not being scared. It's being scared and doing the thing anyway."

Mrs. Patterson nodded slowly. "He is a smart man." She looked at the candle. "I think I forgot that. After my Henry passed, I stopped lighting candles. Seemed silly."

"It's not silly," said Maya.

"No," said Mrs. Patterson. "I suppose it isn't."

They sat for a while without talking. Maya could hear the wind outside, and the creak of the porch swing next door. Down the street, someone laughed. It was a good sound.

"What was your Henry like?" asked Maya.

Mrs. Patterson smiled. "He whistled. All the time. He whistled while he made coffee. He whistled while he tied his shoes. Drove me crazy."

"Do you miss it?"

"Every day," said Mrs. Patterson. "But tonight I remembered the good part. That's something."

Maya leaned her head back against the couch. The candle flame wobbled, then stood straight. She felt warm, even though the house was cold. "I'll come visit you," she said. "When the lights are on. If you want."

Mrs. Patterson's eyes got shiny. "I would like that very much," she said.

***

Then the lights came back. All at once. The lamp on the side table blinked on. The refrigerator hummed in the kitchen. Down the street, someone cheered.

Maya blinked. The room looked different in the light. Smaller, but friendly. She saw a bookshelf with dusty photos. A knitting basket by the chair. A plate with two cookies on it.

"Well," said Mrs. Patterson. "There we go." She stood up and turned off the lamp. "No sense wasting it."

Maya picked up her jar. The candle had burned low. "I should go home," she said. "My parents will worry."

"Of course," said Mrs. Patterson. She walked Maya to the door. On the porch, the air smelled like rain. The street was full of light again, and a few neighbors stood outside talking.

"Thank you for the candle," said Mrs. Patterson. "And for the company."

"Thank you for the song," said Maya.

Mrs. Patterson waved from the porch as Maya ran down the steps. The walk home was short, but Maya took her time. She looked at the houses she passed. In one window, a family was eating dinner. In another, a man was reading a book. She had lived here her whole life, but she had never really seen them.

At her own door, her father was waiting. "How was she?" he asked.

"Good," said Maya. "She knew the song too."

Her father smiled. "I figured she might."

Maya went inside. The kitchen smelled like spaghetti. Her mother was setting the table again. "Everything okay?" she asked.

"Yeah," said Maya. She put the jar on the counter. The candle was almost gone, just a puddle of wax. But it had done its job.

That night, Maya left her bedroom door open. She could hear her parents talking in the kitchen. She could hear the wind in the trees. And somewhere down the street, she thought she heard Mrs. Patterson humming. Just a little. Just enough to know she wasn't alone.
