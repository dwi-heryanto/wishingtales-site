Elara was on the roof when the star fell. She had climbed out her window to watch the sky, which was what she did on clear nights when sleep would not come. The stars over the village were thick as spilled salt. Then one of them moved.

It did not streak. It dropped, slow and steady, like a coin sinking through water. Elara held her breath and followed it with her eyes until it vanished behind the black hump of the hill.

"The old well," she whispered.

She was down the ladder and across the yard before her feet had decided to go. The grass was cold and wet. The path to the hill was silver with moon. At the top stood the well, its stones furred with moss, its rope long gone.

Elara leaned over the rim. Far below, a small light glowed. It was not the moon's reflection. It pulsed, faintly, like something breathing.

"Hello down there," she said.

The light did not answer. Elara found the old iron ladder bolted to the inside wall and tested the first rung with her foot. It held. She climbed down into the cool dark, rung by rung, until her shoes touched water.

The star sat on a ledge just above the waterline. It was no bigger than a plum, and it was warm. When she cupped her hands around it, the light spilled between her fingers and painted the well stones gold.

"You fell a long way," she said softly.

The star pulsed once, as if it agreed.

***

Elara carried the star home inside her lantern. She had emptied out the candle first and set the little light on the metal floor. It filled the glass with a glow the color of honey.

Her grandmother was still up, as she always was, mending a net by the stove.

"You are out late," said her grandmother, without looking up.

"I found something," Elara said. She set the lantern on the table between them.

Her grandmother's needle stopped. She leaned forward, and the firelight moved on her face.

"Where did you get that?"

"It fell into the old well. I climbed down and fetched it."

Her grandmother was quiet for a long moment. Then she sighed, and it was the sigh of someone remembering a thing they had hoped to forget.

"The sky will miss it," she said.

"It is only one star," said Elara. "There are hundreds."

"There were hundreds and one," her grandmother said. "Put it back, child. Before the sky comes looking."

Elara looked at the lantern. The little light pulsed against the glass, warm and patient, and she thought of the cold walk back up the hill.

"In the morning," she said.

Her grandmother shook her head slowly and picked up her needle again.

Elara woke in the dark because the dark had changed.

Her lantern still glowed on the shelf, but the corners of her room had gone gray and thick, like breath on a window. Down the lane, the dogs had stopped barking. She sat up and listened to the silence.

Something moved past the window. It was not a person. It was a long smear of shade, thin as a shadow on a wall, and it slid along the fence and stopped where the light fell.

"Grandmother," Elara whispered.

Her grandmother was already at the door with her shawl pulled tight. "They've come," she said quietly. "The sky sent its hungry ones to fetch what was taken."

Elara pressed her nose to the glass. More shadows gathered at the village edge, where the lamplight ended. They did not walk. They leaned, like grass bends under wind, all of them leaning toward Elara's house.

A cold crawled up her arms. It was not winter cold. It had a pull to it, gentle and steady, as if a hook had caught her sleeve and was drawing her out into the dark.

"Don't open the door," said her grandmother, catching her wrist.

"I wasn't going to." Elara rubbed her arms. "What do they want?"

"The light you carry. It doesn't belong here, so they've come to tug it home. And they'll tug you with it if you stand too close."

Elara looked at the lantern. The star inside was bright and warm and hers. She had found it. She had climbed for it.

"Then I'll give it back," she said. "First thing. Before the sun."

Her grandmother let go of her wrist. "Take the rope," she said. "And go quickly."

***

Elara ran up the hill with the lantern bumping at her side. The shadows followed. They did not hurry. They flowed along the hedges and the quiet walls, always at the edge of the light, and the cold came off them like air from a cellar.

The old well sat at the top of the hill among the nettles. Its stones were gray, and its mouth was wide and black, and somewhere far down, water breathed.

She tied the rope to the post and let it down hand over hand. It went on and on. Then it stopped, slack, and she knew it had not reached the bottom.

"Too short," she said.

She looked for another rope. There was none. She looked down the well, and the dark inside it looked back, deeper than any night she had ever seen.

Then the cold grew teeth. Shadows pooled around the well's rim, thick and slow, and the lantern light shrank against them. One of them brushed her hand, and her fingers went numb.

Elara pulled her arm back. "I'm trying," she said, to the star, to the sky, to anyone.

The shadows pressed closer. Her breath came out in puffs of white. The way down was shut, and the way back was a long cold walk, and the star in her lantern burned on, patient and bright, not knowing it was lost.

She sat down hard on the stones and held the lantern against her chest. "Grandmother will know," she said. "She always knows."

Grandmother was still awake, sitting by the stove with her shawl pulled tight. She took one look at Elara's face and stood up without a word. Elara set the lantern on the table and opened the little door. The star's light spilled out across the wood, and the shadows on the wall leaned away from it.

"You found it," Grandmother said. "And it found you."

"It fell in the well," Elara said. "I can't reach the bottom. And there are shadows now, at the edge of the village. They came for it."

Grandmother nodded slowly. "They always do. A fallen star is bright and small, and the dark would rather have it than lose it." She crossed to the tin chest and lifted out a flat sheet of metal, polished until it shone like still water. "Your grandfather kept this for the boats. He called it a moon-mirror. It holds light and gives it back."

Elara touched the cool tin. "What do I do with it?"

"You send the star home," Grandmother said. "But the well is deep, and no arm is long enough. So we bounce the light. The mirror below, the sky above, and the star between them." She smiled. "Fetch the ladder. We have work before dawn."

They carried the mirror out into the cold. Elara climbed down the well's first rungs and wedged the tin at an angle, so its face looked up into the night. Grandmother held the lantern high above the rim.

"Now," she said. "Open it. And let go."

***

Elara opened the lantern door. The star rose out of it, a small warm ball of white, and floated toward the mirror. For a moment nothing happened. Then the tin caught the light and threw it up, clean and straight, and the star went with it, riding its own bright reflection.

Up and up it climbed, past the well's mouth, past Grandmother's upturned face, past the top of the mill chimney. The shadows at the village edge stirred and thinned. One by one they pulled back from the fields, and the cold that had gripped Elara's fingers let go like a hand unclenching.

"There," Grandmother whispered. "The sky has been missing that one."

The star slowed high above them and settled into the dark between two others, a small steady point that had been an empty space a moment before. Elara thought she heard it, faint as a kettle cooling, a soft contented hum. Then the night was quiet again.

On the well's stone rim, where the star had rested, lay one warm grey pebble. Elara picked it up. It was gentle in her palm, no brighter than a coal, but it held the night's small heat.

"Keep it," Grandmother said. "Some things you borrow, and some things stay."

They walked home together. The village slept under a whole sky. Elara put the pebble on her windowsill, and in the morning it was still warm.
