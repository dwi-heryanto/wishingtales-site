Maya held tight to her grandmother's sleeve as they pushed through the evening market. Paper lanterns swung above the stalls, painting the dark in red and gold. The air smelled of frying dough and pine needles.

"Keep close, little bird," her grandmother said, her voice warm over the crowd's roar. "Our stall is just past the puppet show."

Maya nodded and stepped over a coil of rope. Her heart tapped fast. So many people—laughing, calling, haggling—and all of them taller than her.

At the stall, her grandmother lifted a wooden box onto the counter. Inside, a dozen lanterns nested like eggs, each one folded flat and painted with moons and fishes.

"You unfold the small ones," her grandmother said, handing her a paper circle no bigger than a plate. "Gently now. They tear if you rush."

Maya hooked her finger under the first fold. The paper crackled open, and a tiny goldfish bloomed across it. She set it on its wire frame and hung it on the rack beside the others.

"Good," said her grandmother. "Now the next."

One by one, the rack filled. Maya's nervousness loosened a little with each lantern she opened. When a customer stopped to admire a moon-painted one, her grandmother winked at her.

"See? The crowd is just people," she said. "And people love a good light."

***

A gust of wind barreled down the market lane. It rattled the stall's canvas and set every lantern swaying. Maya grabbed the edge of the counter as a paper crane spun past her nose.

Then the last lantern—the big one, painted with a silver fish—ripped free of its hook. It tumbled into the air and sailed over the heads of the crowd.

"Grandma!" Maya shouted. "The fish lantern!"

Her grandmother looked up from a customer. "Let it go, little bird. The wind will have its fun."

But Maya was already moving. The lantern dipped and rose like a kite with no string, and something in her chest pulled tight and followed it. She ducked under a table of scarves and ran.

"Wait!" she called, though the lantern couldn't hear her.

Behind her, her grandmother's voice rose over the noise. "Maya! Stay where I can see you!"

Maya glanced back once. Her grandmother's face was small and worried between the stalls. But the lantern glowed ahead, a brave little fish swimming through the dark, and Maya couldn't bear to lose it.

"I'll bring it back!" she yelled, and plunged into the crowd.

Maya pushed through a forest of elbows and shopping bags. A man in a straw hat stepped in front of her, and she dodged sideways. "Sorry!" she called, and kept running. The lantern bobbed ahead, its silver fish tail flicking light across the tops of the stalls.

She passed a puppet show where a wooden dragon was arguing with a wooden knight. "I was here first!" the dragon squeaked. "You're a puppet!" the knight squeaked back. Maya didn't stop to see who won.

Next came the noodle stand, all steam and clatter. A woman lifted a huge pot, and the smell of ginger wrapped around Maya's face. "Careful, little one!" the woman said, pulling back the pot just in time. Maya ducked under her arm and kept going.

"Excuse me—pardon—coming through!" she gasped. The lantern dipped low, then soared again. It drifted past a stall selling paper fans, past a man juggling oranges, past a baby in a sling who laughed at the light. Maya's legs ached, but she didn't slow down.

At the edge of the crowd, the lantern paused. It hung in the air as if deciding something, then floated toward the dark line of trees beyond the market. Maya ran after it, her breath loud in her ears. The noise of the market faded behind her. Now there was only the lantern, and the quiet, and the tall shape of an old oak tree.

***

The lantern bumped against the oak's branches and stuck there, tangled in a fork of twigs. Its light flickered through the leaves like a trapped star. Maya stopped at the foot of the tree and looked up. The trunk went on and on. The lowest branch was far above her head.

"Oh no," she whispered. Her stomach did a slow, cold flip. She had never liked heights. Even standing on a chair made her knees feel strange. But the lantern glowed up there, patient and bright, and she thought of her grandmother's face between the stalls.

She reached for the lowest branch. Her fingers barely brushed the bark. "Jump," she told herself. "Just jump." Maya bent her knees and sprang. Her hands caught the branch, and she hung there, feet swinging. The bark was rough and warm. She pulled herself up and sat, breathing hard.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. That wasn't so bad." But when she looked down, the ground seemed very far away. Her hands went tight on the branch. The lantern glowed above her, two branches higher.

"You can do this," she said out loud. Her voice sounded small in the dark. She stood up on the branch, one hand on the trunk. The next branch was just within reach. She stretched, and her fingers closed around it. Up she went, branch by branch, until the lantern was right in front of her.

Carefully, she untangled the wire handle from the twigs. The lantern swung free, its light washing over her face. Maya held it close and looked down. The ground was a long way off, but she had climbed all this way. Going down would be the easy part.

Going down was not the easy part. Maya turned onto her stomach and slid backward, feeling with her toes for the branch below. The lantern bumped against her wrist. "Slow," she whispered. "Slow and steady." Branch by branch, she climbed down, until her feet hit the soft ground. She landed with a thump and hugged the lantern to her chest.

When she looked up, the market had changed. Stalls were closing. Metal shutters rattled down. People streamed toward the exits, carrying folded tables and empty crates. The puppet show tent was dark. The noodle stand's awning had been rolled up. Maya's stomach tightened. "Grandma?" she said, but the crowd swallowed her voice.

She walked past the empty tea stall, past the man stacking chairs. Nothing looked the same in the dark. "I came this way," she told herself, but the aisles twisted like a maze. Every corner looked like the one before. Her eyes stung. The lantern felt heavy in her hands.

A woman hurried by with a cart. "Excuse me," Maya said. "Do you know where the lantern stall is?" The woman shook her head and kept walking. Maya stood still, listening to the shutters and the footsteps. Then she heard something else. A voice, calling her name. "Maya? Maya!" It was far away, but she knew it. Her grandmother.

***

Maya ran toward the voice. "Grandma!" she shouted. "I'm here!" She dodged a stack of crates and nearly tripped on a rope. The lantern swung wildly in her hand. "Maya!" came the call again, closer now. She rounded a corner and saw her grandmother standing at the end of the aisle, her white hair loose from its pin, her hands cupped around her mouth.

"There you are!" Grandma said. She rushed forward and caught Maya by the shoulders. "I turned around and you were gone. Don't ever do that again." Her voice was sharp, but her hands were gentle. Maya felt her face get hot.

"The lantern," Maya said. "The wind took it. I had to get it back." She held it up. The paper was a little wrinkled, but the light still burned steady inside. Grandma looked at the lantern, then at Maya's scratched hands and dusty knees.

"You climbed a tree," Grandma said. It was not a question.

"I was scared," Maya said. "But I did it anyway." Grandma was quiet for a moment. Then she smiled, just a little. "Well," she said. "Then let's light it one last time." They walked back to the stall together, and Grandma set the lantern on its hook. Maya struck the match. The flame caught, and the lantern glowed, warm and bright, as the market went dark around them.
