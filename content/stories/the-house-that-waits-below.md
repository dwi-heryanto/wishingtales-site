The oars creaked in the locks, and the reservoir lay flat as a sheet of tin under the last light. Mara rowed with her back to the shore, watching the hills shrink behind her. She had taken her grandfather's boat without asking, and the rope still hung from the dock post like a question she had not answered.

"You'll want to turn back before full dark," her mother had said that morning, not looking up from the sink. "The water's not a place for you after sunset."

Mara had said nothing. She had packed a flashlight, a coil of rope, and the photograph of her grandmother standing in a kitchen doorway, smiling at someone outside the frame. The photograph was older than Mara, and the kitchen in it was tiled in green, and the door behind her grandmother opened onto a garden with a plum tree. All of that was under forty feet of water now.

She had heard the stories, of course. Everyone in the valley had heard them. The village had been drowned on purpose, the dam built, the houses left to fill like jars. Most people said the water was just water. A few said otherwise, and those few spoke quietly, and only after dark.

Mara's grandmother had died the year the water came. She had refused to leave. That was the part nobody told the same way twice. Some said she had been sick. Some said she had simply sat down at her table and waited. The only thing everyone agreed on was that they had never found her.

A loon called from somewhere near the eastern shore, and the sound rolled over the water like a stone. Mara shipped the oars and let the boat drift. Ahead, the surface broke in a low, dark line. The old church steeple, she knew, though from this angle it looked less like a steeple and more like a finger held up in warning.

Her breath came short. She had not expected the fear to be so physical, a cold band around her ribs. But she had come this far, and the light was going, and turning back now would mean turning back forever.

She picked up the oars again and pulled toward the steeple.

***

The sun had gone entirely by the time she reached it. The steeple rose from the water at a slight tilt, its stone green with algae, its tip broken where lightning had found it years ago. Around it, the reservoir lay black and still, and the hills had folded into the dark like hands closing.

Mara tied the boat to the steeple with the rope, looping it twice around the stone. The knot felt childish in her hands, but it held. She sat still and listened.

At first there was only the water lapping at the stone, soft and regular. Then a sound came up through the hull, a low vibration that she felt in her teeth before she heard it. It was a bell. Not a bell ringing in air, but a bell ringing underwater, its note flattened and stretched, as if it were being rung in a room full of honey.

"That's not possible," she said aloud, and her voice sounded thin and foolish over the water.

The bell rang again. Three slow strokes, then silence. Mara leaned over the side and shone her flashlight down. The beam cut through the water, but the water was not clear. It was brown and grainy, full of drifting silt, and the light died in it after a few feet.

She thought of her grandmother's kitchen, the green tiles, the plum tree. She thought of the photograph in her pocket, now damp at the edges.

"Gran," she said, not loudly. "Are you down there?"

The water did not answer. The bell did not ring again. But the boat began to move, very slightly, turning on its rope as if something below had exhaled.

Mara checked the knot. It was still tight. She checked the flashlight. It was still on.

Then she took a long breath, pulled off her shoes, and looked at the dark water one more time before she went in.

The cold hit her like a hand pressed flat to her chest. Mara kicked down, and the beam of her flashlight swung wild across silt and drowned fence posts. Ten feet. Fifteen. Her ears ached. Then the bottom tilted, and she saw the village.

Walls stood where walls had stood. A lane ran between them, its paving stones furred with green. A gate hung open on one hinge, moving gently in a current she could not feel. The water was not deep here, not the way it should have been, and the light reached further than it had any right to. She passed a shopfront with its sign still lettered above the door, and a bicycle leaning against a wall, rusted into lace.

And then the house.

It sat at the end of the lane with its door shut and its windows bright. Yellow light poured out of the kitchen, steady and warm, the kind of light that came from a bulb and not from any water she knew. A shape moved behind the glass. An arm, a shoulder, the tilt of a head.

Mara stopped swimming. Her lungs began to complain, and she let herself rise a little, then sank again. The figure crossed the window once more. It was a woman, and she was setting something on the table.

Mara's chest burned. She turned for the surface, broke through it gasping, and clung to the boat until her breathing slowed. The steeple stood black against the last of the sky. The bell did not ring.

"You imagined it," she said. The words came out ragged. "You're cold and you're scared and you imagined it."

She looked at her hands. They were shaking.

"Gran," she said again, quieter, "if that's you, I need to know."

Nothing answered but the lap of water on the boat. She thought of the photograph in her pocket. She thought of the last time she had seen her grandmother alive, in a hospital bed, small and cross and tired of being fussed over. That woman had never once asked anyone to stay.

Mara pulled her shoes back on, then kicked them off again. She took three deep breaths, filling herself to the brim, and went down a second time. This time she kept her eyes open. This time she followed the lane straight to the door.

***

The door was not locked. It swung inward when she pushed, and the water moved with her into the house as if it had been waiting for an invitation. Inside, the hall was dry.

Mara stood in the doorway with her feet in the flood and her head above it, and the air on her face was warm. Warm and dry and smelling of onions and thyme. The wallpaper was the same paper she remembered, faded roses on a cream ground. A coat hung on a hook. A pair of boots sat beneath it, neatly paired, dry as dust.

"You'll let the cold in," said a voice from the kitchen. "Shut it behind you."

Mara did not move. Her heart was going like a bird in a box.

"Gran?" she said.

The kitchen door was open. Light lay across the hall floor in a long yellow rectangle. A chair scraped. Footsteps came, slow and certain, and her grandmother stood in the doorway with a dish towel over one shoulder.

She looked exactly as she had looked. Not as she had at the end, thin and grey, but as she had been years before: broad through the shoulders, hair pinned up, apron strings tied twice. Her hands were red from the water. Her eyes were the same.

"Well," she said. "You took your time."

Mara's mouth opened and nothing came out. Her grandmother clicked her tongue and went back into the kitchen.

"Sit," she called. "It's nearly ready."

Mara stepped inside and closed the door. The water stayed behind it, pressed against the wood like a thing listening. She walked down the hall, and the floor was dry under her bare feet, and the photographs on the wall were the same photographs, her mother as a girl on a pony, her grandmother laughing at something out of frame. In the kitchen, the table was laid for two. A pot steamed on the stove. The green tiles were exactly green.

She sat down. Her grandmother set a plate in front of her and then sat across the table, folding her hands.

"You drowned," Mara said. "They told me you drowned."

"I did," said her grandmother, calmly. "And here I am. Eat something. You're all bones and gooseflesh."

Mara looked at the plate. It was stew, thick and brown, and it smelled like every winter of her childhood at once. She did not pick up the fork.

"How long have you been here?"

Her grandmother smiled, and the smile was fond and a little tired, and it did not reach the corners the way it used to.

"Long enough to learn the tides," she said. "Long enough to keep the light on for you."

"Stay for supper," her grandmother said. "You've come all this way."

Mara's hands were cold on the edge of the table. The stew steamed between them. Outside the kitchen window, the water was black and full of night, but inside the room the lamp burned steady and yellow. "I can't stay," she said. "I have to get back before the light goes."

"The light's already gone," said her grandmother. "You rowed out here in the dark. You know that."

It was true. The sun had been down for an hour when she pushed off from the jetty, and the reservoir had swallowed the last of it before she reached the steeple. Still, she had a torch in the boat, and the boat had a rope, and the rope was tied to something that floated. That was the whole of her plan, and it had seemed enough then.

"Just one bowl," her grandmother said. "For company."

Mara looked at the stew. It was the colour of old wood, and the smell of it made her stomach turn over with hunger. She had not eaten since morning. She picked up the fork and put it down again.

"Why did you never come back?" she asked.

Her grandmother's face did not change. "I did come back," she said. "I'm here."

"That's not what I mean."

"I know what you mean." The old woman leaned forward, and her hands were folded on the table, and the skin of them was loose and pale, but the nails were clean and trimmed, and that was somehow worse than if they had been ragged. "I came back the night the water rose. I stood on the roof of this house and I watched the village go under, and I thought, well, there's nothing left to row toward. So I stayed."

"You drowned," Mara said again, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"I did."

"Then how are you sitting there?"

Her grandmother smiled, and the smile was the same tired, fond smile from before. "Because you came," she said. "You're the first."

Mara's foot moved under the table. It was wet. She looked down and saw that the floor, which had been dry when she walked in, was now covered in a thin sheet of water, black and still, reflecting the lamp. It had reached the legs of her chair. It had reached the legs of her grandmother's chair, and her grandmother had not moved.

"The water's coming in," Mara said.

"It does that," said her grandmother. "It's been coming in for years. You get used to it."

"You get used to it?" Mara stood up. The water was over her ankles now, and cold, so cold it ached. "How can you get used to it?"

"Sit down, Mara. Eat your supper."

"No." She took a step back, and the water pulled at her legs. "How long have you been here?"

"Long enough," her grandmother said, and this time her voice was not calm. It was thin and frayed at the edges, like the bell she had heard ringing under the water, like something that had been ringing a very long time. "Long enough to know that no one comes. Long enough to keep the light on for you. And now you're here, and you're going to sit down and eat, because that's what family does."

Mara looked at the plate. The stew was still steaming, but the steam was thinner now, and the smell had changed. It smelled like the reservoir on a hot day, like weeds and old mud and something turning over at the bottom. The water was at her knees. Her grandmother's chair was half submerged, and her grandmother sat in it as if it were a dry kitchen on a Sunday afternoon, her hands folded, her eyes fixed on Mara's face.

"Sit down," she said again.

Mara ran.

***

The water in the hall was chest-high. It had come up faster than she thought possible, and the photographs on the wall were underwater now, her mother on the pony, her grandmother laughing, all of them swimming behind glass. She pushed through it, and it was heavy and cold and it wanted her to stay, and she kicked against it with everything she had. Behind her, in the kitchen, her grandmother was still talking. "Don't go," she said. "Don't go, Mara, it's so quiet here." Her voice was not angry. It was lonely, and that was the worst thing.

Mara reached the front door. It was open, the way she had left it, and beyond it was the flooded street and the dark shape of the church steeple and, far above, the pale grey of the sky, where the moon was trying to break through. She pulled herself through the doorway and into open water, and she swam.

The cold took her breath first. Then the dark took her sight, and she was swimming blind, one hand out in front of her, feeling for the rope. She had tied it to the steeple, she remembered that, she had tied it with a double knot because her father had taught her that, and if she could just find it she would be all right. Her lungs burned. Something brushed her ankle, soft and cold, and she kicked and did not look. The bell was ringing again, low and slow, and it was not a bell at all, she understood that now. It was her grandmother's voice, calling her back.

Her hand closed on the rope.

She pulled herself along it, hand over hand, and the water fought her, and her grandmother called, and the lights of the drowned house glowed below her like a lamp in a window on a winter night, warm and yellow and waiting. For one moment, she wanted to turn around. It would be so easy. She could go back and sit at the table and eat the stew and never be cold again.

She climbed the rope instead.

Her head broke the surface. She gasped, and the air was thin and sharp and real, and she grabbed the side of the boat and hauled herself over the gunwale and lay in the bottom of it, shaking, staring up at the sky. The moon had come out. It was small and white and very far away, and she had never been so glad to see anything in her life.

Below her, the water was black. The lights were going out one by one, the kitchen window, the hall, the little glow at the door, until there was nothing but the steeple breaking the surface and the bell, which rang once more and then was silent.

Mara found the oars. Her hands were numb, and the oars were heavy, but she rowed. She rowed until the steeple was a thin dark line behind her, and then nothing at all. The reservoir lay flat and quiet under the moon, and somewhere under it, in the green tiles and the laid table and the photographs, her grandmother sat and waited for someone else.

When she reached the jetty, her father was standing there with a torch, and he did not ask where she had been. He just took the rope and pulled her in, and she stepped onto the boards, and the boards were solid, and the night was cold, and she was home.
