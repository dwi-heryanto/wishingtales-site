Ivy first noticed the clock at 3:17 because the bakery bell rang eight, and the clock said a quarter past three. The town clock had never been wrong in her lifetime. It was the kind of clock that kept the whole town honest, and now it had stopped at an hour that didn't exist. She stood on the cobbles with her school bag slipping off her shoulder. She counted the chimes again. Eight. Definitely eight.

She ran the rest of the way home, past the fishmonger and the greengrocer, to the narrow shop where her grandfather sat at his bench, a jeweller's loupe screwed into one eye. He was polishing the brass of a carriage clock, but his hands weren't moving.

"Grandad, the clock's stopped," she said.

"Is it?" He didn't look up.

"It says three seventeen. The bakery just rang eight."

"Clocks stop." He set the carriage clock down and picked up a cloth. "They're machines."

"Not our clock. Not ever." Ivy pulled off her coat and hung it on the peg by the door, watching him. He had a way of going still when he was worried, and he was very still now. "Aren't you going to look at it?"

"After supper." He turned back to the bench. "Wash your hands."

At supper he talked about the weather and the price of brass and a customer who wanted a cuckoo clock for a house with no birds. Ivy let him talk. She ate her potatoes and watched the way his eyes kept sliding to the window, to the tower above the rooftops, as if he could feel the stopped hands through the wall.

"Grandad," she said, when he'd run out of things to say. "Why won't you look at it?"

He put down his fork. For a moment he looked old, older than the clock tower, older than the town. "Because some clocks," he said, "are better left stopped."

That was all he would say. Ivy cleared the plates and he sat by the fire with his hands folded. Every few minutes he glanced at the clock on the mantel, which was still ticking, and then at the dark window, and then back again.

***

The tower key hung on a nail in the shop, and Ivy had never used it. She took it at midnight, when her grandfather's breathing had gone slow and even in the room below, and she went out into the cold with her coat over her nightdress.

The tower door opened with a groan. Inside, the stair spiralled up into darkness. She climbed with one hand on the cold stone and the other holding her phone's torch. Dust and cobwebs and the smell of old oil. At the top, the clock room was full of moonlight and the great brass works stood silent, a forest of gears and rods and weights that should have been moving.

She shone the torch across the mechanism. Nothing was broken. Every wheel and pinion was where it should be, clean and oiled and ready. She walked around the frame, counting the gears she knew by heart from the diagrams her grandfather had drawn for her when she was small. The great wheel. The strike train. The escapement. And then the space where the hour gear should sit, a gap like a missing tooth.

The gear was gone. In its place, wedged onto the arbor, was a folded slip of paper. Ivy's fingers were shaking as she worked it free and unfolded it in the torchlight. The handwriting was thin and spidery and very black.

An hour for an hour.

She read it three times. The paper was cold, colder than the tower air, and it smelled faintly of something sweet and burnt. She turned it over. Nothing on the back.

"Grandad," she whispered, and the word went up into the bell chamber and came back changed.

She put the note in her pocket and climbed down the stairs faster than she'd climbed up. Outside, the town was dark and quiet, and above her the clock face showed 3:17, and the hands had not moved. She ran home with the note clutched in her fist. She did not look back at the tower, because she had the feeling that something in it was looking back at her.

The baker was first. Ivy found him stacking yesterday's loaves in the window, his hands white to the wrist. "Mr. Pell," she said, "when did the clock stop?" He didn't turn around. "Clock's been stopped since Tuesday, girl. Everyone knows that." "It says 3:17. It's an hour behind. Why would it stop an hour behind?" Now he looked at her, and his face did something complicated. "Your grandad would know. Ask him." "I did. He wouldn't say." Mr. Pell wiped his hands on his apron, slowly, like he was buying time. "Look. Years back, before you were born, there was a bad winter. The river froze and the fever came through. Your grandad was up at the tower day and night. Then one morning the clock was wrong, just for an hour, and by evening the fever had broken. Nobody asked why. We were too glad." He turned back to his loaves. "Go home, Ivy."

She didn't go home. The postmistress, Mrs. Cray, was locking up, and she was easier to read. "The clock?" She laughed, but it was a thin sound. "I was a girl when it happened. A stranger came to town, tall, in a grey coat. He stood under the tower and your grandad went out to meet him. They talked a long while. Next day the stranger was gone, and the clock had lost an hour. Your grandad never spoke of it. But he stopped winding the tower clock himself after that. Said it kept its own time now." "Kept its own time?" "That's what he said." Mrs. Cray's keys jingled as she turned. "I'd leave it, Ivy. Some things you don't dig up."

But Ivy had the note in her pocket, and it was still cold. She walked to the far end of town, where the old workshop stood with its windows papered over. The door was locked, but the lock was old and the wood was soft. Inside, dust lay thick on the benches. Her grandad's tools hung in neat rows, and on the wall was a chalk tally of something, hundreds of marks, each one a little stroke. At the bottom, in fresher chalk, someone had drawn a single line and circled it. Ivy counted the strokes. Three hundred and sixty-five. A year of days. She felt the cold of the note against her leg and understood that it was not a warning. It was a receipt.

***

Her grandad was in the kitchen with the lamp turned low, his hands wrapped around a cup he wasn't drinking from. Ivy put the note on the table between them. He looked at it for a long time. "I wondered when you'd find that," he said.

"Tell me." She sat down across from him and made herself small and still, the way she did when she wanted the truth.

He set the cup down. "You were six months old. The fever took half the town that winter, and it took you. Your mother was beside herself. I sat up with you three nights, and on the third night a man came to the door. Grey coat. He said he could give me an hour, just one, where the fever would turn. But an hour of the town's time would go to him, and one day he'd come to collect." He rubbed his face. "I said yes. I didn't even ask what collecting meant."

"And the clock stopped at 3:17."

"It stopped when I said yes. The hour went into you. By morning your fever broke. The clock never kept right after that, but I wound it anyway, and I never told a soul." He looked at the note. "Now he's back. He was here last night, under the tower. He said the hour's due, and if I don't give it, he'll take it from the town. From everyone."

Ivy's mouth was dry. "What does he take?"

"An hour of their lives. One hour, from every person in this town, all at once. They'd never know what hit them." Her grandad's voice cracked. "I can't let that happen, Ivy. But I can't give him what he wants either. I've been sitting here trying to find a third way."

Ivy reached across the table and took his hand. It was cold, like the note. "Then we find one together," she said. "Where is he now?"

"The old workshop. He said he'd wait there until morning." Her grandad's eyes were wet. "Ivy, don't. He's not a man you bargain with."

But she was already standing, and the note was already back in her fist. "I'm not going to bargain," she said. "I'm going to listen."

She was out the door before he could stop her. The town clock behind her still read 3:17, and somewhere in the dark the collector was waiting.

***

The old workshop smelled of cold iron and sawdust that had forgotten the sun. Ivy pushed the door open with one finger. Inside, the collector stood at the bench, not moving, not breathing that she could hear. He was tall and thin as a pulled thread, and his coat was the color of the hour before dawn. On the bench in front of him lay a single brass gear, small as a coin. Beside it was a leather bag that held more weight than it should.

"You're the clockmaker's girl," he said. His voice was quiet and dry, like pages turning. "He sent you to talk. That's not how debts work."

"He didn't send me," Ivy said. Her heart was a drum in her throat, but she kept her voice level. "I came to offer something else. An hour for an hour. You said that. So take mine."

The collector tilted his head. "Yours? You're young. One hour from you is barely a breath."

"It's all I have that's mine to give," she said. "My grandad borrowed time to save me. So it's my debt too. Take the hour from me and leave the town alone."

For a long moment the collector looked at her the way a jeweler looks at a stone, weighing it. Then he reached out one long finger and touched the gear on the bench. "An hour from a child is still an hour," he murmured. "But it's not the one owed. The debt is his. He took the time. He pays it back."

"Then take it from me instead," Ivy said again, and her voice shook now, and she hated that it shook. "Please."

The workshop door banged open behind her. Her grandad stood there, coatless, out of breath, his white hair wild. "Stop," he said. "Ivy, step back."

"Grandad, no."

But he was already walking toward the bench, and he was already rolling up his sleeve. "You want your hour," he said to the collector. "Take it. Take it from me, the way it was agreed."

The collector smiled, and it was not a nice smile. "As you wish." He reached out and laid his hand on her grandad's forearm. Ivy saw the skin go pale under his fingers. She saw the lines deepen at the corners of her grandad's eyes. She saw his shoulders round just a little, as if a weight had settled there. It took no longer than a breath. Then the collector lifted his hand, and the gear on the bench was gone.

Her grandad swayed. Ivy caught his arm. "I've got you," she said.

***

They walked home slowly, her grandad leaning on her more than he would admit. The sky was turning gray at the edges. When they reached the square, the clock above them jerked, shuddered, and began to tick. The hands swung forward from 3:17 to 3:18, and the sound of it was the loudest thing Ivy had ever heard.

"It's going," she said.

Her grandad nodded. His face was tired, older than it had been that morning, but his eyes were clear. "It's going," he echoed. "The town won't lose its hour now."

They sat on the bench outside the shop, and he breathed carefully, like a man who had carried something heavy a long way. Ivy watched the clock hands move, one tick at a time.

"How much did he take?" she asked quietly.

"An hour," her grandad said. "Just an hour. But it was an hour I'd already spent once, long ago. Time doesn't like being paid twice." He smiled a little. "I'll be slower now. That's all. Slower, and tireder, and I'll forget things I used to know. But I'll be here."

"You shouldn't have," Ivy said. "I was going to do it. I was ready."

"I know you were." He put his hand over hers. It was warmer now than it had been at the kitchen table. "That's why I couldn't let you. You gave me an hour once, Ivy. When you were born, and the doctors said you wouldn't last the night, I made a bargain to borrow one. Just one. And you lived." He looked at her. "Every hour you've had since then, you've had because of that one. I couldn't let you spend it paying for my mistake."

Ivy was quiet for a moment. The clock ticked on above them, steady as a heartbeat. "So the hour you gave me," she said slowly. "It was yours."

"It was mine to give," he said. "And I'd give it again."

The town was waking now, shutters opening, a cart rattling down the lane. Nobody looked up at the clock. Nobody knew. Ivy sat with her grandad in the gray morning and listened to the ticking. She understood, finally, what he had done. Not a debt paid, but a gift given twice. She held his hand and did not let go.
