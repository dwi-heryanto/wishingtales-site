Nora found the manifesto on a Tuesday, taped to the front of the mailbox with a single gray feather.

It was written in blue ballpoint on the back of a pizza flyer. The handwriting was surprisingly neat.

DEMANDS OF THE RESIDENTS, it began. 1. More breadcrumbs. 2. Fewer of those little plastic flags. 3. The slot must be widened. 4. Nobody may call us "rats with wings." We know what you are saying. We have excellent hearing.

At the bottom, in larger letters: SIGNED, GERALD, OFFICIAL RESIDENT.

Nora read it twice. Then she looked up at the roof of the mailbox, where a fat gray pigeon sat with his chest puffed out like a tiny mayor.

"Did you write this?" she asked.

The pigeon blinked one orange eye. It did not seem like a no.

"Gerald," she said, testing the name. "That's not your name. You're a pigeon. Pigeons don't have names."

Gerald made a low rumbling sound in his throat. It might have been a laugh. It might have been a threat. Nora decided it was probably both.

She went inside and told her mother, who was stirring soup and not paying full attention.

"There's a bird claiming the mailbox," Nora said.

"Mm," said her mother. "That's nice, honey. Set the table."

"He has a manifesto."

"Everyone has a manifesto these days."

Nora went back out. Gerald had been joined by two more pigeons, one on each side of him like bodyguards in gray suits. The manifesto had been adjusted. A fifth demand had been added in fresh ink: 5. A statue of Gerald.

"You can't just add demands," Nora told him. "That's not how it works."

Gerald fluffed his wings and turned his back on her, which is pigeon for a very firm no comment.

By Thursday, the whole block knew. Dev had come over to see it for himself, crouching down to read the pizza flyer with his glasses sliding down his nose.

"This is incredible," he said. "Nora. Nora, he listed his qualifications."

"He listed that he can fly and that he has never once been late for anything."

"Those are excellent qualifications."

"He's a pigeon, Dev."

"He's a pigeon with a platform." Dev stood up, grinning. "You have to admit, more breadcrumbs is a pretty good platform."

Nora folded her arms. Behind Dev, Gerald had hopped down from the mailbox and was marching toward them along the sidewalk with a slow, deliberate waddle. The two bodyguards followed.

"He's coming this way," Nora said.

"He's doing a walkabout," Dev whispered. He actually sounded impressed.

Gerald stopped at their feet. He looked up at Nora. He looked at Dev. Then he did something Nora had never seen a pigeon do in her life. He raised one wing, slowly, and held it out toward Dev like a politician offering a handshake.

Dev shook it. He shook a pigeon's wing.

"Okay," Dev said, his voice a little stunned. "Okay. I'm in. I'm on the bird's side."

***

The campaign spread faster than Nora thought anything could spread on a street where nothing had happened for eleven years.

On Friday morning, pigeons blocked the sidewalk outside the bakery. Not aggressively. They simply stood in a line, shoulder to shoulder, and looked at anyone who tried to pass until that person went around. Mrs. Halloran from the corner tried to step over them and one of them, a small serious bird, looked at her so disappointedly that she apologized and went around too.

By noon, Gerald's people had taken the lawn sign from the Donnellys' yard. It had said VOTE FOR OUR MAILBOX, KEEP IT EMPTY. Now it said the same thing, but backwards, and it had been planted in front of the mailbox, facing the wrong way, like a protest.

"That's a political statement," Dev said, taking a photo.

"That's a bird stealing a lawn sign," Nora said.

"The message is the same either way."

Saturday brought the rally. Nora heard it before she saw it, a low throbbing coo that filled the whole street like a car alarm made of feathers. On the bakery's green awning, twenty pigeons had gathered in a neat arc. Gerald stood at the center, and every time he bobbed his head, the crowd bobbed with him.

Then he began to speak.

It was not words, exactly. It was a rolling, rhythmic cooing, rising and falling, and somehow the pigeons around him responded at the right moments, a wave of sound that swelled and settled. A few humans had gathered to watch. One of them was Dev, and he was clapping.

"He's got a call-and-response," Dev said. "He's got a whole thing."

"You're supposed to be helping me," Nora said. "You're supposed to be my best friend."

Dev lowered his phone. "I am helping you. I'm telling you he's good at this. That's useful information." He looked at her sideways. "You don't have a manifesto, Nora. You have strong feelings."

Nora looked up at the awning, at Gerald presiding over his crowd, and felt her strong feelings get considerably stronger.

"Fine," she said. "Then I'll get one."

The counter-campaign began at seven the next morning, because Nora had read somewhere that serious people start early. She had made twelve posters with a marker that smelled like paint and regret. Each one showed the mailbox, the actual mailbox, with its little red flag, and beneath it the words MAIL GOES HERE. She taped the first one to the pole beside the bakery and stepped back to admire it.

By nine, the poster was gone. So were three others. The tape was still there, hanging in little curls, as if someone had peeled the paper away with great care and no respect at all.

"Wind," Dev said, when she showed him.

"There is no wind, Dev. There has never been wind on this street."

He considered this. "The pigeons, then." He said it the way you might say "the weather" or "the ocean." Like something you could not argue with.

Nora found the posters on the bakery's awning. All four of them, laid out in a neat row, with pigeon droppings arranged in a pattern that could only be called organized. Beside them sat a single crust of bread.

"That's a ransom note," Nora said.

"That's a crust of bread."

"It's a ransom note."

On her way home she passed the mailbox again, and this time she stopped. Gerald sat on it, calm as a judge, and around the base of the pole someone had arranged a small pile of seeds in the shape of an arrow. The arrow pointed at the mailbox door.

"You can't vote," Nora told him. "You're a pigeon. You can't even read."

Gerald looked at her with one round orange eye, then hopped down and walked, unhurried, to the far side of the sidewalk. A line of pigeons formed behind him. They crossed the street single file, and the traffic stopped for them, and not one car honked, because the drivers were too busy laughing.

That was the trouble, Nora thought. Nobody was on her side because everybody thought it was funny. Gerald was winning on charm.

That night she made a chart. She liked charts. Columns for what she wanted, columns for what Gerald wanted, and a column in the middle headed NEGOTIABLE, which she drew a big empty box under, because she was not yet ready to negotiate.

Under Gerald's side she wrote: more breadcrumbs. More crumbs, more pigeons, more of everything. Under her side: one mailbox. That was it. That was the whole platform. It looked thin on paper, and she sat with it for a while, feeling the way you feel when you have brought a spoon to a swordfight.

At half past ten, someone knocked on her window. Dev stood on the lawn, holding two cups and looking apologetic.

"I'm sorry I laughed," he said. "I'll help. For real this time."

Nora let him in. They sat on the floor with the chart between them, and Dev read it twice and tapped the middle column.

"You should fill this in," he said. "Even if it's just one thing. You need to know what you'd actually give up."

Nora did not have an answer for that. Outside, faint but unmistakable, came the low rolling coo of a bird who seemed to know he had all night.

***

Nora found the accomplice on a Thursday, just after dusk, when the street was going blue and the lamps were not quite on. She had followed a pigeon. Not Gerald, but one of his lieutenants, a small grey bird with a white streak on its wing, and it had led her three doors down to the house with the sagging porch and the garden full of feeders.

An old man sat in a lawn chair among them, and pigeons sat on his arms, his shoulders, the brim of his hat. He was speaking to them in a low, careful voice.

"No, no, that's a hop, not a step. Watch me. One, two. You're rushing the second one."

The pigeon on his knee bobbed twice, precisely. The old man beamed.

"Mr. Abernathy," Nora said. "You're coaching them."

He did not look surprised. He looked the way people look when a bill finally arrives. "I am," he said. "Sit down, then. You've earned a chair."

Nora sat. A pigeon inspected her shoe and decided against it.

"The mailbox," she said. "It was you. The posters, the seeds, the arrow."

"The arrow was Gerald's idea. He's got a mind for theater." Mr. Abernathy scratched the pigeon on his shoulder. "The city wrote me a letter in the spring. Said the house is too close to the corner, said they'd take the porch and put a proper feeder on the pavement instead. A public one. With a bench." He nodded toward the mailbox. "And they promised me that box would be the feeder's box. For the birds. Then they forgot, or the money went somewhere, and it just sat there being a mailbox."

Nora felt her chart tilt inside her head. "So you want a feeder."

"I want the birds to have somewhere to go," he said. "I'm eighty-one. I can't do the porch steps much longer. I trained Gerald to make a fuss because a fuss gets noticed, and a notice gets a bench, and a bench means the birds eat whether I'm here or not."

He looked at her then, steady and a little sad. "I'm not your enemy, girl. I'm just older than you and I've been waiting longer."

Nora watched the pigeons settle around him, quiet as a garden. "You could have told me," she said.

"Would you have listened?"

She thought about her chart, and the empty middle column, and the spoon.

"I'm listening now," she said.

Election day came in warm and gold, which was the last good thing that happened all morning.

Gerald's parade started at nine. Nora stood on her porch with a mug she had forgotten to drink and watched forty pigeons walk the sidewalk in a rough column, led by a grey bird wearing a bottle cap on its head like a hat. Dev had painted a banner on an old bedsheet: CORN AND JUSTICE. Someone had tied it between two lampposts.

"I helped him with the slogan," Dev said, appearing at her elbow. "I think it's honest."

"It's a bedsheet."

"It's a movement, Nora."

At the corner, the ballot box sat on a folding table, a shoebox with a slot cut in the lid. Mrs. Ferris from the corner house was supervising it, which meant she was sitting beside it in a hat. People were dropping in slips of paper. Ms. Okon from the bakery put in a slip and then bought nothing, which was unlike her.

"What's the vote actually for?" Nora asked.

"Nobody's sure," Dev admitted. "Gerald didn't specify. It's more of a feeling."

Nora went inside to make a sign of her own, since her last one had vanished in the night. She came back out with fresh cardboard and a plan for a sensible speech about bins and postage. She did not get to make the speech.

A gust came first, snapping the banner sideways. Then the sky went grey in about four seconds and the rain arrived like a dropped bucket. Everyone ran for cover. Pigeons scattered up under the awnings and the elm. The shoebox lid blew off and the ballots went up in a white flurry, curling and pasting themselves to walls, to the bakery window, to Mrs. Ferris's hat.

Nora's sign dissolved in her hands. Across the street, Dev's sign dissolved in his. The banner tore loose at one end and hung flapping, CORN AND JUSTICE reduced to CORN AND, which felt accurate.

Then she saw the box. The shoebox had tipped off the table and was being carried, lidless and sloshing, down the gutter toward the storm drain. Two pigeons were walking alongside it, oddly deliberate. Above the drain, on the wall, someone had propped a small wet sign with a pigeon on it.

"The ballot box," Nora said.

"It's stealing itself," Dev said, delighted. "Nora. It's stealing itself."

She was already running. Rain got into her collar. The box scraped over the curb and she went down on one knee and caught it by the corner, and the ballots inside were pulp, one grey mass. When she looked up, soaked and breathing hard, there was a small wooden sign wired to the drain guard. The rain had not quite washed it out. It read: FEED US AND WE'LL GIVE IT BACK.

She stood in the downpour holding the ruined box, and she started to laugh, because it was funny, and because it was not.

***

The rain stopped at noon as if someone had turned it off. The street smelled of wet bricks. Nora carried the soggy ballot box to Mr. Pell's porch and set it on the step between them, and he looked at it for a long moment.

"Election's a wash, then," he said.

"Nothing on the slips survived," she said. "I checked. It's all one grey lump."

"Gerald's idea, the box. He's got a head for theatre." Mr. Pell sighed. "I overreached. I'll own it."

Nora sat down on the top step. Down the street, the awnings dripped and people were out with brooms, sweeping torn paper into piles. "Here's what I want," she said. "The box goes back to being a mailbox. Letters. The whole thing. No more manifestos."

"And the birds?"

"The birds get a feeder. A real one, on a post, with a bench the way they promised you. Out front where everybody can see it." She took a breath and did the part she had rehearsed on the walk over. "I'll write to the council. I'll get a petition. I'm good at charts. I'll make a chart."

Mr. Pell was quiet. Then he said, "You'd do that?"

"I'm not doing it for free. You teach me the bird calls. All of them. Dev says there are nine and I only know three."

"There are eleven," he said. "I invented two."

He put out his hand and they shook on it, and his grip was dry and firm, and Gerald landed on the railing between them and inspected the agreement from both sides.

"He wants a title," Mr. Pell said. "Official Feeder. Something like that."

"He can be Official Feeder," Nora said. "He's earned it. The parade was good."

That evening a new sign went up on the mailbox, painted by Dev, and the pigeons gathered on the wire above it to read it. Nora stood back with her chart open on her arm. Under the middle column she wrote, in careful letters: TRUCE. Then, smaller, beside it: for now.

Gerald flew down and stood on the box and looked at her, head tilted, and she looked back.

"Don't push it," she said.

He said nothing. He had already won.
