The wind hit the kitchen window like a shoulder against a door. Rosa stood on a chair with her hands full of candles, and the lights went out all at once.

For a moment the house was just shapes. Then the dark softened, and she could hear her own breathing.

"Don't move," Grandma said from the hallway. "I know this floor better than my own face."

A drawer slid open. Something rattled. Grandma came back with the matches tin and set it on the table with a small click.

Rosa climbed down. "The stove's dead too," she said. "Everything's dead."

"Everything's quiet," Grandma corrected. "There's a difference." She struck a match. The flame stood up straight, then leaned, and the kitchen came back to them in pieces. First the table. Then the blue jug. Then Grandma's glasses, flashing.

"Hand me the tall one," Grandma said. "And mind the wick. It's older than you and it's got opinions."

Rosa passed the candles down one by one. They set them along the windowsill, on the table, on the shelf by the bread box. Eight small fires, swaying.

Outside, the storm dragged a branch across the roof. Rosa flinched.

"That's only the oak," Grandma said. "It does that every winter. It's showing off."

Rosa laughed, a short surprised sound. The candles made long shadows that reached up the cupboards and let go again.

"Is it going to be cold?" she asked.

"Likely." Grandma pulled her cardigan tighter. "Cold is when families get interesting. Now sit. You're shivering, and shivering is no way to spend an evening."

***

Grandma went to the back of the pantry and came out with a thermos. It was red once, Rosa thought, though most of the paint had worn down to grey, and the lid was chipped like a tooth.

"This old thing?" Rosa said.

"This old thing." Grandma set it on the table. "Get two bowls and the ladle. The soup's been on since noon, so it's had time to think about itself."

Rosa found the bowls. Grandma unscrewed the lid, and steam came up in a slow white curl, and the smell of it filled the whole kitchen. Carrot. Onion. Something sweet underneath.

"It's still hot," Rosa said. "How is it still hot?"

"Because it doesn't know the power's out." Grandma poured, careful and steady. "It holds the night, this one. The whole night, from a winter a long time ago."

"A thermos can't hold a night."

"This one does." Grandma set the thermos down and touched its side with two fingers, like she was greeting it. "My mother filled it the night of the blizzard, and it's never once gone cold on me. Not really cold. Not the way other things do."

Rosa looked at the thermos. It looked like a thermos. It looked like a thing you'd find at the back of any cupboard.

"Drink your soup," Grandma said. "Then I'll tell you."

The soup was hot all the way down. Rosa held the bowl in both hands and felt her fingers come back to her.

"All right," she said. "Tell me."

Rosa finished her soup and set the bowl down. The kitchen had gone quiet in that heavy way, the wind pressing at the windows like it wanted in. She noticed her toes first. They weren't cold anymore. Then she noticed the dark. The corners of the room had softened. The black beyond the window didn't feel like a wall pushing back at her.

She held her hand out toward the thermos on the table. Warm air moved against her palm, steady as breath.

"Grandma," she said slowly. "The room's getting warmer."

"Is it?" Grandma didn't look surprised.

"It is. It's actually warm. The power's out. We're in a cold kitchen in a storm, and I'm warm." Rosa pulled her hand back and looked at her fingers. "It's the thermos, isn't it? It's doing something."

Grandma folded her hands around her own empty bowl. "What do you think it's doing?"

"Keeping the night in," Rosa said. "Like you said. But that doesn't make sense."

"No," Grandma agreed. "It doesn't. Most true things don't, at first."

Outside, a branch scraped the glass. Rosa barely flinched. The candle flames stood up straight, no shiver in them at all, and the shadows they threw stayed low and friendly on the floorboards. She thought of the dark upstairs, the long hall, the cold sheets. None of it seemed close.

"It's holding the dark back," she said. "I can feel it. Like the dark's out there and we're in here and it can't get through."

Grandma nodded once, slow. "That's what it does. That's what it's always done."

"But how?" Rosa leaned forward, elbows on the table. "You have to tell me how."

"I don't know how," Grandma said. "I know when. And I know who. That's all anyone in this family ever knew."

***

Grandma reached across and turned the thermos so its dented side faced the candlelight. "My mother had this in her hands the night of the blizzard," she said. "Nineteen forty-seven. You couldn't see the road for the snow. The wires came down, the whole street went black, and the cold got into the walls."

Rosa pulled her knees up onto the chair.

"I was small," Grandma went on. "Smaller than you. I remember my mother's hands around this thermos, and her voice saying, 'We'll be all right, we'll be all right.' She filled it with soup before the storm and kept it against her chest under her coat, and it never cooled. Not once. She fed us from it, a little at a time, all night long."

"She fed you from a thermos?"

"She fed half the street from it. Neighbors knocked, and she poured, and she poured, and there was always more. I don't know how. Nobody ever knew how. The soup came out hot every single time."

The candle guttered and steadied. Rosa looked at the thermos, and it seemed heavier now, the way a thing gets heavy when you know what it's been through.

"She gave it to me when I married," Grandma said. "She said, 'It holds the night, this one. Keep it near when the dark comes.' And I have. Every winter. Every storm."

Rosa's chest felt tight and warm at once, like she'd swallowed something too big. "That was your mother," she said. "My great-grandmother. I never met her."

"No," Grandma said gently. "But she's in this room tonight. She's in the soup."

Rosa touched the thermos with one finger. The dented metal was warm as a hand.

Rosa reached for the thermos to feel its warmth again, but her sleeve caught the handle. The thermos tipped, rolled off the table, and hit the floor with a clang that seemed to fill the whole kitchen.

Grandma gasped. Rosa dove for it, but the lid had already cracked. A thin line split the black plastic, and a faint wisp of steam curled out and thinned into the cold air.

"Is it broken?" Rosa whispered.

Grandma picked it up and turned it in her hands. "The soup's still in there. But the warmth, it's leaking out. Feel." She pressed it into Rosa's palms. The metal was cooling fast, like a stone pulled from a fire.

"We have to fix it," Rosa said. Her voice came out higher than she meant.

"I don't think we can, sweetheart. Not the way it was." Grandma's face looked older in the candlelight, tired and soft.

The room seemed darker now. The candles guttered, and the wind pushed at the windows. Rosa held the thermos against her chest, and she could feel the last of the heat seeping away through the crack.

Then she remembered her scarf. It was wool, thick and red, the one Grandma had knitted her last winter. Rosa pulled it off and wrapped it round and round the thermos, tucking the ends in tight.

"What are you doing?" Grandma asked.

"Keeping the night inside," Rosa said. "Like she did." She held the bundled thermos to her ear, as if she might hear something. "It's still warm. A little."

Grandma smiled. "Then let's not waste it."

***

They sat on the kitchen floor with their backs against the cupboards, the thermos between them like a small bundled baby. Grandma unscrewed the cracked lid carefully and poured the last of the soup into two mugs. Steam rose, thin but real.

"Share it," Rosa said. "Like your mother did."

Grandma's eyes shone. "She would have liked you."

They drank slowly, and the soup was still hot, just barely. Outside, the wind rattled the shutters, but inside the kitchen the candle flames stood straight and quiet. Rosa felt the warmth spread from her stomach out to her fingers and toes.

"It's not the same," she said. "The thermos, I mean. It won't stay warm forever now."

"No," Grandma agreed. "But it did its job tonight. And tomorrow we'll find a new lid. Or we won't. Either way, we'll have this." She tapped the scarf-wrapped thermos. "The night's not so big when you share it."

Rosa leaned her head on Grandma's shoulder. The kitchen smelled of soup and wax and wool. The storm went on, but it felt further away now, like a story someone else was telling.

"I'll remember," Rosa said. "About great-grandmother. About the thermos. I'll tell someone someday."

Grandma kissed the top of her head. "I know you will."

When the first grey light came through the window, they were still there, the empty mugs beside them, the thermos warm between their hands.
