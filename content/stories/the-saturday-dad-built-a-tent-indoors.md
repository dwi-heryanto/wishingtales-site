Maya woke to the sound of rain drumming on the roof, steady and loud, like a hundred small fingers tapping. She lay still and listened. Saturday. Soccer. She sat up fast and looked at the window.

Grey water slid down the glass. The backyard was a swamp of puddles, and the maple tree bent sideways in the wind.

"Oh no," Maya said. "No, no, no."

Her phone buzzed on the nightstand. A message from Coach: Game cancelled. Fields flooded.

Maya flopped back on her pillow. All week she had practiced her corner kicks. Now Saturday was just rain.

Downstairs, something scraped across the floor. Then again. Scrape, thump, scrape.

She pulled on her socks and padded down the steps. In the living room, Dad was dragging the big armchair away from the window.

"Morning, Captain," he said. His hair stuck up on one side.

"Did you see the message?" Maya asked.

"I saw it. Terrible weather for soccer." He shoved the chair against the wall. "Wonderful weather for something else."

"What else is there?"

Dad just smiled and kept dragging furniture.

Ben appeared at the top of the stairs in his pajamas, holding his stuffed bear. "Why is the couch moving?" he asked.

"Because it's in the way," said Dad.

"In the way of what?"

Dad pulled the broom from behind the door and held it up like a flag. "You'll see. Both of you, grab a chair."

Maya looked at the empty middle of the room, then at the rain, then at Dad. She grabbed a chair.

***

Dad dumped the whole basket of blankets on the rug. He pulled the big grey sheet from the top shelf and shook it out like a sail.

"Drape it over the table," he said. "Maya, you take that corner. Ben, you hold this one and don't let go."

Ben gripped the corner with both hands, very serious.

Maya climbed up on the ottoman and threw the sheet across the table. It billowed and settled, and the living room turned into something else.

"It's a cave," Ben said. "No, it's a ship. A pirate ship."

"It's a fort," Dad said. "The best kind. Now for the walls."

They tucked blankets under couch cushions and weighted the edges with books. Dad put a flashlight inside, and a warm yellow glow filled the little space.

Maya looked at it. It was cozy, she had to admit.

Then a thought crept in, quiet and cold. She was eleven now. Her friends didn't build blanket forts. Her friends did soccer and math club and read thick books on the porch.

"Maya?" Dad said. "You're the last corner."

She hesitated. "Dad. Isn't this a little... babyish?"

Dad sat back on his heels. "Do you think so?"

"I don't know. Maybe."

Ben poked his head out from under the sheet. "It's not babyish," he said. "It's a ship. Get in the ship, Maya."

Maya looked at her brother's face, round and hopeful. Then she crawled in.

"Fine," she said. "But I'm not a baby. I'm a sailor."

"You're the captain," Ben said. "Obviously."

The rain kept drumming. Inside the fort, it sounded far away.

Inside the fort, the world went soft and close. The blanket roof sagged low, and the flashlight made a gold puddle on the floor. Ben sat cross-legged in the corner, hugging his knees. "Captain Maya," he said, "we need a course. Where are we sailing?"

Maya pulled a cushion into her lap. "Um. To the island of... the kitchen." She pointed at the gap under the sheet. "Full speed ahead."

"Aye aye!" Ben crawled toward the gap and made a motor sound with his lips. Dad sat outside the fort, his back against the couch, reading a book. Every few minutes he made a gull noise. Ben giggled so hard he fell over.

After a while the ship became a cave. "It's a bear cave," Ben whispered. "We're bears. Big sleepy bears." He curled up against Maya's side, and his hair smelled like shampoo and rain.

"Bears don't talk," Maya whispered back. "They only grunt." She grunted. Ben grunted louder. Then Dad grunted from outside, and Ben laughed until he had to wipe his eyes.

"You're a good captain," Ben said, out of nowhere. "The best one. Even if you're a bear now."

Maya felt her face go warm. She was glad the flashlight was pointed at the wall. "Captains don't get mushy," she said, but she put her arm around him anyway.

***

The rain stopped being a drum and became a wind. It pushed against the windows, and the house seemed to lean. A gust slid under the sheet and lifted it like a sail. For a moment the fort was a real ship, billowing, and Ben shouted with joy.

Then the broom holding up the middle tipped. The blanket came down in one soft rush, and the flashlight rolled under the couch.

For a second nobody moved. Ben sat in the middle of a heap of sheets with his mouth open. Then his chin went wobbly. "It's broken," he said. His voice was small. "Our ship is broken."

Dad reached for him. "Hey. It's just a fort. We can build it again."

"But it won't be the same," Ben said. He pressed his face into Dad's shoulder. "It was the best one."

Maya sat on the floor with a blanket across her knees. She could say she was too old for this. She could go to her room and read. The thought felt cold and tidy, like a clean sock drawer. She looked at Ben's back, shaking a little. She remembered being six and crying when a sandcastle fell down. Dad had rebuilt it with her, and it had been better.

"Ben," she said. "We can make it better than before. Captains don't abandon ship. They rebuild."

Ben lifted his head. "Really?"

"Really," Maya said. "But we need a better design. No broom. A chair." She was already on her feet, pulling the sheet straight.

Maya tugged the big sheet off the sofa and shook it out. "Dad, I need the broom and two chairs. The tall ones."

Dad raised his eyebrows but went to get them. Ben scrambled up, wiping his face. "What are we making?"

"A better ship," Maya said. "With a mast." She pushed the coffee table against the wall and spread the sheet on the floor. "Put the chairs here, facing each other. Not too close."

Dad set the chairs down. Maya draped the sheet over both chair backs, then stood back. The sheet sagged in the middle.

"It's a puddle," Ben said.

"It's a sail that needs wind," Maya said. She took the broom and stood it upright between the chairs. "Now the sheet goes over the top. Dad, can you hold this end?"

Together they pulled the sheet tight over the broom handle. Maya crawled underneath and poked the broom head up. The sheet ballooned into a peak.

"A tent," Ben whispered.

"A crow's nest," Maya said. "But we need more room." She took the second sheet and clipped it to the first with two clothes pegs from the kitchen drawer. The new sheet sloped down to the floor like a ramp.

Ben crawled in and out. "It has a tunnel!"

Dad sat on the floor, watching. "Where did you learn to build like this?"

Maya shrugged. "Sandcastles. You have to make the walls thick." She tucked a blanket under the edge to stop the draft. The tent now had two rooms and a pointy roof.

"Captain," Ben said, "can we have a flag?"

Maya found a red scarf in the dress-up box and tied it to the broom. It hung down like a tongue. "Good enough," she said.

***

That night the rain had stopped. The living room was dark except for a small lamp in the corner. Maya and Ben lay inside the tent on a pile of pillows. Dad had brought them each a cup of water and then gone to bed.

"Maya?" Ben said.

"Mm?"

"Are you too old for forts?"

Maya looked up at the sheet ceiling. The broom handle made a line across it. "I thought I was," she said. "This morning I thought forts were for babies."

"But you built the best one."

"Yeah." She turned on her side. "I guess growing up isn't about giving things up. It's about doing them better."

Ben was quiet for a moment. "You're a good captain."

Maya smiled in the dark. "Go to sleep, sailor."

Outside, a car went by and its lights slid across the tent like a wave. Maya listened to Ben's breathing slow down. She thought about the cancelled soccer game. She had been angry about it this morning. Now she couldn't remember why.

The tent smelled of laundry soap and old blankets. It felt like the safest place in the world. Maya closed her eyes. She wasn't too old for forts. She was exactly the right age.
