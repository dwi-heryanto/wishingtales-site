Mara had just pulled the quilt to her chin when the wind slammed the shutters. Rain hammered the glass like handfuls of gravel. Across the room, Leo sat up in his sleeping bag. "Did you hear that?" he whispered.

"Hear what?" Mara said. "The storm? It's loud enough to wake the gulls."

Leo pointed at the window. "No. The light. There's no light on the point."

Mara slid out of bed and pressed her nose to the cold pane. The lighthouse stood at the end of the causeway, a grey finger against the black sky. Its lamp should have swung a slow white beam across the water. Instead, the tower was dark.

"The lamp's out," she said. "That's bad. There's a boat due tonight, remember? Dad said the supply boat comes on the high tide."

Leo joined her at the window, his socks silent on the floorboards. "We should tell someone." He glanced at the door. "But the phone lines are down. I tried before bed."

A gust rattled the frames. Then, from below, came a sound that was not the wind. Tap. Tap. Tap. Slow and patient, like a fingernail on wood.

Mara froze. "Is that the front door?"

"We're three floors up," Leo said. "Nothing's out in this. Nothing human, anyway."

Tap. Tap. Tap.

"It's probably a branch," Mara said, but her voice came out thin. She grabbed her cardigan and pulled it on. "Come on. We'll check together."

Leo grabbed the brass torch from the shelf. Its beam wobbled as they crept down the stairs. The tapping stopped. At the bottom, the front door stood solid and shut. Rain streamed down the small window beside it. Nothing moved outside.

"See?" Mara said. "Just the storm." But her hand stayed on the banister, and she did not go back up.

***

"We should check the lamp room," Mara said. "If we can relight it, the boat will see the point."

Leo shook his head. "Dad keeps the lamp key in the study. But there's something else in there. I saw it once, under the desk drawer."

The study smelled of old paper and pipe smoke. Leo knelt and tugged at the bottom drawer until it slid free. Behind it, taped to the wood, was a thin black notebook. He peeled it off and opened it under the torch.

"It's a logbook," he said. "From the old keepers. Listen to this." He read aloud, tracing the words with his finger. "'The Keeper of the Point walks when the light is out. Do not open the door after dark. Do not climb the tower alone.'"

Mara took the book from him. The handwriting was spidery and old. On the next page, a single line was underlined twice. "'The lamp must burn. The Keeper guards the flame.'"

"That's a warning," Leo said. "Someone wrote that for a reason."

Mara looked at the dark window. Somewhere out there, a boat was pushing through the storm, looking for the point. "Or it's a story," she said. "To scare kids away from the tower."

"It's working," Leo muttered.

She closed the book and tucked it into her pocket. "We're not kids who scare easy. We're the lighthouse keeper's children. We light the lamp."

Leo stared at her. Then he picked up the torch and stood a little straighter. "Fine. But we go together. And if we hear tapping again, we run."

"Deal," Mara said. "Now find that key."

The key was on a nail behind the kitchen door. Mara grabbed it and they stepped into the tower stairwell. Cold air rushed down to meet them. Their torch beam jumped across stone walls.

"Stay close," Mara said. Her voice echoed up the spiral.

Leo nodded and kept one hand on her coat. They climbed. The steps were worn smooth in the middle. Wind moaned through a crack somewhere above.

Halfway up, Leo stopped. "Mara. Listen."

She froze. Below them, soft and slow, came a sound. Footsteps. Not theirs. Someone was climbing the stairs behind them.

"Run," Leo whispered.

They ran, and the footsteps kept pace. Mara's heart hammered. At the top landing she spun around, torch raised. The stairs were empty. Only their own breath filled the silence.

"Maybe it's the wind," Leo said, but he didn't believe it.

Mara pointed at the tall window. Fog pressed against the glass like grey wool. And in the fog, far down by the rocks, a shape moved. It was tall and thin and wrapped in shadow. It seemed to be climbing the cliff path toward the tower.

"There," she breathed. "Something's out there."

Leo pressed his face to the glass. "It's got no face. Just a dark hood."

A tap sounded on the window. Then another. Mara pulled Leo back.

"The lamp," she said, her voice steady. "We light it now."

***

The lamp room smelled of oil and cold brass. The great lens sat like a giant glass honeycomb. Mara pulled the starter handle. Nothing happened. She tried again. The mechanism groaned and stuck.

"It's jammed," she said. "The gears won't turn."

Leo held the torch while she studied the housing. A narrow panel at the base of the lens had come loose. Beyond it was a dark crawlspace full of cogs and dust.

"I can fit in there," Mara said. "I can free the gear."

"It's pitch black," Leo said. "And something's outside."

"Then you keep watch." She handed him the torch. "Shine it through the panel so I can see."

He swallowed hard. "What if the footsteps come back?"

"Then shout. I'll hear you."

Mara got down on her hands and knees. The floor was icy. She wriggled through the panel into the dark. Cobwebs brushed her cheek. The space smelled of old grease and salt.

"Can you see the gear?" Leo called.

"Almost." Her fingers found a metal bar wedged against a cog. She pulled. It didn't move. She braced her feet and pulled again.

Outside, the wind rose to a shriek. Then came a new sound, close and low, like a voice humming through the wall.

"Leo?" she called. "What's happening?"

No answer. Only the humming, and the slow, steady tread of footsteps on the stairs below.

The humming stopped. In its place came a knock — soft, patient, three taps on the tower wall.

"Leo!" Mara called again. Her voice came out thin in the cramped dark.

A clink of metal, then the panel slid open and the torch beam swung across her face. Leo’s voice shook. "There’s someone at the door. An old woman. She says she’s the keeper of the point."

"The logbook said to watch out for the keeper."

"I know what it said. But she’s soaked through and she’s got a key."

Mara pulled the bar one more time and it gave with a clang. The gear spun free. She crawled backward into the lantern room, her knees scraped, and stood up beside her brother.

At the door stood an old woman in a yellow coat. Water dripped from her hood. Her eyes were bright as wet stones.

"You are the ones who lit the stove," she said. "I saw the smoke. I have kept this light thirty-one years, and tonight I could not climb." She held up a swollen ankle. "My name is Nell. This lamp is mine, and I am glad of help."

Leo folded his arms. "The logbook warned about you."

Nell laughed, a dry sound. "The logbook was written by a man who was scared of the dark. He was scared of me, too, but I kept his ships off the rocks all the same."

***

Mara looked at the old woman’s face. There was no shadow in it. Only tiredness, and cold.

"I fixed the gear," Mara said. "The lamp just needs to turn."

"Then turn it, child," said Nell. "I will steady the stairs."

Leo lit the wick. Mara pulled the great brass lever. For a moment nothing happened. Then the lens began to turn — slow, then sure — and the beam swung out across the sea in a long white arm.

Out in the dark, a ship’s horn answered. The sound rolled over the water, low and steady, the way a held breath lets go.

Nell watched it pass. "There," she said. "That is the sound I have been waiting for."

Mara looked down at her hands, grease to the wrists. "I was scared in there. It was so dark I couldn’t see my own fingers."

"And yet you went in," said Nell.

"Only because Leo was watching."

Leo grinned. "And I only stayed because you told me to shout."

Nell pulled her hood down. Rain ran off it in a little stream. "The dark is not the enemy," she said. "Being alone in it is the hard part. You two were not alone. Neither was I, once you lit the stove."

She climbed the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail. At the top she touched the turning lens like a friend’s shoulder.

"Will you keep the light?" Mara asked.

"Until morning," said Nell. "You two have done the night’s work. Go home and sleep."

Mara took the torch, clicked it off, and found she did not mind the dark at all. It held a warm hum, and the smell of salt, and someone breathing nearby.
