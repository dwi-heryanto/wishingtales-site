Tamsin woke to a sky that had forgotten to open. The burrow's mouth showed no grey pre-dawn, no thinning of the dark, only a flat blackness pressing close like a paw over a cup. She nosed through the grass tunnel and out into the clearing. The oaks stood as they always had, but their leaves were invisible, and the air smelled wrong: cold, mineral, like the underside of a stone.

"Get up," she said to the other rabbits, thumping her heel against the turf. "Something's come over the wood."

They came out blinking, the young ones first, then the mothers, then the old buck with the torn ear. He lifted his nose and held it there. "No wind," he said. "No birds. Where's the dawn?"

A crow landed hard on the branch above them and nearly fell off. "The sun's gone," it said. "I flew to the top of the hill and there's nothing to see. Not a red line, not a glow. Gone."

"Don't be stupid," said a hedgehog, unrolling by the roots of the oak. "The sun doesn't go."

"Then look," said the crow, and the hedgehog looked, and said nothing more.

By the time the whole wood had gathered (foxes at the edge, badgers heavy with sleep, mice in a trembling ring), the panic had settled into something worse. A murmur went around the clearing, and the murmur had a shape to it. The elders stood together by the old stump: the badger matriarch, the two grey squirrels who kept the wood's memory, the ancient hare who could barely see.

"It's the buried thing," said the badger matriarch. Her voice was flat and certain. "My grandmother's grandmother told of it. Something under the roots that must never be dug. We've kept it down, and now it's taken the light."

"Taken it where?" called a young fox.

"Down. Where it keeps things."

The squirrels nodded. The ancient hare said, "We do not dig. That is the law. That has always been the law."

Tamsin stood at the back of the crowd and felt the cold coming up through her feet. She had dug before: small scrapes, a bolt hole behind the bramble. But this was different. The whole wood was holding its breath, and the law was standing in the middle of it like a wall.

***

Old Maren lived in the hollow of the lightning-split ash, and she had been blind since before Tamsin was born. The wood still came to her for answers, though fewer came now, and they always came in daylight. Tonight the queue was long and frightened, and Tamsin waited until it had thinned to nothing.

"You're the young one," Maren said before Tamsin had spoken. Her head turned, feathers ruffled. "The one who digs."

"I'm the one who wants to know what's happened," Tamsin said. "The elders say the sun's been taken by the buried thing."

Maren was quiet. Her blind eyes caught nothing, but her face was turned toward the ground, and Tamsin had the feeling she was listening to something under the roots.

"The sun is not taken," Maren said at last. "It is buried."

"Buried?"

"Long before your grandmother's grandmother. There was a winter that would not end, and the wood was dying. So the first animals took the light (the last coal of it) and put it down deep, where the cold could not reach it, and they covered it over. They meant to bring it up when the wood was strong again."

"Then why didn't they?"

"Because the deeper it went, the more it grew. It is not a coal now, child. It is a living thing, and it is warm, and it does not want to be carried. So they made a law instead. Do not dig. Let it sleep." Maren's voice hardened. "The law is not about the sun. The law is about what it costs to bring it."

Tamsin's paws tingled. "What does it cost?"

"That," said Maren, "is what the law was written to stop anyone asking." She turned her blind face away. "Go home. The wood will find another way, or it won't."

But Tamsin did not go home. She stood at the mouth of the ash hollow and looked out at the dark wood, and the dark looked back, patient and cold.

"If nobody digs," she said quietly, "the sun stays down forever."

Maren did not answer. The silence was answer enough.

Tamsin went back to the ash hollow before the last of the grey light had gone, and she brought nothing with her but her own two paws. The hollow was a bowl of soft earth at the foot of the oldest oak, and the ash lay over it like a lid. She began to dig.

The first pawful came away easy. The second was colder. By the third she had reached the packed layer, and the soil there was stiff with frost in a way that made no sense, for the frost had not come down yet. She dug anyway, scraping with her forepaws, kicking the loose earth behind her.

A tunnel mouth opened under her, and she crawled into it.

The walls were smooth as a throat. Her whiskers brushed old roots that had grown through the earth and then stopped growing, and where she touched them she felt a warmth that had no business being there. She paused with her paw against one root and the root remembered.

It remembered a summer when the wood had been loud with bees, and the memory came up through her pads like water through a cloth, warm and sweet and gone too soon. She pulled her paw away and the tunnel was cold again. So the deep earth kept things. She dug on, and the tunnel bent down, and the walls began to remember more.

A root showed her a fox cub chasing its own tail in a clearing that no longer had a clearing in it. Another showed her rain, a long grey rain that fell for a season and made the wood green. Each touch gave her a piece of a world she had never seen, and each time she let go the cold came back a little sharper.

Above her, the wood was getting colder. She could feel it through the ceiling of her tunnel, a pressure of frost pressing down. Somewhere far off a bird called out once and then thought better of it.

The tunnel ended at a wall of loose stones, and behind the stones something breathed. Not loud. A slow, patient breathing, in and out, like a creature asleep and dreaming of waking. Heat came through the gaps in the stones, and Tamsin pressed her nose to the cracks and felt it on her face.

She did not move the stones. Not yet. She climbed back up the tunnel with her heart going hard, and when she came out into the ash hollow the whole wood was white with frost, and the frost had come in the time she had been under.

***

They were waiting for her at the top of the hollow. A ring of them, eyes catching what little light there was: two badgers, a knot of rabbits, the old buck with his antlers grey, and a fox she knew by name.

"You went down," said the buck. His voice was flat. "You went down into the ash."

"I did," said Tamsin. She was filthy, and her paws ached, and she did not lower her eyes.

"The law says no digging." The buck stamped once. "Every creature in this wood knows it. We have kept it since before our grandmothers' grandmothers."

"And the sun has not come up," said Tamsin. "Not for three days. The law kept us warm when the wood was strong. It is not keeping us warm now."

A rabbit made a small sound and pressed against another rabbit. One of the badgers said, "The elders say the buried thing must sleep. They say if it wakes wrong, the whole wood burns."

"Then let them say it to my face," said Tamsin. "I have been down there. I have felt it breathing. It is not a coal and it is not a curse. It is something alive, and it is waiting."

The fox stepped forward then, out of the ring, and the others made room for him. Corrin was lean and red and always had been clever, and he looked at Tamsin the way he looked at a henhouse he was deciding whether to enter.

"You are brave," said Corrin. "I will give you that. But brave is not the same as right. The law is not there to stop us being cold. The law is there to stop us being the ones who let the fire out."

"Somebody has to be," said Tamsin.

"And if it burns the wood?" Corrin's ears went flat. "If it wakes and it does not know us and it takes the oak and the hollow and every burrow under it? You will have done that. You will have done it alone, and we will all pay for it."

The ring was silent. The frost was on every back now. Tamsin looked at them, one by one, and she saw that they were not angry so much as frightened, and that frightened creatures do not move aside.

"Then I will do it alone," she said. "But I am going to do it."

She turned back to the tunnel mouth, and behind her Corrin said quietly, "You will not be forgiven for this. Not by me."

"I know," said Tamsin, and she went down into the dark.

The tunnel closed behind her like a throat. Tamsin kept her whiskers forward and her nose working, but the air tasted of nothing she knew: no loam, no root, no green. Only cold, and under the cold, a faint sweetness, like sap weeping from a cut branch.

She had dug this far on anger. Now the anger was spent, and the dark pressed close. Her paws ached. The tunnel floor sloped down, and the walls were smooth in a way she had not made them. Not clawed. Not scraped. Worn, as if something had passed through here many times, breathing.

The wood above her was silent. No owl called. No shrew rustled. Even the frost seemed to hold its breath.

Then the tunnel opened.

She stepped into a round chamber, and the glow was there, and it was not a stone.

It lay curled in a nest of old roots, no bigger than a hedgehog, and it pulsed. Slow. Steady. A deep orange, like the last coal in a hearth at midnight. Tamsin crept closer, and the glow brightened, and she saw the shape of it: a small, sleeping thing, furred in light, its back rising and falling.

"You're alive," she whispered.

The glow flickered. A sound came from it, low and cracked, like wind through a hollow stem. "Who wakes me?"

"Tamsin. From the wood above."

"The wood above is cold," said the glow. "I have felt it. I have felt the roots go stiff." It uncurled a little, and its light spilled across the chamber floor, and Tamsin saw the walls were carved with old marks, spirals and pawprints, hundreds of them, all pointing up. "I am not meant to be uncovered, little digger. I am meant to be carried. The law is not a wall. It is a warning. I burn what holds me too long."

"Then I will carry you quickly."

The glow made a sound that might have been a laugh. "Quickly is still long enough."

Tamsin crouched and held out her forepaws. The glow hesitated, then rolled into them.

It was hot. Not warm: hot, a heat that went through fur and skin and into the bone. Tamsin gasped and nearly dropped it, but she clamped her paws together and stood. The pain came in waves, and she bit down on her tongue and began to walk.

The tunnel back was longer than she remembered. The glow lit the walls, and the old marks moved in its light, and Tamsin saw that they were not marks at all but small creatures, carved in flight, all running upward. Her paws smoked. She did not look at them.

Above her, faint and far, she heard the wood begin to stir.

***

The tunnel mouth was a grey eye above her. Tamsin climbed with the glow held against her chest, and every pawhold sent a knife of pain up her leg. The glow was silent now, but she could feel it breathing against her fur, and each breath was hotter than the last.

She broke the surface into a world of frost and staring faces.

The whole wood had come. Foxes and badgers and mice and one old hare with his ears flat back. They ringed the tunnel mouth in a wide circle, and the frost had settled on them all, and their breath went up in clouds. Corrin stood at the front, his brush low, his eyes on the glow in her paws.

"You did it," he said. His voice was flat. "You brought it up."

"I brought it up," said Tamsin. Her paws were shaking. The fur had burned away in patches, and the skin beneath was raw and shining. "It has to go to the east hill. It has to see the sky."

"It will burn the wood," said a badger. "It will burn every burrow."

"It will burn the frost," said Tamsin. "It will burn the dark. That is what it is for."

Old Maren was there, her blind eyes turned toward the glow. "The law was never about the light," she said quietly. "It was about the carrying. Some things are not meant to be carried alone."

Tamsin looked at the ring of animals. No one moved.

Then a small voice spoke. "I will help." It was a field mouse, trembling. "I can carry it a little way."

Another voice. "And me." A young rabbit.

Corrin did not speak. But he stepped back, and the ring opened, and Tamsin walked through it with the glow in her paws.

At the east hill, she set it down. The glow uncurled, and its light spread out like water, and the sky above the hill turned from black to grey to a thin, cold gold. The sun rose.

It rose slowly, as if it had forgotten how. The frost on the grass turned to water. The birds began, one by one, to sing.

Tamsin sat down and looked at her burned paws. The wood was full of light now, and the light was warm, and the animals were coming out of their burrows to stand in it.

Corrin came to stand beside her. "You were right," he said. "And I will not forgive you. Both are true."

"I know," said Tamsin.

She looked at the sun, and the sun looked back, and the wood was changed, and she was changed, and she did not look away.
