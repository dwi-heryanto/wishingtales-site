Tobin was the last one awake in his house, as usual. He lay on his back with the window open and counted the stars above the orchard. One, two, three, then a gap where the moon should have hung like a lantern. He sat up so fast his blanket slid to the floor. The sky was full of stars and not one of them was the moon.

He ran to the square in his socks. Other people were already there, crowding the well and pointing up. "It's gone," said the baker, her voice wobbling. "The moon is gone."

"It was there at supper," said the blacksmith. "I saw it over the mill."

"And now it's not," said the baker. "What if it never comes back? What if the dark gets in everywhere?"

The square filled with lanterns and worried faces. Someone blew a horn. Someone else said the tide would forget its times. Tobin stood at the edge with his toes going cold on the cobbles. He had a small lantern of his own, a tin one his father had made, and he held it up as if it might help. It lit only his own two feet.

"Look," said a little girl, tugging her mother's sleeve. "The sky's just a blanket with holes in it now."

Tobin looked. The stars were sharp and cold, and the black between them felt deeper than it ever had. He did not say what he was thinking, which was that the dark seemed to be waiting for something.

***

The oldest villager was called Grandmother Oss, and she lived at the top of the lane where the houses thinned out. Tobin found her on her bench with a shawl around her shoulders, watching the empty sky as if it were a kettle about to boil.

"You've come about the moon," she said, before he could speak.

"Everyone's frightened," said Tobin. "The baker says the tide will forget. Is it true the moon's been stolen?"

Grandmother Oss patted the bench. "Not stolen," she said. "Borrowed. The Night takes it sometimes, when the year turns and it wants a light of its own."

Tobin frowned. "The Night can want things?"

"The Night is as hungry as anything else," she said. "It borrows the moon and keeps it a while. Then someone has to go and ask nicely."

"Ask who?"

"The Night itself. Up the Hill of Lanterns, past the last lamp." She looked at his tin lantern. "You've a small light there. That's the sort it listens for."

Tobin's stomach went tight. "I don't like the dark," he said. "Not past the lamps."

"No," said Grandmother Oss. "That's why it'll be you. The ones who like the dark never ask properly." She stood and pointed up the hill, where the path vanished between the pines. "Go on, then. Before the village talks itself into a panic it can't get out of."

Tobin looked at his little lantern. Then he looked at the black hill, and he started to walk.

The Hill of Lanterns had seven lamps along its path, and Tobin knew each one by name. He had walked this way a hundred times in daylight. Now the lamps made small gold circles on the ground, and between the circles the dark stood thick as wool.

He passed the first lamp at a run. The second one he passed at a walk, and by the third he had slowed to a shuffle. His tin lantern swung at his side and made a thin, brave light of its own.

"You're doing fine," he told himself, and his voice sounded very small on that hill.

Past the fifth lamp, the path narrowed. Pine branches leaned close, and something rustled in the needles above his head. Tobin stopped dead.

"Who's there?" he said.

Only the wind answered, but it answered softly, so he kept going. The sixth lamp flickered. The seventh lamp was the last, and beyond it the path went dark as a shut cupboard.

Tobin held up his lantern. A little circle of light spread around his boots, and he stepped into the dark inside it. One step. Then another. His heart beat hard, but his feet kept moving, and soon he was climbing with the lantern held high, and the dark was not on top of him. It was only around him, the way water is around a fish. He found he could bear that.

Halfway up, he heard someone breathing. He froze, then lifted the lantern.

A badger sat on a stone, blinking at the light. "Evening," it said, as calm as if it had been waiting for him.

"Evening," said Tobin. His voice came out steady. "I'm going to ask the Night for the moon."

The badger nodded. "Then you'll want to keep that lantern lit," it said. "The Night doesn't care for loud voices. It cares for small lights." It shuffled off into the pines, and Tobin climbed on.

***

The top of the hill was flat and bare, and there was no lamp there at all. Tobin stood still with his lantern raised and waited.

At first the dark looked empty. Then it moved.

It was not a shape, exactly. It was more like a slow folding, the way a blanket settles when someone lies down. Where it passed, the stars went quiet.

"You brought a light," said the Night. Its voice was low and near, like a cat under a chair.

"Yes," said Tobin. "I came to ask for the moon."

"Everyone wants the moon," said the Night. "It's the only warm thing I have."

Tobin thought about that. "It isn't yours, though," he said. "It belongs to everyone. The village is dark without it, and my sister can't sleep."

The Night was quiet a long moment. "I could give it back," it said at last. "But I'm greedy, and greedy things don't let go for nothing."

"What do you want?"

The dark leaned closer. "That small light in your hand. The one you brought up the hill. Give me that, and I'll put the moon back where it belongs."

Tobin looked down at his tin lantern. It had been his since he was six. The handle was warm from his grip, and the flame inside leaned and danced.

"If I give it to you," he said slowly, "I'll have to walk home in the dark."

"You will," said the Night. "And you won't like it."

Tobin swallowed. Then he held out the lantern with both hands. "Take it," he said. "But the moon goes up first."

The Night laughed, soft as falling snow, and took the lantern.

***

The Night lifted the lantern from Tobin's hands. Its dark fingers curled around the tin, and the little flame inside flickered once, then went still, as if it had fallen asleep. Tobin felt the warmth leave his palms.

"Now," he said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "The moon."

The Night raised the lantern high. "Look up," it said.

Tobin tipped his head back. At first there was nothing but black, the same black as the inside of his eyelids. Then a thin silver line appeared at the edge of the sky, like a thread pulled tight. The line curved and fattened. It was the moon, climbing out of the dark the way a swimmer climbs out of a pond. Pale light spilled over the hilltop. Tobin blinked. The grass turned silver. The path below him shone like a ribbon.

"There," said the Night. "It's yours again." It cradled the tin lantern against its chest. "And this is mine. I'll keep it warm."

Tobin watched the lantern light shrink into the dark, a small gold dot, until it was gone. The Night was gone too. Only the moon remained, round and steady, and the hill was very quiet.

He stood alone at the top of the Hill of Lanterns with no lantern at all. The path home dropped away into shadows. His heart gave a little squeeze. Then he remembered the moon was watching, and he put one foot in front of the other.

***

The way down was darker than the way up. Tobin kept his eyes on the silver path and counted his steps. One, two, three. The dark pressed close on either side, but it did not grab him. It smelled of wet grass and cold stone.

Halfway down, he heard a voice. "Tobin? Is that you?" It was his sister, Pell, holding a candle that shook in its dish.

"I'm here," he called. "The moon's back."

Pell ran to him and grabbed his sleeve. "You're all right. You're all right." She looked past him. "Where's your lantern?"

"I gave it away," said Tobin. "The Night wanted it. So the moon could come back."

Pell was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "That was brave." She held up her candle. "We'll share mine."

They walked down together, and the village came into view. Doors were open. People stood in the square, pointing at the sky. Someone had lit a fire in a barrel, and its orange light jumped and crackled.

"The moon!" shouted the baker. "The moon is back!"

Grandmother Oss came hobbling across the square. She took Tobin by the shoulders and looked at his empty hands. "You went up the hill," she said. "You asked, and you gave." She nodded slowly. "The village owes you a light."

Tobin shook his head. "I don't need one," he said. "I can see fine now." He looked up at the moon, and it seemed to look back at him, calm and bright. Around him, the village laughed and sang, and Pell squeezed his hand.

"Come on," she said. "Let's go home."

They walked through the square together, under the moon, and Tobin did not look at the dark again.
