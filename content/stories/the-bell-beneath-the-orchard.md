Ansel heard the bell at dusk. The pond lay flat as a dropped coin under the orchard trees. It was not a sound the pond should make. The note came up through the water and the mud and the packed roots, low and patient, like a stone being rung. He stood very still with his bucket half full of windfalls. It rang again. The apples in the bucket trembled against each other.

He ran. The lane to the village was dark under the boughs. He took it at a clumsy sprint, one hand out to keep from cracking his head on the low branches. By the time he reached the green, the elders were already there.

They had not come running. That was the thing Ansel noticed first. Six of them stood in a loose half-circle with their lanterns raised. Their coats were buttoned, and their boots were dry. They had been expecting it. Elder Foss held the big storm lantern, and its light made the lines of his face deeper than daylight ever did.

"You heard it," Foss said. It was not a question.

"The pond," Ansel said. "Something under the pond. It rang like a bell."

"It is a bell," said Elder Mave, and she looked at the water rather than at him. "It has been down there longer than the orchard has been up here."

Ansel's breath was still coming hard. "Then why is nobody pulling it out?"

A few of the elders exchanged a look that he did not like. Foss set his lantern down on the bench and folded his hands.

"Because it is not ours to pull," he said. "Come and sit, boy. There's a thing you should have been told before now, and I'll not have it said I told you badly."

***

They told it plainly, which was worse than if they had made it a story. The bell rang once in a hundred years, Foss said, and the village had known it for as long as there had been a village. When it rang, someone had to go down and answer it. Not with a rope, not with a hammer. With a voice.

"Answer it how?" Ansel asked.

"Give it something to keep," said Elder Mave. "A word. A name. Something of yours that it can hold. Then it goes quiet, and the water settles, and the orchard fruits for another hundred years."

"And if nobody goes down?"

Foss was quiet a moment. "The year my grandfather was a boy, the bell rang and the village hid its head. The trees flowered in spring and dropped every blossom by midsummer. The next year the same. By the third year there were no apples to pick. The children went thin, and the well ran low. So the fourth year, a woman named Sera went down with a rope tied to her waist."

"Did she come back?"

"She came back," said Foss. "She came back without her name. We called her Sera because we had no other word for her, but she never answered to it. She lived another thirty years and she never once said 'I.'"

Ansel looked at the lanterns, at the six dry pairs of boots. "You've all known this since I was small."

"We've known it since before you were small," Mave said. "We hoped it would keep another century."

From the dark at the edge of the green came a voice Ansel knew. "It didn't." Mara stepped into the lantern light with her arms folded, her father's coat hanging to her knees. She was Foss's daughter, and she was looking at her father as if he had sold the roof off the house. "You'd have let him hear it and never told him what it meant."

"Mara," Foss said.

"The bell rang at dusk and you were already standing here," she said. "Don't tell me you weren't going to let some fool volunteer before you'd finished your speech."

Ansel looked from her to the pond, which lay black and quiet beyond the green. The last of the bell's note seemed still to be in his chest, patient as a held breath. "I'll go down," he said.

Foss did not answer his daughter at once. He looked at Ansel the way a man looks at weather coming across a field, measuring what could be done about it and finding the answer was nothing.

"You don't know what you're offering," he said at last.

"Then tell me," Ansel said. "That's all I'm asking. Tell me and I'll still go."

Mave set her lantern down on the wet grass and folded her hands inside her sleeves. "Ninety-nine years ago the bell rang, same as tonight. The village sent down a young man named Corin. He was a good answerer — clear voice, no fear in it. We lowered him on a rope and we heard him speak his answer, and the water went quiet, and we pulled the rope up."

"And?"

"The rope came up empty," Mave said. "The knot was still tied. Corin was not in it."

The orchard behind them stood bare in the dark, every branch crooked and patient. Ansel had eaten apples from those trees his whole life. He thought of them not fruiting, of the village going thin and grey around the edges, and found the fear in him was smaller than he wanted it to be.

"Maybe he climbed out somewhere else," he said.

"There is nowhere else," Mara said. She had come closer, and her voice had lost its edge. "My grandmother was on the rope that night. She said the bell rang twice more after the answer, low, like a man clearing his throat before he speaks again. Then nothing. For a hundred years, nothing."

Ansel looked at her. "You believe it takes someone."

"I believe it asks," Mara said. "And I believe everyone who has ever answered it has gone down thinking they understood the question."

Foss took Ansel by the shoulder, not roughly. "There's no shame in waiting. The bell will ring all night. Someone else may come forward."

"Someone else will come forward," Ansel said, "and it will be old Tam, who can't hear the bell at all, or the blacksmith's boy, who is eight. I heard it, Master Foss. It rang for me before anyone told me what it was." He stepped back from the hand on his shoulder. "Get the rope."

***

The shaft beneath the pond was older than the village. Ansel knew this because the stones of its rim were not cut square like the meeting-house stones. They were fitted the way a stream fits a bank, each one leaning on the next. The water lay over the mouth of it like a lid of black glass, and the men had to break that lid with poles before they could set the frame and the pulley.

"Slow," Foss said, feeding the rope through his fists. "If it snags, pull twice and we'll haul."

Mara held the lantern for him at the edge. "Two pulls," she said. "Not one, not three. Two."

"I know."

"Say it back."

"Two pulls," Ansel said, and stepped over the rim, and the cold took him to the chest.

The shaft went down straight and then bent. The lantern on his belt swung and threw his shadow up the wet stones in front of him like a man climbing to meet him. Water dripped somewhere below with a sound like counting. He let the rope out hand over hand, and the walls narrowed. The grey light of the pond's underside slid away above him until there was only his own lantern and the dark it could not reach.

Then the shaft opened out. His boots found a floor of silt and something else. The lantern showed him a chamber like the inside of a bell turned upside down. The walls were green and glistening. At the far end the bell itself hung from a beam of black wood, its mouth full of water.

He took a step, and the thing his boot had found rolled, and he looked down. Bones lay in the silt, half sunk, arranged anyhow, the way a body lies when no one has laid it out. A rope's end was still knotted around one wrist, frayed through. Beside the skull, a small tin cup sat upright in the mud, placed there carefully, as if someone had set it down meaning to come back for it.

Ansel stood very still. The water moved against the bell's lip, in and out, in and out, and he understood that the bell was breathing.

"You came," it said, in a voice like a man clearing his throat.

It was not the bell's voice. It was a young man's voice, tired and glad and terribly patient, and it came out of the water as if it had been waiting a hundred years to say those two words to somebody.

Ansel's hand found the rope behind him. Two pulls, and they would haul him up, and the orchard would not fruit, and the bones would keep their cup and their silence.

"Yes," he said. "I came."

The voice came again, and this time it laughed, low and dry, the way a man laughs when he has not done it in a long while. "Don't look at the bones. They embarrass me. Sit if you can. The floor's softer than it looks."

Ansel did not sit. "Who are you?"

"Corin. Corin Vale, of the lower orchard. I answered the bell when it rang before you were born, and a hundred years before that it was somebody else, and so on back to when the first tree was planted." The water lapped at the bronze lip. "The bargain's simple. The bell needs a voice to answer it. Names have weight, boy. A freely given name keeps the orchard's roots deep and the fruit sweet. So I gave mine. All of it. Corin went down into the water and stayed."

"You're not in the water," Ansel said. "You're in the bell."

"Near enough." A pause. "The bones are what I left behind. The cup was mine. I set it down meaning to come back for it."

Ansel's grip tightened on the rope. Above, faint through the water, he could see the lanterns moving along the pond edge like slow fish. "So I give my name, and I go home, and the trees fruit."

"You go home," Corin said, and the bell's bronze hummed under the words. "But not as Ansel. The name stays here. You'll walk up that shaft with everything you are and nothing to be called. Your mother will look at you and know your face and not your name, and it will hurt her, and the hurt is part of the price. That's the bargain. It was never kind. It was only fair."

Ansel thought of his mother's hands in flour, of the way she called him in from the orchard at dusk, two syllables tossed across the rows like seed. He thought of the bones with their frayed rope, and how the last answerer had plainly not meant to leave them.

"What happens if I pull the rope and climb out?" he asked.

"The roots go shallow. The blossom comes and the frost takes it. Three years of thin harvest and the elders will send someone else down, and maybe that one will say yes, and maybe they'll leave a rope too." The voice gentled. "I'm not asking you to be brave. I'm asking you to be honest. What is a name worth to you?"

Ansel stood a long moment in the cold, and the lantern guttered, and the water breathed in and out against the bell.

"Corin," he said. "When I give it, will you be free?"

A silence. Then, quietly, "I don't know. No one's ever asked."

"Take it," Ansel said. "Take mine."

***

The rope came up heavy. Hands caught Ansel under the arms and hauled him onto the bank, and he lay on the wet grass coughing pond water while the elders crowded close with their lanterns and their careful faces. His mother pushed through them and knelt and took his head in her hands. Her mouth shaped the two syllables of his name.

He heard it. He understood it was meant for him. But when he reached for it inside himself, the place where the name had lived was smooth and empty, like a shelf after a thing has been taken down.

"He's cold," his mother said. "Get him by the fire." She said his name again, and then again, and each time her voice caught a little.

"He gave it," Mara said from the edge of the lantern light. She had her father's sternness and none of his ease. "Didn't you. You gave it freely."

Ansel sat up. "Yes."

"Then it's done," the eldest elder said, and he did not sound glad. "The roots will hold." He looked at Ansel's mother. "The name is spent. Don't use it. It will only wear you out."

So they walked him home through the orchard, and the trees stood dark and patient on either side, and somewhere under the pond a bell was quiet. In the kitchen his mother fed him bread and honey and did not cry where he could see. At the door, later, Mara waited with a lantern.

"They'll want to call you something," she said. "The elders will say a nameless one brings bad luck to a harvest. So pick one, before they pick for you."

He thought about it. The orchard smelled of wet bark and coming blossom. "Corin," he said at last. "But that's taken. Call me Corin's answer. Short, though. Answer."

Mara considered it. "Answer," she said, and it sounded like a name when she said it. "All right. Answer."

***

He has lived with it since. The orchard fruited that spring, and the next, and the elders stopped watching him sideways. Sometimes at dusk he walks down to the pond and stands at the edge and listens, and the water is only water, and no voice comes up. But once, near dawn, half asleep, he heard something in his own head that sounded like a young man clearing his throat, tired and glad, saying thank you. He does not tell anyone. He only goes on, being called.
