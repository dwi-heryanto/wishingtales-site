The cold came down the chimney of the henhouse like a thief. The hens slept in a huddle, their breath small clouds. Outside, the fox had not eaten in four days. Its ribs showed through the fur. Each step through the snow was a decision. The henhouse door was latched with a hook of bent iron. But the wood at the corner had rotted soft. The fox pressed its shoulder there until a board gave way with a crack that sounded louder than it was.

Inside, the smell of feathers and warm droppings hit like a fever. The hens woke. Their panic was a storm of wings and shrieking. The fox took the nearest one by the neck, quick and clean. But the noise had already run out into the night like a spilled bucket.

Lira was in the kitchen, mending a harness by the stove, when she heard it. Her father was already asleep, and her mother had gone to the village for the midwife. She took the lamp and the pitchfork and went out into the yard, her boots crunching. The henhouse door hung open, and a trail of feathers lay on the snow.

She found the fox in the corner, the dead hen at its feet, its eyes catching the lamplight like two chips of amber. It did not run. It had no strength to run.

"You," Lira said, and her voice was steady though her heart was not. She raised the pitchfork.

The fox watched her. Its mouth was bloody, and its flank was torn where a hen had fought back, but it made no sound. It simply looked at her as if it had already spent everything it had on the hen and had nothing left for fear.

Lira lowered the pitchfork. She did not know why. Perhaps it was the way the fox's ribs moved under its skin like a hand beneath a blanket. Perhaps it was that she had never seen anything so hungry.

"Don't move," she said, and she set the lamp down and went to the door to see if anyone had heard. The village was dark and quiet. The snow was coming down again, soft and steady. She went back and crouched near the fox.

"If I let you go, you'll come back," she said. "And they'll shoot you."

The fox blinked. Its breath was shallow. Lira looked at the dead hen, then at the fox, and made a decision that felt like swallowing a stone. She took off her coat and wrapped it around the animal, and the fox did not struggle. It was too weak to struggle.

"You're coming with me," she said. "Just for tonight."

***

The barn was warm from the goat and the two ewes, and the hay was dry. Lira carried the fox in her coat and laid it in the empty stall at the back, where the light from the lantern did not reach. The fox lay still, its eyes half closed. The wound on its flank was deeper than she had thought. The hen had fought hard, and the tear was jagged.

She fetched warm water and a clean rag from the house, and she knelt in the straw and began to clean the wound. The fox flinched once, then held still.

"You're braver than you look," Lira said. "Or stupider."

The fox made a sound, a low whine that was almost a sigh. Lira worked carefully, dabbing at the dried blood and the matted fur. She had done this for the goat when it cut its leg on a fence, and for a lamb with a torn ear. But a fox was different. A fox was a thief and a killer, and the village had a word for people who helped foxes: fools.

"My father would tan my hide," she said. "He says foxes are vermin."

She wrapped the wound in a strip of clean linen and tied it gently. The fox watched her the whole time, its gaze steady and unreadable. When she was done, she brought it a bowl of water and a scrap of mutton from the kitchen. The fox ate the mutton in two bites and drank. Then it lay its head down on its paws and slept.

Lira sat in the straw and watched it breathe. The lantern hissed softly. Outside, the wind pushed against the barn walls, but inside it was warm and quiet.

"I'll let you go in the morning," she said. "Before anyone sees."

The fox did not wake. Lira pulled her coat up over its shoulders and stayed a while longer. When she finally went back to the house, the sky was beginning to gray at the edges. She did not know if she had done a good thing or a foolish one. She only knew that the fox was alive, and that she had made it so.

In the barn, the fox opened its eyes in the dark and listened to her footsteps fade. It did not understand kindness. It only understood that the pain was less, and that the girl had not hurt it, and that for the first time in many days it was not afraid. It slept again, and dreamed of running.

***

Lira woke before dawn, the way she always did when something worried her. The fire in the hearth had burned down to red knuckles of coal. She pulled on her boots and her coat and went out into the gray light with a bucket of scraps, her breath smoking.

The barn door was open. Just a hand's width, but open. Her heart dropped. She set the bucket down and slipped inside, and the straw where the fox had lain was empty. The bowl was licked clean. A few dark hairs clung to the blanket she had folded for it.

"Stupid," she said, to the empty barn. "I told you to wait until I came."

She stood there a moment, and the cold came in through the door and settled on her. Then she went to check the henhouse, because that was the first thing her father would do. The latch was down. The hens were muttering on their roost, all of them there. The snow around the wire was unbroken except for a single line of small tracks leading away toward the fields.

She followed them a little way, but the wind had crusted the snow and the tracks gave out at the fence. Lira went back to the house and ate her porridge and said nothing, and her father said nothing, and the day went on as days did.

That evening, she went out to shut the barn and found the fox sitting in the yard.

It was thinner than before, and its bad leg was held off the ground, but it was there, and it was looking at her. When she took a step, it took a step. When she stopped, it stopped and looked back over its shoulder.

"You want me to follow," she said.

The fox turned and trotted toward the treeline, slow enough for her to keep up. Lira hesitated, then pulled her coat tighter and went after it. The snow was blue in the last light. The fox led her past the fence and into the pines, along a deer path she had never noticed, to a place where the ground fell away into a hollow under a fallen trunk. She crouched at the edge and looked down.

Four small faces looked back up at her. Their eyes were bright in the dark, and their ears were too big for their heads, and one of them made a thin, high sound that was not quite a bark.

"Oh," said Lira. "Oh, you have a family."

The fox stood beside her, watching her watch them, and its ribs moved fast under its fur.

***

She did not sleep that night. She lay in her bed with the blanket up to her chin and thought about four small faces in a hollow, and about the cold, and about what her father would say.

The village had a rule about foxes. It was not written down, but everyone knew it. Foxes took hens, and sometimes lambs. Once, years ago, a fox had bitten a child and the child had gotten sick. After that the men had gone out with dogs and lanterns. There had not been a fox near the village for three winters. Lira had heard the story a hundred times. She had never once thought about what it meant for the foxes.

In the morning she brought the scraps out to the hollow, and the mother fox watched her from the trunk and did not growl. The cubs were bold. One of them came close enough to sniff her boot, and she held very still, and then it sneezed and scrambled back.

She was late to the house. Her father was at the table with the council man, Garrick, who had a gray beard and a voice like a door closing.

"Tracks," Garrick was saying. "Up by the pines. Fresh ones. Big fox, and small ones too. A whole litter."

Lira's father rubbed his face. "You're sure."

"I know fox tracks," said Garrick. "We'll go out tomorrow with the dogs. Best to do it before they get bold."

Lira stood in the doorway with the cold coming in behind her. She thought about the mother fox's ribs, and the cubs in the hollow, and the way the smallest one had sneezed.

"They're not hurting anything," she said.

Both men turned. Garrick's eyebrows went up. "That so?"

"They're just trying to get through the winter. Same as us."

"And when the winter's through, and they've got a taste for hen?" Garrick shook his head. "You're young, girl. You don't remember what it was like."

Lira's father said nothing. He looked at her, and then at his hands, and she could see him weighing it.

"There's a council meeting tonight," Garrick said, standing. "You want to say something, say it there." He put on his coat. "But you'd better have more than a soft heart, is all I'll say."

The door closed behind him. Lira's father sat still.

"You've been feeding it," he said. It was not a question.

"Yes," said Lira.

He was quiet a long moment. "Then you'd better come tonight and say your piece," he said. "Because I can't stop them, and I won't lie for you."

***

The council met in the schoolroom, because it was the only room in the village with enough chairs. Lira sat at the back with her father and watched the faces arrive: Garrick, the miller, the two women who ran the dairy, old Tom who had lost three hens in one night last winter. The stove ticked as it cooled. Everyone smelled of woodsmoke and snow.

Garrick stood and told it plainly. Foxes at the edge of the wood. Hens taken. A hard winter and a hungry animal that had lost its fear. "We've all seen what happens when you let one get bold," he said. "It's not cruelty to end it. It's sense."

There was a murmur that Lira mistook at first for agreement. Then the miller said, "It's one fox," and the dairy woman said, "It's a vixen with cubs, by all accounts," and the room split into two low arguments. Lira's heart went hard and fast. She had thought they would all be Garrick.

"Lira has something to say," her father said. His voice was flat. He did not look at her.

She stood. Her knees felt wrong. "I've been feeding her," she said. "I know that was wrong to do without asking. But I've watched her. She comes at night and she takes what I put out and she goes back to her cubs. She's not bold. She's desperate." She paused. "There's a difference."

Garrick folded his arms. "And when she's not desperate?"

"Then she'll go back to the wood, where there's food, and she'll stay there." Lira's voice had steadied without her noticing. "She doesn't want to be near us. She wants to be near her cubs. If we make the edge of the wood safe for her, she'll keep to it. If we hunt her, we'll scatter the cubs and they'll be the ones at our henhouses come spring."

Old Tom made a sound that might have been a laugh. "You've thought about this."

"Someone had to," Lira said, and then wished she hadn't, because it sounded like cheek.

The room was quiet. The stove ticked. Garrick looked at her for a long time, and she made herself look back.

"You're asking us to trust a fox," he said.

"I'm asking you to trust me," she said. "I'll take the blame if I'm wrong. I'll pay for any hen taken."

"With what?"

"With my work," she said. "All spring. Whatever's needed."

Her father shifted in his chair. She waited for him to stop her. He did not.

The vote was not close, but it was not easy either. Garrick voted no, and so did the dairy woman, and one of the shepherds. The rest went the other way, some of them slowly, as if they were testing the weight of it. When it was done, Garrick put on his coat without a word and went out into the cold.

***

Lira took the fox out to the edge of the wood the next morning, with a sack of the scrap meat her father had allowed her and a length of rope she did not use. The snow had crusted in the night. The fox walked beside her, not on the rope, not quite trusting, her breath making small clouds.

At the tree line Lira stopped and put down the sack. "This is as far as I come," she said. "You'll have to do the rest."

The fox looked at her. Then she turned and went into the trees, slow, her tail low, and stopped and looked back.

"Go on," Lira said. "They're waiting."

The fox went. Lira stood in the cold and watched the place where she had gone. After a while she heard a small, high sound from deeper in the wood, and then another, and she let out a breath she had not known she was holding.

She came back every few days that winter, not to feed them, only to look. The cubs grew. The smallest one still sneezed. The vixen kept to the wood, and the henhouses stayed whole, and Garrick said nothing but also did not say anything against her, which from Garrick was a kind of peace.

On the last cold morning, Lira found tracks in the new snow: the vixen's, and three sets of smaller ones, going away from the village and not back toward it. She stood a long time looking at them. Home was not a place you stayed, she thought. It was a place you went back to, and you had to be let, and you had to choose it. She turned and walked back through the cold toward the smoke of the chimneys. Her father was at the door, and he held it open for her.
