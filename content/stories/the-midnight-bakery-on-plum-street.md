Rosa pushed open the bakery door at ten o'clock, and the bell above it gave a small, sleepy ring. Warm air smelled of flour and sugar and something buttery from the morning. Her grandfather was already there, sleeves rolled up, a dusting of white on his forearms like the first snow.

"You're early," he said, not turning around. He was shaping dough on the long wooden table, pressing it with the heels of his hands.

"You said the big order was tonight," Rosa said. She hung her coat on the hook by the door, under the clock that always ran seven minutes fast.

"It is. Tomorrow morning, actually. Dawn." He nodded toward a paper taped to the cabinet. "Two hundred rolls for the market committee. Their annual breakfast."

Rosa read the number twice. "Two hundred?"

"Two hundred." Her grandfather smiled. "Which is why I asked for my best helper."

Rosa tied on the apron that hung by the sink. It was too long, so she folded the waistband over twice. The bakery at night felt different from the day. The windows were black, and the streetlamp outside made a soft yellow puddle on the floor. Everything was quiet and waiting, like the room was holding its breath.

"First, we weigh," her grandfather said. "Then we mix. The oven needs time to heat, and so do we."

They worked side by side. Rosa scooped flour into the big metal bowl and her grandfather added water and yeast. The dough came together slowly, sticky at first, then smooth under her hands. She liked the way it pushed back when she pressed it, like a living thing that wanted to stay round.

"Grandpa," she said, "do you ever get tired of the night?"

He thought about it. "The night is when the bread becomes itself," he said. "By morning it's already finished. But at night, it's still a surprise."

Rosa laughed. "That doesn't make any sense."

"It will," he said, and slid the first tray toward the oven door.

***

The old oven gave a deep, rattling sigh, the way it always did when it was warming. Rosa liked that sound. It meant heat was coming, that the bread would rise and brown and fill the whole street with its smell.

But then the sigh stopped. The little blue flame inside the glass window flickered, shrank, and went out.

"Grandpa?" Rosa said.

He was already there, crouched down, peering through the glass. "Hm," he said. Then again, quieter, "Hm."

He turned the knob off, then on. Nothing happened. He tapped the pipe gently with his knuckle, the way you'd wake a sleepy cat.

"It's the valve," he said at last, standing up and wiping his hands. "It sticks when the weather turns cold. It'll need a part, and the shop that sells parts doesn't open until eight."

Rosa looked at the trays. Eight of them, lined up on the rack, each one crowded with pale rolls that had risen and were waiting to bake. "But the breakfast is at dawn."

"I know." Her grandfather rubbed his eyes. He looked tired suddenly, older than he did in the daytime. "Two hundred rolls, and no oven."

Rosa pulled a stool over and sat down. The bakery felt colder without the oven's hum. She could hear the clock ticking and the faint drip of the faucet.

"Could we bake them somewhere else?" she asked.

Her grandfather shook his head. "The diner's oven is small. The school is locked. We'd need a whole kitchen."

Rosa looked out the window. Across the street, past the streetlamp, she could see the window of the neighbor's house. A light was on in the kitchen. Mrs. Alvarez was old, older than Grandpa, and she lived alone. She kept chickens and grew tomatoes and had once told Rosa that she used to run a restaurant.

"Grandpa," Rosa said slowly. "Mrs. Alvarez has a kitchen."

Her grandfather looked at the trays, then at the window across the street. "Her oven is big," he said, almost to himself. "It's an old one too. It might just do."

Rosa jumped up. "I'll go ask her."

"At this hour?"

"The breakfast is at dawn," Rosa said. She was already reaching for her coat.

Mrs. Alvarez opened the door before Rosa knocked twice. She wore a blue bathrobe and held a flashlight, and her white hair was pinned up in a knot. "Rosa? Is your grandfather all right?"

"The oven died," Rosa said. "We have sixty rolls to bake by dawn. Grandpa says your oven is big. Can we borrow it?"

Mrs. Alvarez did not even pause. "Get the dough," she said, and stepped aside to let Rosa in. "I'll have the oven hot in ten minutes."

Back at the bakery, Grandpa was already loading trays onto the big wooden board. "She said yes?" he asked.

"She said hurry," Rosa told him. They worked fast, sliding the trays onto the board, then lifting it together. The night air was cool and smelled of wet pavement. The streetlamp threw their two shadows long across the road.

"Watch the curb," Grandpa said. His voice was tight. "Slow and steady wins."

Halfway across, the first drop touched Rosa's cheek. She looked up. The sky had gone from black to a heavy grey, and the streetlamp showed a curtain of rain moving toward them from the west. "Grandpa," she said. "Rain."

He looked up and moved faster, but the board was heavy and the trays were stacked high. The rain arrived all at once, hard and cold, drumming on the road. Water spotted the first tray of rolls.

"My coat," Rosa said, and she stopped walking. "Give me the corner." She handed her end of the board to her grandfather, then pulled off her coat and spread it over the dough like a blanket.

"You'll freeze," Grandpa said.

"I'm not the one who has to rise," she said. Rain ran down her arms and soaked her shirt, but she held the coat down with both hands. Together they shuffled the last few steps, and Mrs. Alvarez flung her door wide open. Warm air rushed out to meet them.

***

Inside, the kitchen glowed yellow. A kettle hummed on the stove, and the big oven breathed heat from its open door. Mrs. Alvarez wrapped Rosa in a towel before she could even ask.

"Coat on the dough. Smart girl," she said. She peeled the wet coat away and inspected the trays. "A little damp on top, nothing more. Stand them by the stove and they'll dry."

Grandpa set down the board and let out a long breath. "I thought we'd lost them," he said.

"You nearly did," Mrs. Alvarez said. "But you didn't." She slid the first tray into the oven and shut the door with a solid clang. "Now. Who wants tea while we wait?"

Rosa sat at the little table with the towel around her shoulders. Rain beat on the window, but inside it was warm and smelled of bread and cinnamon. Her grandfather sat across from her, and his hands were still shaking a little. "You kept your head out there," he said quietly.

"You told me slow and steady," Rosa said. "I just wanted to be careful."

"Careful and quick," he said. "That was the trick." He looked at the oven, then at Mrs. Alvarez, and he smiled for the first time all night. "Thank you," he said to her. "Truly."

Mrs. Alvarez poured three cups of tea. "I ran a restaurant for thirty years," she said. "I know what a dawn order means." She raised her cup. "To the rolls."

Rosa raised her cup too, and the rain kept falling outside, and the oven kept humming, and the first tray began to smell like morning.

The last tray came out at ten past six, and the kitchen window had gone from black to a soft gray. Rosa pressed her palm against the glass. The rain had stopped, and the street looked washed and new.

"That's the lot," her grandfather said, setting down the tray. "Sixty rolls, twenty loaves, and every one of them golden."

Mrs. Alvarez wiped her hands on her apron. "I'll bring my car around. We can stack the boxes in the trunk."

"Wait," Rosa said. She pulled the biggest roll from the tray. It was still too hot to hold, so she wrapped it in a clean cloth and split it into three. Steam curled up into the cool air.

"First warm one," she said, and held out a piece to each of them.

Her grandfather took a bite and closed his eyes. "That's it," he said. "That's the taste. I've been chasing it all night."

Mrs. Alvarez laughed. "Thirty years in a restaurant, and I never once ate the first roll. It was always for somebody else."

"Not tonight," Rosa said.

They stood by the open window and ate, and the sky turned pink over the rooftops. A dog barked somewhere down the block. A bus sighed at the corner. It was the quietest, busiest morning Rosa had ever seen.

***

The car pulled up to the community hall at half past six, and a woman in a green apron was already waiting at the door.

"You're right on time," she said, and her face went bright when she saw the boxes. "Oh, they smell like heaven."

"Careful, they're warm," Rosa said, handing her the top box.

Her grandfather carried the next one, and Mrs. Alvarez took two at once, and Rosa took the last. They set them along the long tables where people were already sitting.

A man in a wool cap looked up from his coffee. "You baked all this?" he asked Rosa.

"I helped," she said, and then, because it was true, "I kept the dough dry."

"Well," he said, "that's the important part."

When the last box was empty, her grandfather put his arm around her shoulders. "I couldn't have done it without you," he said.

Rosa leaned against him and watched the hall fill with light and steam and voices. All night she had been awake while the rest of the street slept, and it had felt like a secret. Now she knew what the secret was. Some things are made in the dark, quietly, so that other people can wake up to them.

"Same time next week?" her grandfather asked.

"Same time," Rosa said.
