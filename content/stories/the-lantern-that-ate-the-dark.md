The sun did not come up.

Nell had woken at the hour the roosters usually started, and the roosters were silent. She lay in the dark of her loft room, listening, and heard instead the shuffle and mutter of the whole village gathering in the square below. When she climbed down, her father was already pulling on his boots by the door.

"Stay in the house," he said, and then he looked at her face and didn't say it again.

Outside, the sky was the color of a bruise that never breaks. Lanterns bobbed between the houses, small nervous lights, and people stood in knots with their hands out as if they could feel for the missing warmth. Old Rennick the lamplighter sat on the well's edge with his coat buttoned wrong, and Nell went to him because he was the only person not shouting.

"The dark has weight to it today," Rennick said. "Can you feel it? It leans."

She could. It pressed against her skin like the air before a storm. "What's happened?" she asked.

"Something has gone out that shouldn't have gone out." He reached into his coat and brought out a lantern no bigger than a jam jar, its glass smoked and dark, and when he set it in her hands she felt a faint warmth, like a mouse breathing. "There's a seed of sunlight in there. One day's worth, maybe less. It has to reach the peak before it dies, or it will not be worth carrying."

"Why me?"

"Because I'm old and the mountain knows it." He folded her fingers around the handle. "The cradle at the summit is where the sun is kept. Something has taken the light out of it. Put this back, and the morning comes back. Do you understand me?"

Nell looked at the lantern. The seed inside was a small gold thread, curled, pulsing like a slow heart. "And if I'm too slow?"

Rennick's face did not change. "Then it goes out in your hand, and you'll know it."

***

Tam was waiting at the field gate with a sack over one shoulder and a coil of rope over the other, and he did not look surprised to see her.

"I heard Rennick talking to you," he said. "I'm coming."

"You don't know where I'm going."

"Up." He jerked his chin at the mountain, whose shape was only just visible, a blackness against a slightly less black sky. "It's the only place left to go."

They crossed the fields with the lantern held low between them. The stubble was wet and the grass at the edges had gone strange, and Nell kept seeing movement just past the reach of the light. A shadow lifted off a fence post, spread, and settled again. Another slipped along the ditch like something with a body.

"Those aren't shadows," Tam said quietly.

"Keep walking."

"I am walking. I'm just saying it."

They came to the stile and Tam went over first, then reached back for the lantern so she could climb with both hands. His knuckles were white on the rope. In the dark behind them came a soft rustling, many small sounds moving together, and Nell did not turn her head to look.

"How far's the forest?" Tam asked.

"Not far."

"Good." He swallowed. "I don't like the fields."

"Neither do I."

A shadow flowed across the path in front of them, long and low, and both of them stopped. It poured itself along the furrow and was gone. The lantern flickered, and the gold thread inside it curled tighter, as though it had felt something breathing on it.

"Nell," Tam said. "If the light goes out, what do we do?"

She tightened her grip on the handle. "Don't ask me that yet."

They walked on, and the mountain grew, and behind them the shadows kept pace, patient as animals who know the path leads one way.

The forest began with a wall of trees so old their branches met overhead like fingers laced in prayer. Nell stepped into the dark first, holding the lantern high, and the gold thread inside it flared as if it had been waiting for shade to show its worth. Tam came behind with the rope coiled at his belt.

"Stay close," she said. "The light doesn't reach far."

"It reaches enough." He was trying to sound easy. "I can see your feet, and that's all I need."

For a while the path was clear. Then the moths came. Nell heard them before she saw them: a dry, papery whispering, like a hundred pages turned at once. They descended from the canopy in a soft gray cloud, each one no bigger than her thumb, and where they touched the lantern's glow the light simply vanished.

"They're eating it," Tam said, and his voice had gone thin. "Nell, they're eating the light."

She swung the lantern, and the swarm parted and closed again. A moth landed on the glass, and the gold inside dimmed where its wings rested. She brushed it away with her sleeve, but three more took its place.

"Run," she said.

They ran. Tam was faster, and he pulled ahead, and then he cried out, a short, sharp sound, and stopped dead with his hands over his face. A moth had settled on his eyelid, and when he tore it away, a red mark bloomed beneath his eye.

"I can't see," he said. "Nell, I can't... everything's gone gray."

She caught his arm before he fell. The swarm lifted as suddenly as it had come, and the lantern burned low and steady. Tam blinked at her, and his eyes were wide and unfocused, fixed on something past her shoulder.

"Can you see the light?" she asked.

"I can see the dark," he said. "That's all."

She led him to a hollow in an old oak, where the roots made a dry cradle. His hands shook when she pressed the glow-worm charm into them, a small glass bead the lamplighter had given her for luck.

"It's warm," he said. "Why is it warm?"

"Because it's alive. Hold it and wait. I'll come back."

"Nell." His voice cracked. "What if you don't?"

She did not answer that. She took the rope from his belt and left him the food, and when she walked away the lantern's light stretched her shadow long behind her.

***

The path climbed, and the lantern dimmed. Nell watched the gold thread narrow, thread by thread, until it was only a spark at the bottom of the glass. The forest thinned, and the dark grew thicker, and then the shadow spoke.

"You're nearly there," it said. The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, soft as soot settling. "The peak is close. But you won't reach it before the light dies."

"Show yourself," Nell said.

The shadow pooled on the path ahead of her, and in its surface she saw a face that was almost her own: the same narrow chin, the same stubborn jaw, but made of nothing solid.

"I can carry you," it said. "A shortcut through the dark. You'll be at the peak before the spark goes out."

"What do you want?"

"A memory. One small one. You won't miss it."

She thought of Tam in the hollow tree, blind and waiting. She thought of the sun's cradle, cold and empty, and the village below with its thousand windows dark.

"Which memory?" she asked.

"The one you hold dearest. That's the only kind worth taking."

Nell looked at the spark in the lantern. It was smaller than a grain of wheat now, and it would not last the climb. She thought of her mother's face: the crooked smile, the smell of bread, the way she hummed when she thought no one was listening.

"Take it," she said.

The shadow smiled, and the smile was the worst thing Nell had ever seen, because it was gentle. Then it poured over her, cold as well water, and for a moment she could not remember why she was standing on a mountain path at all.

When it withdrew, the memory was gone. She knew her mother had existed, but the face was a blank place, like a page torn from a book. The shadow led her upward, and she followed, and the lantern in her hand held one last spark.

The shadow moved ahead of her like a stain on the air, and the path unrolled beneath her feet as if it had been waiting for permission. Nell climbed. The lantern weighed nothing now, just a shell with a spark inside, and the spark was so faint she could only see it when she looked away from it. Her legs burned. Her breath came in short, cold pulls. Above her, the mountain's peak cut a black shape out of the black sky.

"How much farther?" she asked.

"Not far," said the shadow. Its voice had changed now that it held something of hers. It sounded almost kind. "You gave me a good memory. I'll see you to the top."

Nell did not answer. She kept her eyes on the path and her mind on the spark. The memory of her mother was gone, but the shape of its absence was there, a hollow that ached when she touched it. She tried to picture the crooked smile and found only a smooth, blank place. The lantern flickered.

At the summit, the wind stopped. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the silence: no night birds, no insects, no rustle of grass. The peak was a flat bowl of stone, and in the center of it stood the cradle.

It was a ring of iron, waist-high, and it should have held the sun. Nell had seen it once, on a clear winter morning, when the old lamplighter had lifted her up to look through his spyglass. The cradle had glowed then, a soft gold that made her eyes water. Now it was cold. Frost had furred the iron. And in the middle of the ring, where the light should have pooled, there was a hole.

The hole went down. Nell stepped closer and looked into it, and the dark looked back. It was not empty: it was full of nothing, the way a mouth is full of hunger. The edges of the hole were smooth, as if something had been pulled out of the stone.

"It's gone," she said. Her voice cracked. "The light is gone."

"Not all of it," said the shadow. It hovered at the edge of the cradle, keeping its distance from the lantern. "You carry the last of it. Pour it in."

Nell held up the lantern. The spark inside was barely a spark now, a fleck of gold no bigger than a pinprick. She thought of Tam in the hollow tree, blind and waiting. She thought of the village, dark and cold. She thought of her mother's face, and found the blank place again, and the ache of it.

"Will it be enough?" she asked.

"It will be what it is," said the shadow. "Pour it."

Nell unlatched the lantern's little door. The spark clung to the wick, trembling. She tilted the lantern over the hole, and for a moment nothing happened. Then the spark let go.

It fell slowly, like a snowflake, and where it touched the stone, the stone remembered warmth. A thread of gold ran around the inside of the cradle, thin as a hair. It met itself and kept going, and the hole began to fill (not with light, exactly, but with the promise of it), a glow that started deep and rose.

The shadow screamed. Nell had never heard a sound like it: thin and tearing, like cloth ripped in two. The shadow pulled back from the cradle, and as the light rose, it shrank. "You didn't say it would hurt," it hissed.

"You didn't ask," said Nell.

The light reached the rim of the cradle and spilled over. The frost on the iron turned to steam. The hole was gone, filled with a slow, churning gold that rose and rose until it lifted off the stone and hung in the air above the peak, a small round sun.

Nell stood in its warmth and felt the ache in her chest ease, just a little. She looked for the shadow, but it had gone. The path down the mountain was clear in the new light.

***

Tam was sitting where she had left him, his back against the hollow tree, his hands wrapped around the glow-worm charm. He turned his head when he heard her footsteps. "Nell?"

"I'm here." She knelt beside him. The charm in his hands had gone dark, its little life spent. "Can you see?"

He blinked. Then he blinked again, and his eyes focused on her face, and he let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "I can see you," he said. "You look terrible."

Nell laughed too, though it came out shaky. "You should see yourself."

They walked down the mountain together as the sun rose properly, a real sun now, warm and gold and climbing. The fields were full of people. Nell saw the baker and the blacksmith and the schoolteacher, all standing in the road with their faces turned up, and she saw the old lamplighter at the edge of the crowd, leaning on his stick.

He came to meet her. "You did it," he said. "I knew you would."

Nell held out the empty lantern. "It's done," she said.

The lamplighter took it and turned it over in his hands. "And what did it cost you?"

She opened her mouth to answer, and found she could not. The words were there (my mother's face, the crooked smile, the humming), but when she tried to picture the face, there was only the blank place. She knew it had been there. She knew it was gone.

"Something," she said at last. "Something I can't get back."

The old man nodded slowly. He did not say he was sorry. He did not say it was worth it. He just put a hand on her shoulder, and the warmth of it was real.

That night, Nell sat on her doorstep and watched the sun go down. It would come up again tomorrow. She knew that now. And when her mother came out to call her in for supper, Nell looked at her and saw a stranger's face: kind, tired, familiar in every way except the one that mattered.

"Coming," Nell said, and she followed her inside, and she did not cry until the door was closed.

The lantern sat on the shelf by the window, empty and dark. Nell left it there. Some things you keep, even when they hold nothing.
