Maya sat cross-legged on the floor of the living room, a roll of packing tape in one hand. June worked across from her, wrapping the last of the good plates in newspaper. The house already felt emptier than it had three days ago. Boxes lined the walls like quiet guests.

"This one's heavy," June said, lifting a shoebox from the hall closet. "What did Mom keep in here?"

Maya took it. The lid was soft with age. Inside were folded maps—gas station freebies, a state atlas, a hand-drawn sketch on the back of an envelope. Every one had a route traced in red pen, with small crosses and question marks. None of the roads led anywhere they had ever gone.

"She planned trips," Maya said slowly. "All these years. She never took them."

June looked over her shoulder. "She was always going to. After we left home. After things settled down." Her voice flattened on the last words.

Maya traced a line that ran north from their town toward the mountains, then west. A note in the margin read: *Stonewall Pass—pie at the diner. Worth the detour.*

"We could take this road," Maya said. "To Aunt Ruth's. It's not much longer."

June shook her head. "We need to get there, Maya. Unpack. Figure things out."

"It's one day."

"Fine." June folded the newspaper around a plate. "But we don't stop for every cross on a map."

***

Two hours into the drive, the truck began to rattle. June turned down the radio. The rattle grew to a knock, then a groan, and she pulled onto the shoulder just as the engine quit.

"No, no, no," she said, turning the key. The engine coughed and died. "Come on."

Maya watched the heat shimmer above the hood. "There's a sign for a garage about a mile back."

They walked, keeping to the gravel. The garage was small and smelled of oil and coffee. A woman in overalls looked up from a clipboard.

"Truck quit on you?" she asked.

"About a mile east," June said. "It just died."

"That'll be the fuel pump. I can get to it tomorrow morning." The woman wiped her hands on a rag. "I'm Lou. You two need a place to stay? Motel's two blocks down. Tell them I sent you."

June's jaw tightened. "How much will it cost?"

Lou named a price that made June go quiet. Then Lou shrugged. "Or you can help me sort parts in the morning and we call it even. I'm short-handed."

June stared at her. "You don't know us."

"I know a dead fuel pump," Lou said. "And I know you didn't plan to spend the night here. That's enough."

At the motel, June sat on the edge of the bed and didn't unpack. The room smelled like bleach and old carpet, and a moth knocked itself against the window glass. Maya sat beside her.

"You didn't tell me the truck was that bad," Maya said.

"I didn't know." June's voice was tight. "I didn't know a lot of things. Like how I was going to afford any of this."

Maya waited.

"I got into two colleges," June said finally. "The letters came in March. I hid them in my sock drawer. I was going to defer a year, stay close, make sure you were okay. I didn't want to tell you that."

Maya looked at her sister's hands, clenched around the bedspread. "You should have told me."

"I know." June's voice cracked. "I was scared you'd think I was leaving you."

Outside, a streetlight hummed. Maya thought of the maps in the shoebox, all those roads their mother had drawn but never driven.

"You're not leaving," Maya said. "We're both going somewhere. It's just not the same place."

June let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "When did you get so smart?"

"I had a good teacher." Maya leaned her head on June's shoulder. "Now get some sleep. We have parts to sort in the morning."

***

By seven the garage was already warm. Lou handed them each a cardboard box of bolts and told them to sort by size into the plastic bins on the bench. June worked quickly, sleeves pushed up, and Maya matched her, though she kept losing count and starting over.

"So where are you two headed?" Lou asked, without looking up from her clipboard.

"Aunt Rosa's," Maya said. "She lives past the state line. We're moving in with her."

Lou nodded slowly. "And your folks?"

June's hands stopped moving. "Our mom died in February. Our dad's not—he's not around."

Lou set the clipboard down. "I'm sorry," she said, simply, and then she picked it back up, which Maya liked better than anything else she could have done. "Fuel pump'll be in Tuesday. You're welcome to keep helping till then. I'll pay you for the extra days."

June looked at her, then at the bins, then at Maya. "We can't take your money."

"You're working for it," Lou said. "That's not charity. That's a job."

They sorted parts until noon and ate sandwiches Lou brought from the diner across the street. Maya watched June's shoulders come down a little, inch by inch, the way they did when she stopped carrying a thing and set it on the ground.

***

The next morning, while June walked to the garage, Maya unpacked the shoebox again. She spread the maps across the bedspread, smoothing the creases with her palm. Underneath the last one, a spiral notebook she hadn't noticed before. The cover was soft with age, and on the first page, in their mother's looping hand: *Things I Meant to Do.*

Maya turned the pages slowly. Most entries were small—learn to make bread, plant a lilac bush, write a letter to Aunt Rosa. But halfway through, a page was folded over at the corner. *The House of Glass,* her mother had written. *Route 9, near the state line. Dad took me when I was eleven. You could see the whole valley from the top. I always meant to go back. Never did.*

Maya read it twice. Then she found the road atlas in the glove box and traced the route with her finger. Route 9 ran east, just twenty miles past the turnoff to Aunt Rosa's. Twenty miles. All this time, they'd been driving right past it.

When June came back, Maya was sitting on the edge of the bed with the notebook open on her knees. "The part's ordered," June said. "We're stuck here till Tuesday."

"June." Maya held up the notebook. "Look at this."

June sat down beside her and read the page. Her face changed slowly, the way a window changes when the sun comes out. "She never went back," June said. "She wrote that years ago."

"She never did." Maya closed the notebook. "But we could."

June looked at the maps, then at her sister. "It's not on the way to Aunt Rosa's."

"It's twenty miles off. We have a whole day to kill."

June was quiet. Outside, someone's dog barked, and a screen door slammed. "She would have liked that," June said finally. "She would have laughed at us for going."

"She would have laughed and then made us take pictures." Maya stood up. "Come on. Before you change your mind."

June grabbed her jacket. "You're a menace."

"I learned from the best."

***

They left the motel before eight, the sky still pale and the coffee from the front office too hot to hold. June drove with her window cracked, and Maya navigated from the old paper map spread across her knees. The route their mother had marked in pencil was faint but clear: a squiggle off the main road, then a dotted line to a place called the Garden of Giants.

"She drew a star next to it," Maya said. "And a little smiley face with its tongue out."

"That's either very promising or very embarrassing," June said.

The turnoff was easy to miss, a gravel drive between two fields of dry grass. They bumped along it for a mile, past a mailbox painted with a lumberjack riding a rabbit, and then the trees opened up and there it was: a clearing full of enormous concrete statues, painted in peeling colors. A giant holding a tiny house in one hand. A giant sitting on a rock, eating a sandwich the size of a car. A giant asleep with daisies growing out of his ears.

June parked and cut the engine. "Oh no," she said. "Oh no, this is amazing."

Maya got out. The air smelled like pine needles and old paint. A hand-lettered sign near the gate said PLEASE DON'T CLIMB THE GIANTS and, in smaller letters, WE MEAN IT.

"Which one was her favorite?" June asked.

Maya checked the notebook. "She didn't say. She just wrote, 'Best day of the summer I was twelve. The giant with the daisies. I wanted to live in his ear.'"

They found him near the back, half-hidden by a stand of birches. The daisies in his ear were mostly chipped away, but someone had stuck fresh ones in, real ones, their stems poking out of the concrete. June stood looking up at him for a long time.

"She was twelve," she said. "In her notebook she was twelve, and then she was our mom, and then she was sick, and then she was gone. All of that happened in the same life."

Maya didn't say anything. She took out her phone and took a picture of the giant's ear. Then she took one of June standing in front of it, arms crossed, trying to look unimpressed.

"Come on," Maya said. "Let's be stupid about it."

They climbed the giant. Not all the way up, just onto his enormous shoe, which was big enough for both of them to sit on. June laughed until she had to wipe her eyes, and Maya wasn't sure if it was the kind of laughing that turns into crying. She held her sister's hand and looked out at the clearing, at all the broken, painted giants, and it felt like her mother was nearby, just out of sight, laughing at them for being ridiculous.

***

Aunt Rosa's house was yellow, with a porch that wrapped around two sides and a rocking chair that had been there since before Maya was born. They pulled in just after six, the truck grumbling its way up the last hill. Rosa came out with her sleeves rolled up and her hands covered in flour.

"There you are," she said, and hugged June so hard that June made a small surprised sound. Then she hugged Maya, and she smelled like bread and the same hand soap their mother used to buy.

Unloading took an hour. Rosa's neighbor came by with a dolly and didn't ask any questions, just helped them carry boxes until the truck was empty. Maya stacked her own things in a corner of the spare room: the notebook, the maps, the shoebox of photographs. June stood in the doorway, holding a duffel bag she hadn't unpacked yet.

"You can put your stuff in the blue room," Rosa said from the hall. "There's a desk in there, and the light's good in the morning."

June didn't move. "I don't know if I'm staying," she said. "I got in somewhere. I got in a few places. I was going to defer a year, but I haven't sent anything."

Rosa leaned against the doorframe. "You know what your mother told me when she was pregnant with you? She said she was terrified she'd do it wrong. And I said, well, you'll do it wrong sometimes, and then you'll do it right. That's the whole job."

June looked at the duffel bag. Maya watched her sister's face do something complicated, something that had been waiting a long time to happen.

"I don't want to leave her alone," June said quietly.

"I'm right here," Maya said. "I'm not a house. I don't need to be packed up and moved."

June blinked at her. Then she laughed, a real one, tired and surprised. "When did you get so bossy?"

"I learned from the best."

They ate dinner on the porch because it was warm and the light was gold and Rosa said she wasn't hauling plates back inside for anyone. The garden was full of bees and the smell of cut grass. Maya took out the notebook and set it on the table between them, open to the page with the giant.

"We should finish her map," she said. "All the places she marked and never got to. One at a time."

June tore a piece of bread in half. "That could take years."

"Good," Maya said. Rosa reached over and squeezed her hand, and the three of them sat there as the sky went pink and then grey, and the first stars came out over the yellow house, and nobody said anything for a while, because nothing needed saying.
