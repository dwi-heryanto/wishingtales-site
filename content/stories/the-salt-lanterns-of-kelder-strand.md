Juno knew the tide by its sound, not its clock. Every night it breathed out along the shingle and breathed back in, a slow grey rhythm under her window. But tonight the breathing had stopped.

She climbed out of bed and pressed her palms to the cold glass. The moon was full and low, the color of old bone. Where the sea should have been, there was only a wide dark plain of mud and weed and shining stone, stretching out farther than she had ever seen. The water had pulled back like a held breath.

Then she noticed the silence. The village bell, the great bronze bell on the harbor wall that rang the hours and warned the boats home, had not spoken since dusk. Juno waited. The silence went on and on, thick as wool.

She pulled on her boots and her coat and slipped out. The lane was empty. The cottages were dark, and the dogs that usually barked at her passing only whined and pressed their noses to the doors. Juno walked down to the harbor wall and put her hand on the bell rope. It hung slack, and when she tugged it, nothing answered. The bell above her was still. She could feel the cold of it through the rope, and a kind of hollowness, as if the bell had swallowed its own voice.

"That's not right," she said to no one. Her voice sounded small and flat in the open air.

She climbed the wall and looked down at the seabed. That was when she saw the light. Far out, where the water should have been deep, a small green glow pulsed on the mud, steady as a heartbeat. As she watched, another kindled beyond it, and another, until a crooked line of lights ran out toward the horizon.

Juno's breath caught. She had heard stories of salt lanterns, lamps that burned in the drowned places and were never lit by any hand she knew. Her grandmother had called them the sea's own candles. Juno had always thought they were a tale to keep children from the water at night.

She looked back at the village. No one had come out. No one had seen. The bell was silent, and the sea was gone, and the lights were waiting.

Juno climbed down the wall and set her boots on the wet seabed. The mud gave under her like a soft hand, and the smell of salt and rot rose up around her. She walked toward the first light.

***

A voice called her name from the top of the harbor steps. Juno turned and saw Tobin, his hair sticking up and his coat half on, one boot unlaced.

"Juno! What are you doing?" He scrambled down the wall, sliding in the weed. "The whole shore's empty. I woke up and the sea was just — gone."

"I know," she said. "And the bell's stopped. Listen." They stood still. The silence was enormous. "Something's wrong out there. There are lights."

Tobin followed her pointing finger. His face went pale in the moonlight. "Salt lanterns," he whispered. "My dad says they only burn when something's drowned and wants finding."

"Then something's drowned," Juno said. "And the bell won't ring to warn anyone. Come on."

They walked together along the line of lights. Each lantern was a squat glass jar half sunk in the mud, filled with coarse white salt that glowed from within, green and cold. The light cast their shadows long and thin across the seabed. Once, Tobin stepped on something that crunched, and he jumped, and Juno grabbed his sleeve and held it until he stopped shaking.

"What if the water comes back?" he said.

"Then we run," Juno said, though she did not know if that was true.

The lanterns led them out past the old mooring stones and the place where the fishing boats usually floated. They went farther still, to a deep hollow in the seabed. At the bottom of the hollow lay a great dark shape, crusted with barnacles and weed. The bell. The village's bell, or one like it, cracked down one side, its bronze green with age and salt. It was not hanging from anything. It lay on its side in the mud like a dead thing.

"It's the bell," Tobin breathed. "It fell. How did it fall?"

Juno knelt beside it and put her hand on the cold metal. The crack ran deep. Inside, the bell was dark and full of shadow.

"It didn't fall," she said slowly. "It was pulled down. Look at the rope marks." She pointed to the scored bronze at the crown. "Something took it, and now it can't ring, and the village doesn't know."

Tobin looked back the way they had come. "Juno," he said. His voice had gone thin. "The lanterns are going out."

She turned. Behind them, one by one, the green lights were dimming, sinking back into the mud. And far off, where the sea should be, there was a low sound like a long inhale.

The water was coming back.

They ran for the keeper's house, the one that stood on stilts at the edge of the cliff where the bell had hung. The door was unlocked. Nobody locked anything in the village. Inside it smelled of tar and old rope, and the walls were lined with shelves of logbooks bound in oilskin.

Juno pulled down the newest one. Her hands were shaking so badly she could hardly turn the pages. "Read it," she said, pushing it at Tobin. "You read faster."

He read aloud, his finger moving down the page. "The bell must be rung from inside. The clapper is not enough. When the sea takes the bell, the keeper must go down into it and strike the note with the whole body, or the note will not carry far enough to turn the tide." He stopped. "Juno, that's the last entry. It's dated forty years ago."

"Keep going."

"There's nothing after. The keeper wrote one more line." Tobin's voice dropped. "'I am going down. If the bell does not ring, do not follow me.'"

Outside, the low inhale had become a rumble. The green lanterns were dead now, all of them, and the sea was coming back across the flats in a wall of black water that swallowed the mud as it came.

"We have to go," Tobin said. "We have to get up the cliff."

"The bell has to ring." Juno was already at the door. "If it doesn't ring, the boats come home tonight with no light to guide them. You know what the rocks are like."

"Then I'll go in."

"No."

"Juno, I'm taller. I can reach the crown."

She turned on him, and her voice cracked with fear she was trying to hide. "You don't swim. You've never swum in your life. If that bell fills, you drown, and I'm not standing on the cliff watching it." She grabbed his arm. "I'll go. I'm smaller. I fit."

"That's not a reason."

"It's the only reason there is." The rumble was louder now, and the first cold spray of the returning sea stung her cheek. "Go up the cliff. Light the lamp in the window. If I ring it, you'll see the water turn. If I don't, run."

Tobin stared at her. Then he nodded, once, and ran.

***

Juno reached the bell with the water at her knees. It stood on its side in the mud, the crack gaping like a mouth, and the dark inside of it smelled of salt and rot and something older, like a cellar that had never once been opened.

She climbed in. Her feet found no floor, only a deep drift of old salt that shifted and gave way, and under the salt, cold water, black and still. It came up to her waist. She gasped and gripped the bronze.

The crown of the bell was above her, scored with the rope marks she had seen from outside. A chain hung down from it, and at the end of the chain there should have been a clapper, a heavy iron tongue to strike the bronze.

There was nothing. The chain ended in a broken link, and the clapper was gone, torn away, lost somewhere in the dark.

"No," she said. "No, no."

She felt along the inside of the bronze, searching, and her fingers found only the crack, the salt, the cold. The water was rising inside the bell now, faster than outside, or perhaps it only felt that way. It was at her ribs. The sea was pouring in through the crack.

She had a lantern. The salt lantern she had carried down, still burning green, its little iron handle hooked through her belt. She tore it loose. It was small, too light, but the handle was iron and solid, and iron against bronze would make a sound.

She wrapped the handle in her fist and struck the bronze. Once. It rang, a thin, cracked note that died against the roar of the water.

Not enough. She struck again, harder, and this time the note went out of the bell and into the dark, and the water around her shuddered. Again. The crack in the bell widened with each blow, and she could hear the bronze screaming, but she struck it anyway, and the note grew, and the water began to turn.

The water came back the way a held breath comes back, all at once and without mercy. Juno felt it lift her feet from the bell's floor, and through the crack she saw the dark sea standing up on the horizon like a wall. Out on the seabed, Tobin was still on the ladder, halfway down, and the returning tide was already between them.

"Climb!" she shouted, though her voice inside the bronze came back to her flat and strange. "Tobin, climb!"

He turned. She saw him understand, saw him look at the water and then at the bell, and she saw him do the arithmetic and find it wrong. The distance was too far. The water was too fast. He would never cross it in time, and if he tried, it would take him.

"Don't you dare," he called, and his voice cracked on the last word. "Don't you dare come out here for me."

The water reached the ladder. It took his ankles, then his knees, and he clung to the rungs with both arms and looked back at her across a widening silver gap that was filling fast.

Juno understood the thing she had not understood before: the bell was not for her. It had never been for her. It was for everyone standing in the water, and if she did not make it speak, the sea would take them all, and it would take Tobin first, and it would be her fault for arguing instead of ringing.

She wrapped the iron handle in her fist and struck the bronze. Once. It rang thin and cracked, and the water outside shook but did not stop.

"Again!" Tobin shouted. The tide was at his chest now. He had stopped climbing. He was holding on and watching her, and his face was very calm in a way she hated.

She struck again, and this time she put her whole body behind it, and the note came out of the bell like something alive. The bronze split further. She felt the crack travel under her hand with a sound like a bone giving way, and a shard of light came through, and she did not stop. Again. Again. Each blow broke the bell a little more and woke the note a little wider, and the note was not a thin dead thing now; it was low and long and it went out across the water and did not die.

The returning sea met the sound and slowed. She saw it happen through the crack: the wall of water checked, shivered, and began to lean back, as if it had remembered something it had forgotten, as if the bell had reminded it of its proper place.

"It's turning!" Tobin's voice, wild now, half a laugh. "Juno, it's turning!"

She struck the bell one last time, and the bronze rang out full and ruined and true, and the sea went back down the shore like a dog called home.

***

Juno climbed out of the bell into air that tasted of iron and salt and morning. The seabed was still wet, but it was seabed again, and the water stood off at a distance, sulking at the edge of the world. Tobin was on the ladder, soaked to the shoulders, both arms wrapped around a rung, breathing as if he had forgotten how and was relearning it.

"You broke it," he said, when he could. "You broke the bell."

"I rang it," she said. Her hands would not stop shaking. There was blood on her knuckles and she did not remember cutting them. "It rang. That's the part that matters."

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he laughed. It was a raw, cracked sound that was almost a sob. She laughed too, and they stood there in the wet dark laughing at nothing, because they were alive.

They climbed the cliff path as the sky turned grey, and the village was already awake. Lamps moved along the shore. People stood at the edge of the water in their nightclothes. They stared at the seabed where the tide had been. They stared at the long bright line of salt lanterns still burning out across the flats. They stared at the drowned bell standing open and cracked and silent in the dawn.

The bell keeper met them at the top of the path. She was old and her hands were steady, and she looked at Juno's ruined knuckles and then at the bell, and something in her face changed.

"You rang it," she said. It was not a question.

"It was cracked," Juno said. "The clapper was gone. I used my lantern's handle. I think I made it worse."

The old woman looked out at the bell for a long time. The tide was well out now, and the first light was on the bronze, and the crack in it showed like a scar that had been earned.

"It will never ring the way it did," the keeper said at last. "That bell is finished. But it rang when it was needed, and it drove the water back, and it did not ring alone." She put her hand on Juno's shoulder, briefly, and her grip was warm. "Go home. Sleep. Tomorrow we will talk about what a bell is for."

Juno walked home through a village that had changed in the night. The shore was different. The lanterns still burned on the flats, and no one had gone out to fetch them, and no one would, not yet. At her door she stopped and looked back at the sea. It was small and grey and ordinary in the morning light. She thought that she would never again hear a bell without listening for the crack in it, and the note that got through anyway.
