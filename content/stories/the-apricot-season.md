Nora stepped off the bus into heat that smelled of dust and apricots. Her grandmother waited by the gate, straw hat in hand, and the orchard behind her looked smaller than Nora remembered. The rows of trees had thinned; gaps showed raw earth where trunks had been.

"You came," Gran said, and hugged her hard enough to hurt.

"Mom said you needed help." Nora pulled back. "She didn't say with what."

Gran picked up Nora's bag and started walking. "With the tree. The old one."

The apricot tree stood at the far end of the orchard, where the rows gave out and the field began. Its trunk was split down the middle, blackened at the wound, and half the branches were bare. The other half held leaves, but they curled at the edges like paper left in the sun.

Nora touched the split bark. It felt hot. "It's dying."

"It's been dying since before you were born," Gran said. She set the bag down and stood beside Nora. "But it's worse this year. The roots are going."

"Can't you cut it down? Plant a new one?"

Gran was quiet for a moment. "I could. But this tree was your great-grandmother's. It's the last one from the old stock. Everything else in this orchard came from it." She looked at Nora. "I'd like to try to save it. But I'm old, and my hands aren't what they were."

Nora looked at the split trunk, the curling leaves. "What do we do?"

"First," Gran said, "we prune. Tomorrow. Tonight, we eat, and you tell me what your mother's been feeding you."

They walked back to the house in the long evening light. The orchard hummed with insects, and somewhere a gate creaked. Nora looked back once at the dying tree, its bare branches black against the sky.

***

The next morning Gran handed Nora a pair of shears and stood her in front of the apricot tree. "Watch," she said. "Then you do it."

She reached into the canopy and cut a branch that crossed another. "You cut what rubs. You cut what grows inward. You leave the ones that reach for the sun." The shears clicked. "That's the rule. Simple."

Nora took the shears. The first branch was harder to judge than it looked. "This one?"

"That one's fine. It's reaching."

They worked through the morning. Gran hummed while she worked, a low tune with no words, and after a while Nora caught the rhythm of it. "What's that song?"

Gran stopped humming. "An old pruning song. My mother sang it. I haven't thought of it in years." She cut another branch. "The words are about the tree and the seasons and what you give it. You want to learn it?"

"I don't know it."

"You will." Gran straightened. "It starts: 'Old tree, old tree, lean on me.'"

Nora repeated it. The tune was simple, almost a lullaby. "What comes next?"

"'I cut the dead, I keep the green.'" Gran's voice was rough. "'I leave the branch that bears the fruit, and the roots that hold the rest unseen.'"

Nora sang it back, and the words fit the work. She cut a branch that rubbed against another, and the shears felt lighter in her hand.

"My mother sang that to me when I was your age," Gran said. "She said the tree liked it. Said it grew better when it heard the song."

"Do you believe that?"

Gran smiled. "I believe the tree's still here. That's enough." She pointed at a branch near the split. "Now that one. It's growing into the wound. Cut it clean."

Nora cut, and the branch fell. The tree looked barer than before, but the remaining branches stood clear of each other. For the first time, she could see the shape of it: the trunk splitting into two main limbs, each one reaching out and up, like arms.

"It's like a person," she said.

"It's like this family," Gran said. "Split down the middle and still standing." She picked up her shears. "Come on. We're not done."

The storm came three nights later, and it did not knock. It shouldered the orchard like a drunk at a door. Nora woke to rain hitting the window in handfuls and the apricot tree creaking somewhere out in the dark. Gran was already up, standing at the glass with her robe pulled tight. "It's the split," she said. "The wind's got into it."

They went out together. The rain was warm and blinding. Nora held the flashlight while Gran braced her shoulder against the trunk, and the whole tree shuddered above them. The split had opened wider; one of the two main limbs leaned out over the fence like a tired arm. Bark had peeled back in a long strip, showing pale wood underneath. When the wind eased for a moment, Nora heard a crack deep in the trunk, quiet and final.

Back inside, Gran sat at the kitchen table and did not move for a long time. Her hands were shaking. Nora made tea and put the cup in front of her, and Gran looked at it as if it had come from another house.

"We could cut it," Nora said. "Take it down before it falls on something."

"Yes."

"Or we could brace it. Prop the limb."

Gran lifted her head. "That's a fool's game. It's old. It's split. You'd be nursing a dead thing."

"It's not dead."

"It will be." Gran's voice was flat, and worse than angry. "I've watched it lean a little more every year. I sang to it, and it leaned anyway. Some things you can't sing back."

Nora thought of the pruning song, the words about roots holding the rest unseen. "You stopped singing," she said. It was not a question, but Gran answered it anyway.

"Your grandfather sang it with me. After he went, the words didn't fit my mouth anymore." She wrapped both hands around the cup. "Go to bed, Nora."

Nora went, but she did not sleep. Through the window, the tree stood in the rain like a person waiting to be told something, and by morning she had decided.

***

The reclusive neighbor lived at the end of the lane, past the last mailbox, in a house with shutters that never opened. Gran called him Old Merton and said he had been an orchard man before her time. "He'll not talk to you," she said when Nora told her the plan. "He's not talked to anyone in years." But she did not forbid it, and that was as good as permission.

Nora walked down the lane with the pruning song in her head and a photograph of the split tree in her pocket. The house smelled of woodsmoke and oil. When she knocked, a long silence answered, then a voice from behind the door: "Whatever it is, I'm not buying."

"I'm not selling," Nora said. "I'm Nora. From the orchard up the lane. Our apricot's split, and my gran says you knew the old ways."

The door opened a hand's width. The man behind it was small and bent, with a face like a walnut and one eye clouded white. "The Halloway tree?" he said. "That tree's older than both of us. What's it done now?"

She showed him the photograph. He held it close to his good eye and made a sound low in his throat. "Storm split it along the old seam. It's been waiting to do that for fifty years."

"Can it be saved?"

He looked at her for a long moment, then opened the door wider. "Come in before the flies get in. There's a thing or two you can do, but you'll not like it."

His front room was crowded with tools and jars and lengths of waxed cord. He sat her down and drew a shape on the back of an envelope: the trunk, the split, the leaning limb. "You brace it," he said, tapping the pencil. "Not with rope. Rope rots. You want a prop under the limb and a bolt through the seam, and you want to pack the wound with beeswax and pine tar so the wet can't get in." He looked up. "But a bolted tree is a tree that owes you. You'll be watching it every season. Some years it'll give you fruit, and some years it'll give you nothing but worry."

"My gran says it's dead already."

"Your gran's been saying that since her husband passed. The tree's not dead. It's tired, same as her." He set down the pencil. "You'll need to keep it warm tonight. There's a cold coming, and a wounded tree feels cold worse than a whole one. Old blanket round the trunk, hot stones at the roots. And someone to sit with it."

Nora stood. "Will you help me?"

Merton was quiet. Then he reached for his coat. "I'll get my lantern," he said. "It's been a while since anyone asked me for anything worth doing."

The cold came down the valley like water finding a crack. Merton set his lantern on a stump and the light made a small yellow room of the orchard. Nora wrapped the old blanket around the trunk while he buried hot stones from the stove at the roots, one by one, with a garden trowel.

"Don't pack them tight," he said. "Let the heat breathe up. A tree warms from the ground the way a person does from the feet."

Nora knelt and arranged the stones in a ring. The bark under the blanket felt rough and cold, but when she pressed her palm flat against it there was a faint warmth underneath, like a hand under a pillow.

"It's still alive," she whispered.

"It's been alive longer than you've been on this earth," Merton said. He sat on an upturned crate and pulled his coat tighter. "Your gran and I planted it the spring after she married. She sang to it then. Every pruning, every grafting, she sang."

Nora looked at the house. The kitchen window was dark. "She doesn't sing now."

"No." Merton's voice was low. "She stopped the year your grandfather died. The tree was his, mostly. He did the cutting and she did the singing. After he went, she couldn't find the tune." He picked up a stone and turned it in his hands. "I think she thought if she sang to it without him, it would be like pretending he was still here. So she let it go quiet. And a tree that goes quiet goes tired."

The wind picked up and the lantern flame leaned. Nora put another stone in place and tucked the blanket's edge under a root. "My gran taught me the pruning song this summer," she said. "She said her mother taught her."

Merton nodded slowly. "Then you know it."

"I know the words."

"That's the same thing, if you mean them." He stood and brushed the dirt from his knees. "I'll keep the fire going in the stove. You stay out here as long as you can stand it. Talk to it, if you want. It doesn't need to understand. It just needs to know it's not alone."

He went inside, and Nora sat on the cold ground with her back against the trunk, and she sang the pruning song very softly. The words were about opening and closing, about taking only what you need, about waiting for the right season. Her breath made clouds. The stones gave off their heat. After a while she wasn't sure if she was singing to the tree or to her grandmother, or to the empty place where her grandfather used to stand.

***

The last apricot hung on the highest branch, and it took them three days to decide it was ready. Merton brought a ladder and held it while Nora climbed. The fruit was small and freckled with orange, soft at the tip.

"It's the only one," she called down.

"Then it's the only one," Merton said. "Bring it careful."

She carried it in both hands like an egg. Her grandmother was sitting on the porch, a blanket over her knees, watching. She had not come out to the orchard since the storm. Nora walked up the steps and held out the apricot.

Her grandmother looked at it for a long time. Then she took it and turned it over in her fingers, and her mouth did something that was almost a smile and almost not.

"I thought I'd never see another," she said.

"Merton says the tree owes us now," Nora said. "He says some years it'll give and some it won't."

"He's right about that."

Nora sat down on the step beside her. "You should have some. It's yours."

Her grandmother was quiet. Then she reached into her apron pocket and took out a small knife, the one she used for peeling apples, and she cut the apricot in half. The flesh was bright and wet. She gave one half to Nora.

"Eat it slow," she said. "It's the last of the summer."

They ate it on the porch while the light went long and gold across the orchard. The juice ran down Nora's wrist. Her grandmother hummed, low and off-key, and Nora realized it was the pruning song, the same tune she had sung to the tree in the dark. Neither of them said anything about it. The tree stood where it had always stood, propped and patched and warm, and the cold was still coming, and the wind was still going to blow, but the fruit had ripened and they had eaten it together, and that was enough. That was what home was, Nora thought. Not a place that never changes. A place you keep singing to, even when the tune is hard to find.
