Milo's sock drawer had a hole in it. Not a hole in the wood — a hole in the sock supply. On Monday there were seven socks. On Tuesday there were four. By Wednesday morning, the drawer held one lonely grey sock and a smell of clean laundry.

"Dad!" Milo shouted down the stairs. "My socks are gone again!"

"Check under your bed," Dad called back.

Milo checked. He found a pencil, two crumbs, and half a sticker. No socks. He thumped down to the kitchen, where his sister Rosa was eating cereal and humming.

"You took them," Milo said. "Admit it."

Rosa put down her spoon. "Why would I want your smelly socks?"

"Because you're a sock thief," Milo said. "A sneaky one. A sneaky sock-stealing—"

"Milo," said Dad, from the doorway. "That's enough shouting. Apologise to your sister."

"But my socks!"

"And no screen time tonight," Dad said. "You can sit in your room and think about your manners."

Milo stomped upstairs. From the laundry basket in the hall he pulled out one striped sock. Its partner was missing. Again. He held it up like evidence at a trial.

"Somebody is taking them," he muttered. "And it isn't Rosa."

That was the trouble. If it wasn't Rosa, who was it? Milo sat on his bed and stared at the drawer. The house was very quiet. Somewhere below, the washing machine hummed its one long note.

"Fine," he said. "I'll catch you myself."

***

That night Milo built a trap. He laid a pair of striped socks on the floor by the laundry basket. Then he tied a bell to a long piece of string and stretched the string across the hallway, knee-high, where nobody tall would walk.

"What is that?" Rosa asked, leaning over the banister.

"A scientific experiment," Milo said.

"It's a tripwire."

"It's a scientific tripwire."

Rosa shrugged and went to bed. Milo sat in the dark beside the wall, wrapped in his blanket, and waited. The clock ticked. The fridge hummed. He yawned twice and pinched himself awake.

Around midnight, something moved. Small footsteps pattered along the hallway, light as raindrops. Then — ping! The bell rang, and a squeak followed it.

Milo leapt up and switched on the light. There, tangled in his string, sat a tiny creature about the size of a teacup. It wore a coat made of knitted wool and a hat shaped like a thimble. It had a long nose and enormous ears.

"Let go!" it squeaked, wriggling. "You're squashing my elbow!"

Milo crouched down, eyes wide. "You're a — what are you?"

The creature stopped wriggling and looked offended. "I'm a sock gnome," it said. "Obviously. And you, sir, are standing on my hat."

Milo stepped back. The little thing dusted itself off and folded its arms.

"I suppose you want an explanation," the sock gnome said. "Everyone does."

"Yes," said Milo. "But first — where are all my socks?"

"Socks," said the gnome, "are collected. Gathered. Rescued." He tugged a loose thread on his sleeve. "My name is Pipkin, by the way. Pipkin Treadle, Third Sock of the Loom."

"That's a lot of names," said Milo.

"It's a lot of socks." Pipkin climbed onto Milo's overturned shoe and sat down like it was a bench. "Here's the thing. Socks get lost. You know this. One goes into the wash and two come out, or none do, or a sock turns up somewhere strange, like the freezer."

Milo thought about the sock he had found in the freezer last winter. He had blamed his dad.

"Where do they go?" he asked.

"To us," said Pipkin, spreading his little hands. "We take the lonely ones. The ones the dryer eats, the ones under the bed, the ones nobody claims. We mend them. We give them to socks who need a home."

Milo frowned. "So you're a sock thief."

"A sock rehomer," said Pipkin, offended again. "There's a difference. Ask any sock."

"My socks had a home. My drawer."

"Did they, though?" Pipkin raised one eyebrow, which took effort, because his eyebrows were wool. "When did you last wear the yellow ones with the ducks?"

Milo opened his mouth. Then closed it. The duck socks had been at the back of the drawer for a year.

"Exactly," said Pipkin kindly. "We don't take the favourites. We take the forgotten. Mostly." He coughed into his sleeve. "Occasionally there are mistakes."

Milo sat down on the floor. "You still shouldn't take things that aren't yours."

"Fair," said Pipkin, and he actually looked a bit ashamed. "You're not wrong. But you should see the village. It's all socks down there. Socks on the walls, socks on the beds, socks for curtains." His ears perked up. "You could come and see. Since you caught me and all. It's only polite."

Milo looked at the clock. It was very late. His mum would not be pleased if she found him up. But curiosity is a strong thing at midnight.

"Show me," he said.

***

Pipkin led him to the laundry cupboard, where the dryer sat humming in the dark. Milo had never noticed the little gap behind it, low down by the skirting board.

"Mind your head," said Pipkin, and slipped through.

Milo got down on his hands and knees. The gap was warm and smelled of clean cotton. He squeezed through and found himself in a tunnel lined with soft fabric, lit by tiny lanterns made from bottle caps.

"Keep up," called Pipkin. "The floor's a bit bouncy here."

The floor was a sock. The whole tunnel was made of socks, stitched together in a patchwork of stripes and spots. Milo crawled along it, and the walls gave gently under his hands.

Then the tunnel opened out, and he stopped.

Below him lay a village. Little houses stood in rows, each one knitted from a different sock — a red house, a blue house, a house with a toe for a chimney. Sock gnomes hurried along the lanes, carrying bundles of yarn. A sign above the square read WELCOME TO HEEL END.

"It's beautiful," Milo whispered.

"It's home," said Pipkin, and his voice went soft. "Come on. I'll show you the loom."

They climbed down a ladder made of shoelaces. Gnomes waved at Pipkin as they passed. One offered Milo a tiny cup of something warm, and he took it, because it seemed rude not to.

"This is lovely," he said.

"It's tea," said Pipkin. "Mostly."

Milo sipped it. It tasted of socks. He decided not to mention this. Then he noticed that the village square was strangely quiet, and that many of the little houses had holes in them, and that the gnomes kept glancing up at the ceiling as if something might come through it.

"Pipkin," he said. "Why does everyone look worried?"

Pipkin stopped walking. His ears drooped right down to his collar.

"Because," he said, "we have a problem. And it's got four legs and very sharp teeth."

“Four legs and very sharp teeth?” Milo repeated. “You mean a dog?”

Pipkin nodded miserably. “They got a new puppy. It arrived last week. It’s small and it’s bouncy and it thinks our village is a chew toy.”

He pointed to a row of houses with their roofs torn open. Fluff spilled out like stuffing. “That’s the bakery,” he said. “And that’s the school. The puppy got the school on Tuesday.”

Milo crouched down. The holes were exactly the size of a puppy’s mouth. “Can’t you just move?” he asked.

“We’ve been here for two hundred years,” said Pipkin. “This is home.”

A gnome in a bobbled hat hurried past, carrying a tiny bucket. “Pipkin! The puppy’s coming! It’s coming now!”

A thumping noise echoed above them. Dust sifted down from the ceiling. Gnomes squeaked and ran for cover. Milo looked up and saw a gap in the floorboards, and through it, a wet black nose.

“Right,” said Milo. “I’ve got an idea. Do you have anything the puppy really wants?”

Pipkin blinked. “It really wants our houses.”

“No, something else. Something it can chew that isn’t your home.”

A gnome held up a tiny shoe. “It likes shoes.”

“Perfect,” said Milo. “Give me that. And a long piece of yarn.”

***

Back in the laundry room, Milo tiptoed to the puppy’s bed. The puppy was called Biscuit, and Biscuit was chewing the corner of a rug. Milo dangled the tiny shoe on a string. Biscuit’s ears shot up.

“See this?” Milo whispered. “This is way better than a village.”

He tied the shoe to the end of the string and pulled it across the floor. Biscuit pounced. The shoe skidded under the dryer, and Biscuit followed, tail wagging like a flag.

Milo grabbed the baby gate from the cupboard and wedged it across the gap under the dryer. “There,” he said. “Now you can’t get in.”

Biscuit whined and pushed his nose through the bars, but the gate held. Milo tossed the shoe through the gap, and Biscuit lay down with it between his paws, chewing happily.

Pipkin popped out of a hole in the wall. “You did it!” he said. “The village is safe!”

“For now,” said Milo. “But you should probably find a new hiding place for your loom.”

Pipkin grinned. “We will. And we don’t forget a favour.”

He whistled, and three gnomes appeared, carrying a bundle. Inside were Milo’s socks, all of them, washed and folded. On top sat the striped pair, and a tiny knitted hat.

“For you,” said Pipkin. “From Heel End.”

Milo put on the hat. It fit perfectly. “Thanks,” he said. “And sorry for shouting at my sister.”

“Tell her,” said Pipkin. “That’s the important bit.”

Milo nodded. Then he climbed back through the hole, and the laundry room smelled of warm cotton and dog breath. He went to find his sister.
