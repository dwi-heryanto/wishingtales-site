Mara woke because the dark had changed. It was not the soft dark of her room at bedtime, friendly as a blanket. This dark pressed in at the window and scratched like a cat wanting in.

She sat up. The village lamp should have been glowing on the green, a steady yellow eye that never blinked. But when she looked, there was nothing. Just the empty post and the sky without stars.

"Mum?" she whispered. "The lamp is out."

Her mother was already at the door. Half the village was out on the grass, holding candles that guttered in the wind. Old Tam had his lantern, and little Bex was crying into her mother's skirt.

"The flame's gone," said Tam. His voice was low. "I've never seen it go out. Not once."

Mara stepped outside. The shadows along the wall moved when she moved. They stretched long fingers toward her boots. She took a quick step back, and they followed.

"It's only dark," she told herself. But her voice shook.

A woman called from the far end of the green. "The shadows are creeping past the well!"

Everyone turned. It was true. The dark was thicker there, sliding over the stones like water. It was slow, but it was coming.

Mara's heart beat hard. She had always been afraid of the dark. Not the dark of her own room, but this dark. The kind that had no edges.

Her mother put a hand on her shoulder. "Inside, love."

But Mara didn't move. She looked at the cold lamp post. She thought of the way the flame had always flickered goodnight to her. Every night of her life.

"Someone has to light it," she said.

Tam shook his head. "No one knows how. The flame came from somewhere far. No one's ever had to bring it back."

Mara squared her shoulders. The shadows crept closer. She could feel them, cold on her cheeks.

"Then I'll find out," she said.

***

Mara walked to the edge of the village, where the fields began. The grass was wet and the wind blew through her coat. Behind her, the candles of the village looked like tiny stars.

She had no idea where to go. No one did.

A rustle made her stop. Something small and quick slipped out of the hedge. It was a fox, red as a coal, with bright eyes.

"You're going the wrong way," said the fox.

Mara's mouth fell open. "You can talk."

"Of course I can talk," said the fox. "The question is, can you listen? The flame you need is east, in the Whispering Woods. There's a cave there with a spark that never dies."

Mara stared at him. "How do you know?"

"I know a lot of things," said the fox. "I also know you're afraid. That's all right. I'll walk with you."

She looked back at the village. The shadows had reached the first cottage wall.

"Why would you help me?" she asked.

The fox sat down and wrapped his tail around his paws. "Because the dark is no good for foxes either. And because you're the first person to come out here tonight. That counts for something."

Mara took a deep breath. "All right," she said. "Lead the way."

The fox turned and trotted toward the trees. Mara followed, one step, then another. The night was huge around her. But the fox's tail was a small red flag, and she kept her eyes on it.

The Whispering Woods began where the last garden ended. Mara stepped under the first branch, and the dark closed behind her like a door. The trees were tall and thin, and their leaves talked. Not words. Just whispers, like people telling secrets about you.

"What are they saying?" Mara asked.

The fox didn't look back. "They're saying it's dark. They're saying you're small. They're saying you should turn around."

"Are they right?"

"About the dark? Yes. About you? No." He trotted on. "Keep walking."

Mara's hands were cold. She wanted to hold something, but the jar of embers was all she had, and she needed both hands to carry it. The whispers grew louder. A branch brushed her cheek like a finger. She stopped.

"I can't see anything," she said. Her voice shook. "I can't see my own feet."

The fox came back and sat in front of her. His eyes were two small lamps. "What can you see?"

"You."

"Good. See me. That's enough for now." He stood. "The dark is only dark. It can't hurt you. It can only hide things. And hiding isn't hurting."

Mara breathed in. The air smelled of wet leaves and old wood. She took a step. Then another. The whispers faded to a hum, then to nothing. She could hear her own feet on the path. She could hear the fox's soft paws. The dark was still there, but it was just dark now. Not a monster. Just a room with the lamp off.

"I'm still scared," she said.

"Good," said the fox. "Scared is fine. Scared and walking is brave."

They walked until the trees thinned. Ahead, a bridge crossed a gully. It was made of rope and old planks, and it swayed in the wind. Mara stopped at the edge.

***

The bridge groaned when Mara put her foot on it. The planks were grey and cracked. Ropes hung loose like tired arms. Below, the gully was full of mist, and the mist was full of sound. Water, maybe. Or wind. Or something else.

"Is it safe?" Mara asked.

The fox tested a plank with one paw. It creaked. "Safe enough," he said. "If we go one at a time. I'll go first."

He trotted across, light as a leaf. The bridge barely moved. On the other side, he turned and waited. "Your turn. Slow and steady. Don't look down."

Mara looked down. The mist swirled. She looked up again, fast. "I can't."

"You can. One step. Then the next."

She took a step. The bridge swayed. She took another. The jar of embers was heavy in her arms, and the glass was warm. Halfway across, a plank snapped under her foot. She jumped forward. The jar slipped. She caught it, but the lid popped off. Embers spilled into the mist like tiny orange stars. They hissed and went out.

"No!" Mara grabbed for them, but they were gone. The jar was empty. Cold.

The bridge lurched. Another plank broke. Mara dropped the jar and grabbed the rope. The jar fell into the mist and didn't make a sound. She hung there, swinging, her hands burning.

"Fox!" she shouted.

"Hold on!" He was already running back. "Don't let go!"

The rope bit into her fingers. The mist was cold on her face. She couldn't see the fox. She couldn't see anything but grey. "I'm slipping!"

"Then don't slip," said the fox, right beside her. His teeth caught her sleeve. "Climb. I've got you."

Mara pulled. Her arms shook. The rope creaked. She got one knee onto the last plank, then both. The fox tugged. She rolled onto solid ground and lay there, breathing hard. The bridge swung empty behind her. The jar was gone. The embers were gone. Everything was gone.

"I lost it," she whispered. "I lost the light."

The fox sat beside her. His fur was warm. "You didn't lose you," he said. "That's the part that matters."

Mara sat up. Her hands were raw. The dark was all around, and now it had no embers to push against it. She felt small again. But the fox was there. And somewhere east, a cave was waiting.

Mara followed the fox into the dark, her raw hands stuffed in her pockets. The ground rose, and the trees thinned. "How much farther?" she asked.

"Not far," said the fox. "The cave is just past that ridge."

"I can't see a ridge."

"You will," he said. "The moth will show us."

As he spoke, a pale flutter drifted out of the night. It was a moth, big as Mara's palm, with wings like moonlight on water. It circled once around her head, then floated ahead.

"Follow it," said the fox.

They climbed. Mara's legs ached. The moth's glow lit one stone at a time, just enough to step. Behind her, the Whispering Woods had gone quiet. Ahead, the dark pressed close, but the moth never let it swallow them.

At the top of the ridge, a cave mouth opened in the rock. Cold air breathed out of it. Mara stopped.

"I don't want to go in there," she said.

The fox sat down. "Neither do I," he said. "But you don't have to want to. You just have to go."

The moth drifted inside. Its light grew small, then smaller.

Mara took a breath. She thought of the village lamp, dark and cold. She thought of her mother waiting. Then she walked in.

The cave was cold and smelled of wet stone. The moth hovered over a crack in the floor. Inside the crack, something glowed. One tiny spark, no bigger than a crumb.

"There," said the fox. "That's the last fire in the hills."

Mara knelt. Her hands were still shaking. She cupped them around the spark, and it warmed her fingers.

"It's so small," she said.

"So are you," said the fox. "And you're enough."

She lifted the spark carefully. It didn't go out. The moth landed on her shoulder, and its light wrapped around her like a blanket. Together, they started back down the ridge.

***

Morning was still far off when Mara reached the village. The lamp stood in the square, cold and grey. People huddled on their doorsteps, wrapped in blankets. They saw her and went quiet.

"Mara?" called her mother. "Is that you?"

"It's me," said Mara. "I brought a light."

She climbed the three stone steps to the lamp. Her hands ached. The spark in her palms flickered, tiny and stubborn. She tipped it into the bowl of the lamp.

For a moment, nothing happened.

"It's too small," whispered a neighbour.

Mara leaned close. "Come on," she said. "You can do it. I did."

She blew on the spark, gentle, like blowing on soup. It shivered. Then it caught. Flame ran around the wick, gold and warm, and the whole square filled with light.

People cheered. Her mother hugged her. The fox sat at the edge of the light and yawned.

"You did it," he said.

"We did it," said Mara. "The moth too."

But when she looked, the moth was gone. Only the fox remained, and he was already fading, like breath on a window.

"Will I see you again?" she asked.

"When you need me," he said. "You know where to look."

Then he was only a shape in the dark, and then nothing.

Mara stood by the lamp. The light was steady now. She touched her chest, where something warm still sat, small and bright. It had been there all along. She just hadn't known how to find it.

That night, she slept with the window open. The lamp burned on, and so did she.
