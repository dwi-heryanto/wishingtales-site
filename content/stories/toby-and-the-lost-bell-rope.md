Toby's father pulled on his oilskin coat so fast the buttons clicked. The lamp in the tower still turned above them, throwing its slow white arm across the water.

"A boat's gone onto the rocks past Gull Point," his father said. "I'm taking the launch. You watch the fog bell, Toby. If the fog rolls in thick, you ring it. Understand?"

"I understand," Toby said. He stood up straight, though his stomach felt like a bag of eels.

His father paused at the door. "It's just a rope and a bell. Pull steady. That's all."

Then he was gone, and the launch engine coughed and faded into the dark.

Toby climbed to the bell platform. The fog bell hung in its iron frame, heavy and green with salt. A thick rope ran from the bell's lever down through a hole in the floor. You pulled the rope, and the bell swung and spoke.

He put his hand on the rope to feel if it was sound.

It came apart in his fingers. The old fibers crumbled like dry bread, and the frayed end dropped and swung, useless.

Toby stared at it. Out on the water, the fog was gathering in gray folds, quiet and slow. Somewhere beyond it, a boat was in trouble, and his father was out there too.

"All right," Toby said, to hear his own voice. "The rope's gone. I need a rope."

***

He knelt and examined the broken end. Maybe he could knot it back together.

His fingers worked quickly, making a splice the way his father had shown him. He pulled it tight. The knot held for a moment. Then the old rope split again, a hand's width higher up, and the bell's lever stayed silent.

Toby sat back on his heels. The whole rope was rotten, soft as wet string all the way through. Knotting it was no good. He needed a new one.

Then he remembered. In the boathouse, on a peg by the door, hung a coil of spare rope, thick and yellow and strong. His father had bought it in the spring.

The boathouse sat at the end of the long pier. To reach it, Toby would have to go down the tower stairs, out into the wind, and across the wet boards in the dark.

He looked at the fog. It was thicker now, swallowing the end of the pier.

"Toby?" called a voice from below. It was Nell, the harbor master's girl, her lantern bobbing at the tower door. "Your dad told me to check on you. Is the bell ready?"

"Not yet," Toby called down. "The rope snapped. I have to fetch the spare from the boathouse."

A pause. "In this fog?" Nell said. "I'll come with you."

"No," Toby said, surprising himself. "Someone should stay and watch the water. I'll be quick."

He took the lantern from its hook and started down the stairs.

The tower door swung open and the wind grabbed it, slamming it back against the stone. Toby leaned into the gust, lantern swinging wild, and stepped out onto the path. Fog curled around his ankles like cold smoke. Somewhere below, waves smacked the harbor wall, hard and steady.

He made it down the tower steps and onto the shore road. Halfway along, the wind turned mean. It pushed at his chest and pulled at his coat, and the lantern flame guttered until it was no bigger than a pea.

"Come on," Toby muttered to himself. "It's just wind."

At the pier, the boards were slick with spray. Toby put one foot out, then the other, holding the lantern low. A wave burst against the pilings and flung cold water across his boots. The pier was only two planks wide, and the fog hid where it ended.

He took three more steps. Then his foot slid.

Toby dropped to his knees and caught the edge with one hand. The lantern clattered, wobbled, and stayed lit. His heart was hammering so loud he could hear it over the sea.

"I could go back," he whispered. "Nell's there. She'd go."

But the boats were out in this fog, and the bell was the only thing that could call them home. Toby set the lantern down between his knees and breathed. Then he stood, slower this time, and felt each plank before he trusted it. One at a time. Like the game where you step on the cracked stones.

The boathouse loomed out of the fog at last, dark and small, its door banging softly on its hinge.

***

Inside, the boathouse smelled of tar and salt. Toby held the lantern high and found the peg by the door. The coil of yellow rope hung there, thick as his wrist, wound tight and tied with twine.

He lifted it down. It was heavier than he expected. He looped it over his shoulder and staggered out into the wind.

The wind had grown teeth. It shoved him sideways along the pier, and the rope dug into his shoulder. Twice he had to stop and grip the rail. Once a wave came over the boards and soaked him to the knees.

"Almost there," he told himself. "Almost there."

His arms ached. The rope slipped, and he hoisted it back up. Fog pressed close, and the tower light was only a pale smudge above him.

Then, through the wind, came a voice. "Toby! Toby, is that you?"

Nell stood at the shore end of the pier, lantern up, squinting into the fog. When she saw him, she hurried onto the boards without waiting for an answer.

"Give me half," she said, and took the back of the coil before he could argue. The weight lifted, and Toby breathed out.

"I told you to stay," he said.

"You told me," Nell said, hauling the rope up. "I watched the water first. Nothing's come in. Now walk."

Together they crossed the last wet planks, and the wind seemed smaller with two of them leaning against it.

The tower door banged shut behind them, and the quiet made Toby's ears ring. He dumped the coil on the bottom step and rubbed his shoulder. "Up," he said. "The bell's at the top."

Nell looked at the stairs. They spiralled up into the dark, worn smooth in the middle. "I'll hold the lantern low so you can see your feet," she said.

Toby took the front of the coil and started up. The rope was heavy and cold, and it banged against his shins on every turn. Halfway up, his legs began to shake. Nell's lantern threw their shadows big on the curved wall.

"Don't stop," she said. "If you stop, you'll want to sit down."

So he didn't stop. At the top, the bell hung in its wooden frame, grey-green and wide as a cartwheel. The old rope dangled from the headstock, frayed and useless. Toby dropped the new coil, pulled the loose end through the wheel, and began to tie. His fingers were stiff. The first knot slid loose, and he had to start again.

"Pull it tighter," Nell said. "Like the net knots."

He pulled it tighter. The second knot held. He wrapped the tail twice and tucked it under, the way his father had taught him. Then he stepped back and pulled the rope.

The bell moved, just a little. But no sound came out.

Toby pulled again, harder. Nothing.

"It's stuck," he said. "Something's stuck."

***

Nell lifted the lantern to the mouth of the bell. "There," she said. "Look."

Inside, the clapper hung crooked. A strip of torn canvas, blown in by some old wind, had wrapped itself around the clapper's arm and jammed it against the side. It was wound tight and stiff with salt.

"I can't reach it," Toby said. The bell's mouth was above his head.

"Climb the frame," Nell said. "I'll steady you."

He put his foot on the crossbeam and hauled himself up. The frame creaked. Nell gripped his ankle with both hands. Toby leaned in through the mouth of the bell, and the dark inside smelled of rust and old rain. His fingers found the canvas strip. He pulled. It didn't move.

"Harder," Nell said.

He set his teeth and pulled again, and the canvas tore away in his hand. The clapper swung free and knocked against the bell's side with a dull clank. Toby almost fell.

"Down!" Nell shouted.

He dropped to the floor. "Now pull the rope!" he said. "Both of us!"

They each took a length of rope and leaned back. The bell swung. It rang out, one deep note that rolled through the tower and out over the water. Then again. And again. Toby's arms burned, but he kept pulling, and Nell kept pulling, and the bell kept ringing.

Outside, the storm broke at last. Rain hammered the windows, but through it Toby heard an answering sound: a boat horn, low and long, from out in the fog. Then another.

"They heard it," Nell said. "They're coming in."

They rang the bell until their hands were raw. Then, far below, a door opened, and a voice called up the stairs, wet and hoarse and wonderful.

"Toby? Toby, lad!"

Toby let go of the rope and ran down the stairs two at a time. His father stood in the doorway, dripping, his big coat dark with rain. He looked at the rope in Toby's hands, then up the stairs at the bell, still swinging.

"You rang it," his father said. "You rang it yourself."

"Nell helped," Toby said.

His father put a hand on each of their shoulders. "Then you both did," he said. "Every boat's home safe. Every one." He smiled, tired and proud. "Now let's get dry."
