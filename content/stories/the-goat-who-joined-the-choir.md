Every Tuesday the Plumford choir met in the village hall, and every Tuesday the village pretended not to hear.

"Again from the top!" called Mrs. Pemberly, rapping her stick on the piano. "And this time, please, listen to each other."

The choir drew a breath. Then they sang. It was not quite singing. It was more like a cart full of saucepans rolling down a hill. The sopranos were too high. The altos were too low. Old Mr. Fitch, who sang bass, mostly just hummed and hoped for the best.

When the last note wobbled away, there was a silence.

"Well," said Mrs. Pemberly, taking off her glasses. "Well."

"It's my throat," said Mrs. Tutley. "It's the damp."

"It's the song," said Mr. Fitch. "Too many notes in it."

Mrs. Pemberly tapped her stick again. "The harvest concert is in three weeks. We cannot play saucepans at a harvest concert." She looked along the row of faces. "We need one more voice. A high one. Something that floats on top of all this."

"My cousin's boy has a lovely voice," said Mrs. Tutley.

"Your cousin's boy lives in Whitby," said Mrs. Pemberly. "We need someone here."

Nobody had an answer. The choir shuffled their sheet music and looked at the floor. Outside, the wind pushed at the hall door, which had never quite shut properly since the summer fair.

***

The door opened about four inches. Then a head came through it sideways, and the rest of the goat followed.

It was a shaggy grey goat with one bent horn and a look of enormous confidence. It walked straight up the aisle, as if it were late for choir practice too.

"Who let that in?" said Mr. Fitch.

"Nobody let it in," said Mrs. Pemberly. "It let itself in."

The goat stopped at the front row. It sniffed the air, then lowered its head and took a calm, steady bite out of Mrs. Tutley's sheet music.

"It's eating the song!" wailed Mrs. Tutley.

"It's eating the high notes," said Mr. Fitch, who was watching closely.

The choir rushed at the goat. The goat did not rush anywhere. It simply moved along the row and took another bite, then another. Pages fluttered. Paper crunched. Somebody's glasses fell off.

"Gently!" cried Mrs. Pemberly. "Gently, it's a goat, not a fire!"

The goat finished the soprano part, looked up with a piece of paper hanging from its mouth, and chewed with great satisfaction. Then it sat down, right in the middle of the aisle, like a dog that had lived there for years.

"It's sitting," said Mrs. Tutley.

"It is," said Mrs. Pemberly. "It very much is."

The choir stood around the goat in a ring, breathing hard, and nobody knew what to do next.

Mr. Fitch tried first. He was tall and had a voice like a friendly door creaking, so he leaned over the goat and said, "Out you go, my friend." The goat looked at him and chewed. Mr. Fitch waved his hat. The goat ate a corner of the hat.

"Not the hat!" cried Mrs. Tutley.

"It's only an old hat," said Mr. Fitch, but he held it behind his back after that.

Mrs. Pemberly took charge the way she always did, with her hands on her hips. "Everyone," she said, "make a wide line. We shall walk forward and gently herd it to the door." The choir made a wide line. They walked forward. The goat walked backward, keeping exactly the same distance, and then sat down again with a thump. "It's herding us," said little Mr. Oakes from the back row. "Don't be silly," said Mrs. Pemberly, but she sounded less sure.

Mrs. Tutley fetched a broom and held it out like a sword. The goat ate the bristles. It did not seem angry. It seemed to think this was a party, and everyone had brought snacks.

"Right," said Mrs. Pemberly. "We shall ignore it and finish the rehearsal. Goats hate being ignored." So the choir stood in their rows and began the harvest song again. They sang about golden fields and full barns. They sang it the way they always sang, which was loudly and slightly wrong.

And then, from the aisle, came a sound.

It was a high, clear, steady note, and it landed right on top of their wobbly tune. The choir stopped. The goat stopped. Everyone looked at everyone else.

"Sing again," said Mrs. Pemberly slowly. They sang again. The goat lifted its chin and bleated, and the bleat was a note, a real one, clean as a bell.

***

Mrs. Pemberly stood very still. She had been leading the choir for eleven years, and she knew a thing or two about notes.

"Again," she said. "From the top. And nobody look at the goat."

The choir sang. The goat bleated. Mrs. Pemberly closed her eyes and listened with her whole face. When the song ended, she opened them and said, "It's an E. A perfect, shining E."

"Is that good?" asked Mr. Oakes.

"It's the note we've been missing for eleven years," said Mrs. Pemberly. "The one I keep asking the sopranos for. The one nobody can reach without going red in the face."

Mrs. Tutley went red in the face just thinking about it. "But it's a goat," she said. "We can't have a goat in the harvest concert. People will talk."

"People already talk," said Mrs. Pemberly. "They talk about how we sing like a wheelbarrow full of spoons." She turned to the goat. "Can you do it again?"

The goat blinked its pale eyes. Then it tilted its head back and let out the E, long and true, and held it while Mr. Fitch counted to eight.

"Well," said Mr. Fitch. "Well, well, well."

Mrs. Pemberly knelt down, which took some doing in her good shoes. "I don't know your name," she told the goat, "so I shall call you Trill." The goat chewed a little paper and seemed pleased. "Trill," said Mrs. Pemberly, "you are joining the choir." The choir made a surprised noise all together, like a flock of pigeons. "Rehearsals every Tuesday," she said. "And no eating the music until after we've sung it." Trill the goat bleated her perfect E, and the whole hall seemed to hum along.

The night of the harvest concert, the whole village crowded into the hall. Lanterns hung from the beams, and the stage smelled of fresh apples. Mrs. Pemberly gathered the choir backstage in her good shoes and her best hat. "Where is Trill?" she asked.

Everyone looked around. Mr. Fitch checked behind the curtain. Tutley checked under the piano. No goat.

"She was here a minute ago," said Tutley. "I gave her a bit of paper to settle her nerves."

A small bleat came from inside the big wooden cupboard where they kept the songbooks. Mrs. Pemberly opened the door. Two pale eyes blinked in the dark.

"Trill," she said gently, "it's time."

Trill did not move. She had stage fright.

"She's scared," whispered Tutley. "Goats don't like crowds."

"Neither do I," said Mr. Fitch. "But we go on anyway."

Mrs. Pemberly knelt down by the cupboard. "Trill, you have the best note in this whole choir. We need you."

The goat only pressed herself further into the corner. The audience outside was humming and shuffling. In two minutes the curtain would go up.

Tutley had an idea. She pulled a sheet of music from her pocket, the one with the high E on it, and held it out. "Look, Trill. Your note. Remember?"

Trill's ears twitched. She took a step forward and nibbled the corner of the paper. Then she bleated, soft and shaky at first, then strong and clear. The perfect E filled the little backstage space.

"That's it," said Mrs. Pemberly. "That's our girl."

***

The curtain rose. The choir stood in two rows, and there, in the front, stood Trill with a red ribbon around her neck. The audience laughed a little, then went quiet.

Mrs. Pemberly raised her hands. The choir began to sing. They were still a bit like a wheelbarrow full of spoons, but when they reached the high part, Trill lifted her head and let out her note. It rang out over the whole hall, sweet and sure.

People sat up straighter. Old Mr. Hodges stopped frowning at his program. The note held, and the choir's sound came together around it like a hand fitting into a glove.

When the song ended, there was a moment of silence. Then the clapping started, loud and long. Someone stamped their boots. Someone else whistled.

"Encore!" shouted a voice from the back.

The choir sang two more songs, and Trill bleated her E in every one. By the last song, the audience was singing along.

Afterwards, in the crowded hall, Mrs. Pemberly found Tutley. "We did it," she said. "We actually did it."

Tutley looked at Trill, who was calmly eating a fallen program. "She's not just a goat," said Tutley. "She's our goat."

Mrs. Pemberly smiled. "Official mascot," she said. "I'll have a badge made."

Trill bleated her perfect E one last time, and the whole choir laughed.
