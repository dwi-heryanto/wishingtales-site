Ana had never kept a plant alive. Not the cactus on her windowsill, not the parsley in the little pot from the school fair. Both had turned brown and quiet within a month. So when Mrs. Alvarez stood on her porch with a watering can and a worried look, Ana almost said no.

"It's just the window box," Mrs. Alvarez said. She was leaving for her sister's house for two weeks. "Water it every evening, just until the soil looks dark. That's all."

Ana looked at the box under Mrs. Alvarez's window. Green leaves spilled over the edges. Tiny buds, closed tight like little fists, dotted the stems.

"What are they?" Ana asked.

"Moonflowers," said Mrs. Alvarez. "They're night bloomers. You'll see."

Ana took the watering can. "I'll try," she said. "But I'm not very good at this."

Mrs. Alvarez smiled. "Trying is the whole thing," she said, and got into her car.

***

That first evening, Ana filled the watering can and carried it next door. The street was quiet. A dog barked somewhere far off. She tipped the can and watched the water soak into the dark soil. The leaves shivered a little. The buds stayed closed.

"Okay," Ana told them. "I'll be back tomorrow."

She went home, did her homework, and went to bed. At midnight she woke up thirsty. Moonlight poured through her window, bright and silver. Something made her look out at Mrs. Alvarez's house.

The window box was glowing. Or not glowing, exactly, but the flowers were open wide. Each one was pale and round as a saucer, shining in the moonlight. They looked like little moons themselves, soft and white against the dark leaves.

Ana pressed her nose to the glass. "Wow," she whispered.

She watched for a long time. The flowers swayed a little in the breeze. Then, slowly, she went back to bed and fell asleep with the picture of them behind her eyes.

The next morning, Ana woke early and ran to the window. The flowers were closed tight, plain green buds again. She frowned. "You were just open," she said. "I saw you."

At breakfast, she told her brother, Leo, about the glowing flowers. He looked up from his cereal. "Flowers don't glow," he said. "That's impossible."

"They did! They were all white and round like little moons," Ana said.

Leo shrugged. "You were probably dreaming."

Ana tried to show her mother after school, but the buds stayed shut. "They're just buds, sweetheart," her mother said, touching Ana's hair. "Maybe they'll open later."

Ana wanted to explain that they only opened at midnight, but she knew how it would sound. She went to bed that night, determined to stay awake. But she fell asleep before midnight and missed it entirely.

All week, Ana was busy. She had a spelling test, a birthday party, and a school project. On Thursday, she remembered the window box with a jolt. She had not watered it since Monday.

She ran next door after dinner. The soil was bone dry and cracked. The leaves drooped, sad and gray. "Oh no," Ana whispered. "I'm so sorry."

She filled the watering can, but the water just ran off the hard soil. She needed help. Across the street, Mr. Okafor was sitting on his porch, reading. Ana crossed the street quickly.

"Mr. Okafor?" she said. "Can you help me? I'm taking care of Mrs. Alvarez's window box and I forgot to water it. The soil is all dry and the plants look sad."

Mr. Okafor looked up and smiled. "Ah, the moonflowers," he said. "I know them well. Let's see what we can do."

***

Mr. Okafor brought a small trowel and a bucket of water. He showed Ana how to loosen the soil gently. "You have to break up the crust," he said, "so the water can sink in."

Ana copied him, careful not to hurt the roots. "Will they be okay?" she asked.

"Moonflowers are tough," Mr. Okafor said. "They just need a good soak and a little patience. But you have to remember them every day."

Ana nodded. "I will. I promise."

Together, they watered the box until the soil was soft and dark. Mr. Okafor wiped his hands on a rag. "You know," he said, "these flowers only open at night. That's why they're called moonflowers. They wait for the moon."

Ana's eyes widened. "So I wasn't dreaming!"

Mr. Okafor chuckled. "No, you weren't. But not everyone knows to look at midnight. You have to be patient and keep watch."

Ana looked at the plants. "I'll keep watching," she said. "And I won't forget to water them."

"Good," Mr. Okafor said. "Come by tomorrow and we'll check on them together."

Ana walked home feeling lighter. The moon was rising over the street, and she thought she saw the tiniest hint of white on the buds. But she didn't need to prove it to anyone. She knew what she had seen, and she knew how to care for it now. Tomorrow, she would water them again.

The next afternoon, Ana knocked on Mr. Okafor's door with her watering can. He came out wearing a straw hat and carrying a small trowel. "Ready for your first lesson?" he asked.

"I think so," Ana said. "But the leaves still look droopy."

"They will for a few days," Mr. Okafor said. "Moonflowers are tough. They just need consistency." He led her to the window box and gently lifted a vine. "See how the stem is still green? That means it's alive. We'll water it every evening, just before sunset. That's when they like it best."

Ana nodded and poured water slowly, watching it soak into the soil. "How long until they open again?"

"A week, maybe two," Mr. Okafor said. "You have to be patient. But I'll help you keep watch."

Every evening that week, Ana watered the box. Mr. Okafor checked the soil with his thumb and told her stories about his own garden. "My grandmother grew moonflowers in her yard," he said. "She said they were for night owls and dreamers."

Ana laughed. "I guess I'm a night owl now."

By Friday, new buds had formed, small and pale. "They're coming," Mr. Okafor said. "Just a little longer." Ana smiled. She could wait.

***

On Saturday morning, a taxi pulled up outside Mrs. Alvarez's house. Ana ran over as Mrs. Alvarez stepped out, her suitcase bumping on the pavement. "Ana!" Mrs. Alvarez said. "How is my window box?"

"Come see," Ana said, taking her hand. The moonflower vines had climbed the trellis, and dozens of white blooms were closed tight in the morning sun. "They open at night," Ana explained. "Mr. Okafor taught me. They're moonflowers."

Mrs. Alvarez smiled. "I know. That's why I asked you. I thought you might enjoy the surprise."

Ana's mouth fell open. "You knew?"

"I hoped," Mrs. Alvarez said. "And it looks like you did a wonderful job."

Just then, Mr. Okafor walked over with a plate of cookies. "Welcome back," he said. "Your flowers are in good hands."

Mrs. Alvarez looked at Ana. "You know, I think you've met more neighbors this week than I have all year."

Ana glanced down the street. There was Mr. Okafor, and Mrs. Chen from next door, and the twins who lived across the way. She knew them all now. "I guess I have," she said.

That evening, Ana watered the box one last time. The first flower was beginning to open, a pale star in the dusk. She didn't need to prove anything to anyone. She just watched, and waited, and felt the whole street around her, quiet and bright.
