The attic smelled of hot dust and old paper. Ivo had been sneezing since the third box. His father worked the far end of the space, where the roof sloped down and the light came thin through a single grimy window.

"Hand me the tape," his dad said, not turning around. "This one's coming apart at the seam."

Ivo picked his way across the floorboards, between stacks of his grandmother's plates and a rocking horse missing its head. His father had said the clear-out would take one Saturday. It had taken three, and they were barely into the corner where the boxes had no labels at all.

"What's in these?" Ivo crouched by a tower of them.

"No idea. Your mother packed them when we moved. Before." His father said the word the way he always did, flat and quick, like stepping over a puddle.

Ivo opened the top box. Inside were candle stubs, a broken clock, three mismatched gloves, and something heavy wrapped in newspaper so old it had gone yellow at the folds. He peeled the paper back.

A lamp. Brass, or what had been brass, gone green and brown with tarnish. It was shaped like a squat teapot with a long spout, and it had a lid, and it had no handle at all. He turned it over. The bottom was scratched with a pattern that might have been letters, or might have been someone's idle knife.

"Dad. Look at this."

His father glanced over, then came and crouched beside him. Something crossed his face, too fast to name, and then he shook his head. "That's junk. Your mother got it at a car boot sale, I think. She liked rubbish like that."

"It's not junk. It's got writing on it."

"Then it's engraved junk." His father stood, dusting his knees. "Bin bag's by the door. I'll finish the boxes."

Ivo did not put it in the bin bag. He turned it in his hands until the light from the window caught the lid. Tucked under the lid, folded small and stiff as a beetle's wing, was a scrap of paper. He worked it loose with his thumbnail and unfolded it.

The handwriting was cramped and slanted, and it was not his mother's.

Write a question, it said. I will answer with light.

Ivo read it twice. Then he looked at the lamp. The lamp looked like nothing at all: a tarnished old thing with no handle and a lid that did not quite sit flush.

"Ivo?" His father's voice came from behind a wall of boxes. "You still there?"

"Still here," Ivo said, and he slipped the lamp into his jacket, where it sat against his ribs, cold and heavier than it had any right to be.

***

That night he sat on his bed with the door shut and the lamp on his desk. His room was the smallest in the house, but it had a window that faced the street, and the streetlamp outside threw a bar of orange across the floor. The lamp sat in the dark part, where the light did not reach.

He found a pencil and tore a page from the back of his maths book. For a long moment he did not write anything. The paper waited.

What did he want to ask? That was the trouble. He could ask anything. He could ask whether the note was a joke, whether the lamp was a joke, whether he was sitting here at eleven at night being an idiot for no reason.

He wrote: Are you real?

Nothing happened. The lamp sat there, green and dull, and the streetlamp hummed beyond the glass, and Ivo felt his face go hot with embarrassment. He was about to crumple the page when the lamp flickered.

Not a flame. There was no flame. The brass itself glowed for a single second, a weak amber pulse that ran from the spout to the lid and died. Then the lamp was dark and ordinary again.

One flicker. He counted it. One.

He sat very still. Then he wrote another line beneath the first: If I ask you something true, will you answer it truly?

The lamp pulsed once.

Ivo's heart was going hard now, and he had to press the pencil down to stop his hand shaking. "One for yes," he whispered. "Two for no." The lamp did not flicker. He understood it was waiting, and that waiting was its own kind of answer.

He thought of a question that had no answer he could check. Then he thought of one that did. He wrote, small and careful: Is my name Ivo?

One flicker.

He wrote: Am I thirteen?

One flicker.

He wrote: Am I sitting in my room right now?

Two flickers. He blinked, and then he almost laughed, because of course, of course the lamp was precise. He was sitting on the edge of his bed with the page on the desk, not in his room in the way he had meant it. He moved to the chair, and wrote it again, and got one flicker.

The laugh went out of him. This was not a joke. The lamp knew things. The lamp was answering, and the answers were true, and there was a question he had not let himself write since he was eight years old.

Outside, a car went past and the bar of orange light slid across the wall. Ivo's hand hovered over the page. His pencil tip touched the paper, and he did not write, and the lamp waited in the dark, patient as a held breath.

***

Ivo wrote the question at the bottom of the page, and his pencil pressed hard enough to leave a groove in the paper.

Where did my mother go?

The lamp flickered. Once, twice, three times, and then it stopped, and the attic stairs creaked as his father came up with a box in his arms.

"Still up here?" his father said. "I thought you'd have gone down for supper."

Ivo slid the page under his leg. "I'm sorting. Like you said."

His father set the box down and looked at him a moment too long. Then he nodded and went back down. The lamp did not flicker again while the footsteps faded. Ivo understood, somehow, that it was waiting to be alone with him. When the house went quiet, it answered. Three slow pulses, then a pause, then one more. Four. He counted. He did not know what four meant, and there was no paper left to write on that did not already have his question on it.

He wrote: Four what?

One flicker. Then two. Ten. He counted eight, and the lamp kept going, and he lost the count somewhere past twelve and sat back with his heart going hard. It was not a number. It was a direction, or a distance, or a word spelled out in light, and he could not read it.

He tried again the next night. He wrote: Is she alive? One flicker. He sat with that for a long time, and then he wrote: Does she think about me? One flicker, and the room felt warmer than it had any right to.

The answers cost him nothing he could feel. Not at first. He wrote questions until his wrist ached, and each time the lamp gave him a flicker, and each time he felt a small bright satisfaction, like finding a coin in a coat pocket.

Then, on the third night, his father said, "You haven't mentioned your mother in a while."

Ivo opened his mouth to say she had a coat, a good winter coat. He could not remember the color of it. Blue? It had been blue. He was almost certain it had been blue. But the certainty was a smooth stone with no marking on it, and when he tried to picture her standing in the doorway in it, the doorway was empty.

"She had a coat," he said, slowly.

His father looked at him. "She did. The green one."

"Green," Ivo said, and the word did not fit the shape in his head at all.

***

He kept the lamp under his bed after that, wrapped in a towel, and he told himself he would ask it only important things. But the questions came anyway, small ones, hungry ones. What was her voice like? Two flickers, no, and he frowned, because that could not be right. Then he understood: the lamp could not tell him a voice. It could only answer yes or no, and every yes he got had to be paid for out of the same purse. He wrote: Did she sing? One flicker. He tried to hear it and heard only the fridge humming downstairs.

At supper his father said, "You used to tell me the story about the fair, the one with the dropped ice cream. You remember that one."

"I remember," Ivo said, and he did, but the ice cream was gone from it now. The fair was a gray blur, and his mother was a shape in a doorway with no coat on at all.

His father set down his fork. "Ivo. Look at me."

Ivo looked.

"What's going on?" his father asked. "You've been up in that attic every night. You don't talk about her. You don't talk about anything."

"I'm fine," Ivo said.

"You're not fine. You're sitting there like you're waiting for bad news." His father's voice was quiet and careful, the way it got when he was holding something back. "I know it's hard. It's hard for me too. But you can't just stop."

Ivo wanted to tell him. The words were right there, and the lamp was right there under the floorboards of his bed, and if he said it out loud it would sound like a boy making excuses for forgetting his own mother. So he said, "I'm not hungry," and carried his plate to the sink, and his father did not follow him.

Upstairs, he unwrapped the lamp and set it on the desk. It was warm in a way the towel could not explain. He wrote: What was her name?

His hand shook. He crossed the question out before the lamp could answer, because he was afraid, suddenly and completely, that one flicker would come back and he would not know which name it meant. He would have traded the last of her for a yes.

That night the lamp would not let him sleep.

It did not flicker or knock. It simply sat on the desk, warm as a held hand, and every time Ivo closed his eyes he could feel the question he had not asked pressing against the inside of his skull. What was her name. He knew it. He was almost sure he knew it. He said it to himself in the dark, tried it out, and it fit wrong, like a shoe from another house.

Around two in the morning he got up and lit the stub of a candle and sat down with the lamp and a pencil.

He wrote: How many questions do I have left?

The lamp flickered once. Then, after a long breath of nothing, it flickered again, twice more, and stopped. Three. Then, while he watched, the brass dimmed. A small brass nub he had never noticed on the base turned itself a quarter of the way around, like a dial, and he understood without being told that this was the last of it.

Three questions, and the lamp was asking him to spend them.

He wrote: What happens when they run out?

One flicker. The lamp went dark for a moment, darker than dark, and then it came back, and the brass on the side nearest him was smoother than before, polished by nothing.

He understood that too. It would go quiet. It would become a lamp again, an object, something his father could sell or throw away or keep in the attic for another forty years. And whatever he had not asked, he would never know.

Ivo put the pencil down and pressed his palms against his eyes.

***

The question he wanted was not the name. He knew that now. The name was a door, and behind the door was the thing he actually wanted: where she went, and whether she was all right, and whether she had chosen to go, and whether she had thought of him at the last. He had asked where she went, and the lamp had answered, and the answer had cost him the color of her coat. If he asked the big thing, the whole thing, he did not know what it would take. Maybe the shape of her face. Maybe the sound of her voice. Maybe the last of her, entirely, traded for a flicker of light that would tell him something he could not hold.

He picked up the pencil and wrote: If I ask, will I remember her after?

One flicker. Yes.

He waited. The second flicker did not come. He waited until the candle guttered and his hand ached and the floorboards creaked with the cold, and still the lamp only glowed, steady, patient, one answer given and no more.

Yes, he would remember her. It did not say how much.

Ivo looked at the scrap of paper, at the words on it, and he thought about the way his father had said it's hard for me too. He thought about the way he had carried his plate to the sink and not looked back. He thought about the coat. He could not remember the coat. He had traded that, and he had not even known he was spending it.

He folded the paper in half, and in half again, and set it under the lamp. Then he blew out the candle and lay down in the dark, and the lamp sat warm on the desk, and he did not spend the last two questions.

***

In the morning he did not touch the lamp. He went down to breakfast and ate, and his father looked at him across the table with that careful look, and Ivo said, "I want to show you something."

They went up to the attic together. His father had to duck under the beams, and he stood in the middle of the boxes with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders up around his ears, the way he always did in that room.

Ivo brought the lamp out from under the bed and set it on an upturned crate. It was just brass. It was dull and dented and it did not glow.

"I found it up here," Ivo said. "The day we cleared the boxes. There was a note under it. It said you could write a question and it would answer with light."

His father did not laugh. That was the thing Ivo had been most afraid of, and his father did not laugh.

"And did it?" his father asked.

"Yes." Ivo's voice came out smaller than he wanted. "It answered true. Every time. But it took things. Memories. I asked about her, and afterwards I couldn't remember her coat. I couldn't remember the color. And I kept asking, and I kept losing, and I don't know what's left."

His father was quiet for a long moment. Then he crossed the attic and sat down on a box of books, and he pulled Ivo down beside him.

"Her coat was red," his father said. "Dark red, with a tear at the left cuff she never mended. She wore it to the market and came home with the pockets full of apples."

Ivo held very still.

"She used to sing while she washed the dishes," his father said. "Badly. Off the tune. It drove me up the wall." He laughed, and it broke in the middle. "And she called you Ivo because she said it sounded like a name that could stand up in a wind."

Ivo's eyes were hot. "I couldn't remember the coat," he said. "I'm sorry."

"You don't have to remember alone," his father said. "That's what I'm for."

Ivo took the cloth from his pocket and draped it over the brass lamp. The light under it went out, and the attic was only an attic, dusty and cold, with the sun coming through the small window and the two of them sitting together on a box of books.

"Tell me another one," Ivo said.

And his father did.
