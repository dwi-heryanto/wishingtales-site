Ada found the window on the first cold morning of October. The old farmhouse had a hundred windows, her mother liked to say, and most of them rattled. This one, at the end of the upstairs hall, rattled more than any of them.

It was open a hand's width, and the wind was pushing through it like it had somewhere to be. Ada reached up to pull it shut and stopped. There was a cat on the sill.

It was gray, the color of a rainy afternoon, with one white paw and a torn ear. It looked at her the way a landlord looks at a late visitor.

"Shoo," Ada said. "Go on. Out."

The cat did not shoo. It yawned, showing a pink curl of tongue, and settled its chin on its white paw.

Ada clapped her hands. The cat blinked slowly, once, as if she had told it a boring story. Then it stood, stretched so long its front legs shook, and poured itself back out through the gap.

"Good," Ada said, and shut the window with a bang.

At breakfast she told her mother about it. Her mother was buttering toast and not quite listening.

"A gray cat?" she said. "There's been a gray cat around the barn since the summer. Nobody's claimed it."

"It was on the windowsill. Inside."

"Then you shooed it, I hope."

Ada nodded and took her toast. She felt a little proud of the shooing, though she could not have said why. The cat had not seemed shooed at all.

***

The next morning the window was open again. Ada knew she had shut it. She had heard the latch click.

The gray cat was curled on the hall rug in a tidy circle, its tail wrapped over its nose. It opened one eye when Ada's boots came down the floorboards.

"You," she said.

The cat made a sound like a question. Ada picked it up, which it allowed, and carried it to the window. It hung in her arms like a sack of warm flour. Out it went, and down it dropped, and away it trotted toward the barn without a backward glance.

For one whole day the window stayed shut. Ada checked it twice. She checked it a third time before bed, and the latch was cold and firm under her thumb.

On Thursday the cat was back. It had brought a dead leaf in with it and left the leaf on the hall rug, which felt like a comment.

Ada went down to the shed and came back with a board, a hammer, and a fistful of nails. She nailed the board across the lower half of the window. Then she stacked her heaviest books on the sill: an atlas, a dictionary, and three volumes of an encyclopedia that nobody ever read.

"There," she said. "Try that."

The cat sat on the floor and washed its torn ear. It did not look worried.

That night Ada woke to a soft thump. She lay still and listened. Another thump, then a scrape, then the click of her books sliding against each other.

In the morning the window was open by a hand's width, the board was still nailed, and the cat was asleep in her laundry basket. The dictionary lay face down on the floor, which seemed rude.

"How?" Ada asked it.

The cat stretched one white paw and did not answer.

All week the sky stayed low and grey, like a lid on a pot. On Friday the wind got up, and the rain came sideways at the farmhouse windows. Ada sat at the kitchen table with her homework and listened to the roof creak.

Then came a sound under the rain. A thin, high meow.

Ada went to the window. The yard was all silver and shaking grass. By the shed door, a small shape crouched low, fur flat with wet, and meowed again.

"You're out there," Ada said. "You got yourself out, and now you're out."

The cat looked up at her. Rain ran off its torn ear in a little stream.

Ada rattled the window. It was nailed and stacked and jammed, and the rain had swollen the wood. The cat cried louder.

"Hold on," she said. "Hold on, hold on."

She ran for the hammer. The nails shrieked coming out. The board fell with a clatter. The books slid to the floor, the atlas first, and she didn't care. The window stuck, then gave, and a cold gust pushed in like a hand against her chest.

"Come on!" she shouted into the storm. "Get in here!"

The cat climbed the shed wall in two jumps, crossed the gap of air, and landed on the sill. Ada seized it and pulled it inside. It was shuddering and light as a wet mitten.

***

Ada sat on the kitchen floor with the cat pressed against her jumper, and the storm banged at the house all around them. The cat's heart went like a bird's. She could feel it through the wet fur.

"You're a stupid cat," she whispered. "You're a stupid, stupid cat."

Her mother came down in her dressing gown, took one look, and went straight for a towel.

"Well," Mum said, rubbing the cat dry. "There's a rat that got in the window, then."

"I blocked it," Ada said. "I nailed a board. I stacked the whole encyclopedia. It found a way every single time."

Mum held up the towel. A wet leaf dropped out of it, and then another.

"Ada," she said. "Look at the sill."

Ada turned. The wind was pushing through the window, and the loose frame shifted. Only a crack, but enough. She put her palm flat against the opening.

"It's not just a hole in the wall," she said slowly. "It's the way the house breathes."

Mum sat down beside her on the tiles.

"Then I suppose," Mum said, "the house can breathe around a cat."

The cat stopped shivering. It walked out from under the towel, sat in the exact middle of the kitchen floor, and washed one paw, as if nothing had happened at all.

Ada laughed, which surprised her. "Fine," she said. "You can stay tonight."

Outside, the storm went on and on. Inside, the kettle began to sing.

Morning came in grey and quiet. The storm had left the yard littered with leaves and a broken branch by the gate. Ada woke to find the cat asleep on the kitchen chair, curled into a comma of fur.

Mum was already at the window, prying the last board loose with a screwdriver. "Hand me the small nails," she said. "And the hammer. We are going to do this properly."

Ada fetched them from the toolbox under the stairs. The window frame was loose, and the glass rattled when the wind nudged it. Mum held the frame steady while Ada tapped in a nail. It took three tries to get it straight.

"Not bad," Mum said. "Now the bottom corner. A little lower."

They worked side by side, and the cat watched from the chair with its head on one paw. When the frame was tight, Mum stood back and wiped her hands on her jeans.

"There," she said. "It won't whistle anymore. But a cat still needs a way in and out that isn't a broken pane."

Ada looked at the window, then at the cat. "We could cut a little door," she said. "Down at the bottom. In the wood, not the glass."

Mum raised her eyebrows. "You want to cut a hole in my house?"

"A small one," Ada said. "Just cat-sized. So it doesn't have to squeeze through cracks."

Mum thought about it. Then she fetched the saw. Ada held the wood while Mum cut, and the cat came over to sniff the sawdust. The opening was low and square, with a flap of old rubber mat hung over it.

"Try it," Mum said.

The cat walked to the flap, pushed it with its nose, and slipped through. A moment later it was back, sitting in the kitchen as if it had never left.

***

That afternoon, Ada sat on the back step with a bowl of milk. The cat came and drank, then washed its face in the sun. The yard smelled of wet earth and cut grass.

"You need a name," Ada said.

The cat did not answer. It had a smudge of grey on its chin and one white foot. Ada thought of the storm, and the way the cat had sat in the middle of the kitchen floor. Not frightened. Just certain.

"Barnaby," she said. "You look like a Barnaby."

Mum came out with two cups of tea and sat beside her. "Barnaby," she repeated. "That's a good, solid name."

"It's a name that knows its way around," Ada said.

Barnaby finished the milk and walked to the new cat flap. He pushed through it, then came back in, then out again, as if testing a new toy. Each time the flap swung shut behind him with a soft thump.

"He likes it," Mum said.

Ada nodded. The farmhouse felt different now. Not bigger, exactly. Fuller. As if the house had been holding its breath and had finally let it out.

That evening, Ada lay on the kitchen floor with her chin on her hands. Barnaby curled against her side, warm and purring. The window was fixed, the storm was gone, and the cat door tapped gently in the breeze.

"You can stay," Ada whispered. "For good."

Barnaby blinked slowly. Then he closed his eyes, and the kitchen was quiet, and the house breathed easy around them both.
