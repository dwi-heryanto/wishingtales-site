The ferry had one more crossing in it, and Mara meant to make it count. She stood at the stern with the pole across her shoulders, pushing the flat boat out from the landing where the lantern hung on its crooked nail. Behind her the village lights were going out one by one, windows darkening like eyes closing. Ahead, the river lay flat and black and patient, holding the moon in pieces.

Her father had taught her the crossing in the spring, when the water was high. He had stood where she stood now and said, "Count the strokes. Always count, and the river never gets ahead of you." So she counted. One, two, three, the pole biting mud and pushing free.

Halfway out, the river began to talk.

It did that sometimes, at night, when the wind had gone down and the water had nothing to do but murmur. Mara had heard it all her life, a soft babble under the hull like a room full of people talking behind a closed door. Her father said it was only current and stones. She had never believed him, and she did not believe him now.

"Mara," the river said.

She stopped counting. The pole hung in her hands, dripping.

"Mara," it said again, and then another name, one she did not know. It was a long name, three syllables, with a soft ending, like the name of someone who had lived a hundred years ago and been forgotten. The water repeated it politely, the way a stranger repeats a name to be sure of it, and then let it sink back under the current.

"Who is that?" Mara said. Her voice sounded thin over the water.

The river did not answer. It only went on murmuring, and now and then, very faint, she heard the long name again, turning over among the stones.

A passenger was waiting at the far landing. Old Tamsin, who crossed every week to visit her sister's grave, sat on the bench with a basket of eggs on her knees. "You're late," Tamsin said, but she said it kindly.

"The river was talking," Mara said.

Tamsin climbed aboard and settled herself. "It does that. Your father never listened." She looked at Mara sideways. "Or he listened, and never said. Same thing, from the outside."

The crossing back took longer than it should have. Mara counted her strokes, and the river whispered its unknown name, and the moon slid down the sky like a coin into a pocket.

At the landing, her father was waiting with his coat over his arm. He was a big man, stooped now at the shoulders, and his hands were river-rough and always cold. "You took the last one," he said. "I told you I'd take the last one."

"You were sleeping," Mara said. "Sit down. I'll tie her up."

He did not sit down. He stood looking at the water for a long moment, and then he said, "Did she say anything to you?"

"The river?" Mara laughed. "She said a name. I don't know whose."

Her father nodded slowly, as if he had expected it. "Go home," he said. "I'll take the dawn crossing. Go home and sleep."

***

Mara woke at first light because the bell was ringing wrong. It was the landing bell, the small cracked one her father rang to call passengers, and it was ringing in a hurry, a clatter, as if someone were shaking it instead of pulling the rope.

She was out of the house before she had her boots on properly, running down the lane with her coat half fastened. The mist lay on the river like a white blanket, and the landing was full of people standing very still.

The boat was back in its place. Her father was not in it.

Mara went down the steps two at a time and stopped at the water's edge. The boat sat low and steady, water sloshing in the bottom, an inch or so of it, the color of weak tea. His coat was folded on the passenger seat, neat as a parcel, and on top of the coat lay the pole.

"He took the dawn crossing," Mara said. "He told me he'd take the dawn crossing."

Nobody answered her. Old Tamsin was there, and the baker, and the two men who mended nets. They all looked at the boat and then at each other, and none of them looked at Mara.

"Where is he?" Mara said.

The baker cleared his throat. "The boat came back empty about an hour ago. Floating free, bumping the steps. We rang the bell because we thought, well. We thought you'd want to know."

Mara stepped into the boat. The water in the bottom soaked through her boots, cold as a hand closing around her ankle. She picked up the coat. It was dry, and it still smelled of him, of tar and tobacco and the river. She held it against her chest and could not make herself put it down.

"He wouldn't leave the coat," she said. "He never left the coat. He was always cold."

"Mara," Tamsin said gently.

"He's a strong swimmer. He taught me to swim."

"Mara."

She turned around. The whole village was on the landing now, and the mist was lifting, and the river ran past bright and innocent as if it had never said a word in its life. Somewhere out in the middle of the water, a bird called once and was quiet.

"I'll go and look for him," Mara said.

She put the coat on the seat, folded exactly as she had found it, and reached for the pole.

The elders met her in the boathouse, where the light came grey through the slats and the tar smelled of her father. There were four of them: Tamsin, who kept the shop; old Brannoc, who had been ferryman before her father and had the crooked hands to prove it; the two sisters who read the weather in their knees. They did not sit down. Mara stood with her back to the door and her father's pole across her shoulders like a yoke.

"You went out in the dark," Brannoc said. "That was foolish."

"I went out to find him."

"There's nothing to find." He said it without cruelty, which was worse. "The river takes a ferryman every generation, girl. It has always been so. My grandfather went. I gave my hands to the pole and it let me keep them, but it took my brother instead, and I have never once pretended to know why."

"My father was careful."

"Your father was the best of us," Tamsin said. "That is why it wanted him."

The two sisters nodded together, like reeds in one wind. One of them said, "Sell the boat. Someone from the far bank will buy it and put a new name on the stern, and the river will forget whose it was."

"And then?"

"And then you live," Brannoc said. "You are seventeen. You have a whole life that the water has not asked for yet. Do not go handing it over because you loved him."

Mara looked at the boat through the open door. It sat in the shallows with the pole lying along its length, and the river ran past it, bright and ordinary, as if it were innocent of everything. "If I sell it," she said, "who rows the dead across?"

Nobody answered that. The sisters looked at their knees. Brannoc looked at his hands, at the crooked fingers that had held the pole for forty years. "The river finds someone," he said at last. "It always does. That is the trouble with it."

She walked out and left them standing in the tar-smell, and behind her Tamsin called her name once, and she did not turn around.

***

At dusk she took the boat out alone. The mist came down like a lid. She had brought the pole and her father's coat and a lamp with a stub of candle in it, and she rowed until the landing was gone and the far shore had not yet arrived, and then she stopped and let the current hold her.

"You said a name," she said to the water. "I heard you. Say it again."

The river said nothing. It only breathed, in and out, against the boards.

Then a light went down. Not a reflection: a lantern, small and yellow, sinking away below the black surface as if the water were only air with a lid on it. She watched it fall until it was a pinprick, and then she shipped the pole and stood up in the rocking boat and looked at it, and the whole of her was cold except her hands, which were hot.

"All right," she said, and she filled her lungs and went over the side.

The cold took her like a fist. Down she went through the dark, and the lantern grew, and the water did not close over her but opened, and she was standing on a road of wet stones with her hair floating around her face and her boots on the ground.

A table was set in the middle of the drowned fields. It was long, and there were many at it, and the lantern hung above it, and every chair was filled except one at the head. The drowned were quiet. They had river weed in their hair and their eyes were calm as coins, and they turned them all on her at once.

Her father sat at the far end. He was wearing no coat. He looked up at her and his mouth moved, and no sound came out, and he shook his head slowly, slowly, the way he shook it when she was small and had done something reckless near the water.

An old woman at the head of the table lifted her hand. She was older than the water, older than the stones, and her voice came to Mara through the dark as clearly as if she had spoken into her ear.

"Sit down, ferryman's daughter," she said. "We have kept your place."

Mara did not sit down. The chair at the head of the table waited, and the water waited, and the drowned waited with their calm coin eyes, and she stood with her boots sinking a finger's width into the soft ground and said, "Where is my father's coat?"

The old woman smiled, and her teeth were small and white as river stones. "Bold," she said. "Good. The river likes a bold ferryman. It has less work to do with them."

"I asked about the coat."

"The coat is in the boat, where he left it. He left it because he thought the river wanted him. They all think that. Every generation, one of your family hears the water say a name and rows out, and never rows back, and the village says the river takes a ferryman and shakes its head and sells the boat." She folded her hands. "The river does not take. The river keeps what is given. There is a difference, and nobody has taught it to you."

Mara's father moved at the far end of the table. He tried to rise and could not, and his hands pressed flat on the wood, and his face was terrible with the effort of a shout that would not come.

"What does it want?" Mara said.

The old woman leaned forward, and the lantern above her swung though there was no wind at all. "A promise," she said. "Someone rows the dead across and takes what they are given and asks for nothing twice. That is all the river has ever wanted. Your father took a coin from a woman who had nothing else, and then he asked her for another, because he was tired, and the water heard him. Not greed. Tiredness. The river is not cruel, child. It is exact."

"Then give him back."

"Take him," said the old woman. "The table is set for one more, and I have told you why, and it is your choice which of you fills it. Row him home and take up the pole, and mind you never ask twice." She lifted her hand again, and the drowned turned their faces to the dark water, and the lantern went out.

Mara stood alone in the black fields with her heart going like a fist on a door. Then she turned and climbed, and the water let her go.

***

Her father was lying on the wet stones at the water's edge, and his chest was not moving, and his face was grey as the underside of a leaf. Mara got her arms under his shoulders and dragged him into the boat. He was heavier than she expected, the way the dead are heavier, and she cried while she did it, and she did not stop to be ashamed of the crying.

She laid him in the bottom of the boat and put his coat over him, and then she took the oars.

The crossing was long. The river ran strange and slow beneath her, and once or twice she felt something brush the hull, gentle as a hand, and she did not look over the side. She rowed the way he had taught her, long and even, and she counted her strokes under her breath the way he had taught her, and the far shore came up out of the dark by inches.

When the keel touched the shingle, her father drew a breath. It was a thin, ragged sound, and then another, and his eyes opened and found her. "Mara," he said. His voice was a ruin. "You went down."

"I went down."

"You should not have. The river will want its fee, and I have nothing left to pay it, and you are not going to give it what it asks."

"It asked already," Mara said. "It asked for a promise. I gave it one."

He tried to sit up and could not, and his hand found the gunwale and held it. "No," he said. "No. You take the boat and you sell it and you go inland where there is no water, and you forget the whole of this."

"I can't," she said. "The dead are waiting, and the table is set, and someone has to row them. You did it your whole life and you were tired, and one tired coin was all it took. I would rather be careful than tired."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed, which turned into a cough, which turned into something that was nearly weeping, and he said, "Your mother would have said the same thing, and she would have said it louder."

Mara stood up in the boat and took the long pole from its cleat, and set it against the stones, and pushed off. The river moved under her, and it settled, the way a dog settles, and the whispering stopped. It did not say her name. It did not need to. She knew the water now, and the water knew her, and the crossing was hers to make.

By the time the sun came up over the reeds, the whole village was on the bank, and the elders were there with their mouths open, and her father was sitting up in the bow with his coat around his shoulders.

Mara brought the boat in and set the pole down and said, to no one in particular, "Fares are one coin. The second time you ask, I will not take it. That is the rule, and the rule is mine now."

Nobody argued. The river ran quiet behind her, and the day was ordinary, and she was the ferryman.
