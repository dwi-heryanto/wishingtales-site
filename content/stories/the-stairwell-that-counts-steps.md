Toby counted the steps. It was a habit, like the way he tapped each railing post twice before letting go. The building had seven floors and a hundred and twelve steps from the lobby to his family's door on the seventh, and he had counted them so many times that the number lived in his bones. At night, though, it was a hundred and thirteen.

He first noticed it on a Tuesday, coming home late from his friend's place. The stairwell was dim, the bulbs buzzing like trapped flies. He climbed, his sneakers soft on the concrete, and counted under his breath. "A hundred and ten, a hundred and eleven, a hundred and twelve..." His foot landed on nothing, then something, and he stumbled. He caught the railing and stood still, heart thumping. The landing outside his door was there, but he had taken one extra step to reach it. He looked back. The stairs behind him looked ordinary, a grey ribbon folding down into shadow. He counted them again on the way up the next morning, in daylight, and got a hundred and twelve. At night, it was a hundred and thirteen. He told himself he had miscounted. But the number stuck in his head, a splinter he couldn't pull out.

His mother worked double shifts, so Toby often walked his little sister, Ada, home from the sitter's. Ada was six and believed in monsters under the bed but not in the stairwell. "It's just stairs," she said, when he mentioned the extra one. "Stairs don't grow." He wanted to believe her. But every night, the count was the same. A hundred and twelve in the day, a hundred and thirteen after dark. He started taking the elevator, but it was slow and creaky, and Ada liked the stairs. "You're being weird," she said, tugging his hand. He didn't tell her why he hurried her past the third-floor landing.

One night, he brought a coin. A quarter, cold in his pocket. He stood on the third-floor landing, where the extra step appeared, and dropped it. The quarter spun in the air, caught the light, and fell. He waited for the clink. It didn't come. He leaned over the railing, listening. Nothing. The stairwell was silent except for the hum of the bulbs. Then, faint and far below, a voice. Counting.

"A hundred and eleven... a hundred and twelve... a hundred and thirteen..." The voice was a child's, thin and patient, like someone reciting a lesson. Toby's skin prickled. He gripped the railing and stared down into the dark well of the stairs. The voice stopped. Then it started again, from the beginning. "One... two... three..." He backed away, his breath shallow, and ran up to his door. He didn't sleep well that night. The counting went on in his head, a loop he couldn't break.

***

The next evening, Ada raced ahead of him on the stairs. "I'll beat you!" she called, her footsteps light. Toby was tired, his mind on the voice, and he didn't notice she had stopped until he nearly bumped into her. She was standing on the third-floor landing, one foot lifted over the step that shouldn't be there. "Ada, no!" He grabbed her arm and pulled her back. She stumbled against him, her face surprised. "What? I was just going to see..." The step was there, a normal grey slab, but something about it made his stomach turn. "Don't," he said, his voice shaking. "Please. Just don't." She stared at him, then nodded. They took the elevator the rest of the way. That night, Toby lay awake, listening. The counting was still there, soft as a drip. He knew he had to do something.

The power cut came three nights later. Toby was in the kitchen, and the fridge died mid-hum. The whole flat went dark, and from the hallway came Ada's voice, high and thrilled. "Blackout! Toby, it's a blackout!"

He found her by the front door, already in her coat. "Mum's still at work," she said. "We should go down and wait in the lobby. It's boring up here."

Toby grabbed a torch from the drawer. "Fine. But we take the elevator."

"It's a blackout, dummy. Elevator's dead." Ada was already through the door, her feet finding the stairs by memory. "Race you to the bottom!"

He followed fast, the torch beam bouncing. On the second-floor landing he caught up, and that was when he saw her foot lift over the step that shouldn't be there. The extra step. In the dark it looked like every other, but Toby knew. "Ada, stop!" He lunged and caught her sleeve, hauling her backward. She stumbled into him and dropped her torch. The light spun, and for a second the beam caught the step from below. It wasn't grey now. It was black, black as a hole in the floor, and the counting came up from it, clear and close. "Forty-one... forty-two..."

Ada grabbed his arm. "Toby, what is that?"

"Don't look. Don't listen." He pulled her up the stairs, away from the landing, and they stood together in the dark, breathing hard. The counting faded. "We'll wait on our floor," he said. "By the door. With the torch on."

She didn't argue. They sat on the floor outside their flat, the torch between them, until the lights came back and the fridge started humming again. Ada fell asleep against his shoulder. Toby stayed awake, watching the stairwell door. He had to know what was down there.

***

The library had old newspapers on microfilm, and the librarian, Mrs. Patel, knew Toby by name. "Building history?" she said. "We've got the local paper from way back. What are you after?"

"My building. The Kestrel. Anything weird."

She raised an eyebrow but showed him the machine. Toby scrolled through grainy pages until he found it. A headline from 1971: BOY, 9, MISSING IN STAIRWELL. The article was short. A boy named Simon Hale had vanished from the Kestrel's stairwell one night. Police searched every floor. No trace. The last person to see him said he was counting steps, a game he played to feel safe in the dark. Then he was gone.

Toby read it twice. His hands were cold. He printed the article and took it home.

That night he spread it on the kitchen table. Ada read over his shoulder. "Simon Hale," she whispered. "He was nine. When I'm nine, that'll be me."

"He counted steps," Toby said. "Every night. And then one night there was one more step than there should be, and he stepped onto it."

Ada was quiet for a moment. "You think he's still there."

"I think he's been counting ever since. Waiting for someone to count with him."

Ada looked at the stairwell door. "Then maybe you should."

Toby waited until the building had gone quiet. At half past eleven he stood at the stairwell door in his socks, listening. The pipes ticked. A television murmured two floors down. Then he pulled the door open and stepped through.

The stairwell was darker at night, lit only by one bulb on a timer that would click off in four minutes. He went down slowly, touching the rail. Seventh floor. Six. Five. His lips moved with the count, the way they had since he was small.

On the landing between the fourth and third floors, he stopped. The bulb above him buzzed. Below, where the next flight should begin, there was a step that wasn't there by day — a pale tread of concrete, slightly too wide, set into the flight like a tooth that didn't belong.

Toby crouched at the top of the flight. His heart was loud. "Simon," he said.

The word went down the stairwell and came back changed. For a long moment nothing answered. Then, small and thin, from somewhere below the extra step, a voice said, "Four."

"Simon Hale," Toby said again. "I know who you are."

The voice stopped counting. "Nobody knows that," it said. It sounded like a boy trying not to cry, and failing in a way that had gone on so long it was ordinary. "Who's there?"

"Toby. I live on the seventh. I count the steps too."

Silence. The bulb ticked and dimmed. Toby saw, at the bottom of the extra step, a shape the dark had made — thin shoulders, a bent head, a hand resting on the tread. "I don't want to be down here," the voice said. "But I can't find the top. I keep counting up and it never ends. It just goes round again."

"Why don't you stop counting?"

"Because then I'm just here," Simon said. "And it's so quiet, Toby. It's so quiet and so long. I count so I'm not alone. If I count, somebody might count with me."

Toby gripped the rail. His mouth was dry. "I'll count with you."

"You'll go away."

"No," Toby said. "I promise."

The bulb clicked off. In the dark, the thin voice began again, and Toby answered it, and the two of them climbed.

***

Toby sat down on the top step of the flight, his back against the wall, and said, "Ready?"

"Ready," said Simon.

"One," Toby said.

"One," the voice agreed, and it was closer now, or maybe Toby only wanted it to be.

They counted the flight together. Sixteen steps by day; seventeen with the pale one. Toby said each number and Simon said it back, and on the seventeenth Simon's voice caught, like a foot finding a stair in the dark.

"Again," Simon said.

So they went up. Toby climbed backwards, keeping his hand on the rail, calling the numbers, and Simon echoed him from below, and with every flight the echo came a little nearer. Fourth floor. Fifth. The numbers blurred with the floors and Toby's legs burned and he kept going.

On the sixth landing he paused, breathing hard. "Simon. Are you there?"

"I'm here." The voice was at his shoulder now, almost a whisper. "I can see the top. I haven't seen the top in so long."

"Then let's finish." Toby turned and climbed the last flight, counting out loud, and Simon counted with him, and their voices made one voice. Seventeen. Seventeen. Seventeen.

On the last landing, the extra step was gone. There was only the ordinary concrete, grey and clean, and the door to the seventh floor, and the dim bulb burning steadily.

"Simon?"

"I found it," Simon said. His voice was fading, and happy, which was the strangest part. "The top. You counted me to the top."

"Are you all right?"

"I'm not counting anymore," Simon said. "It's quiet. But it's a good quiet now."

Then the stairwell was only a stairwell. Toby sat on the landing for a while, listening to nothing, and then he went home and sat on the edge of Ada's bed until she woke up.

"Did you do it?" she asked.

"I did it," Toby said. "He's gone. Properly gone, I think. Not lost anymore."

Ada thought about it. "Will you still count the steps?"

Toby looked at the stairwell door across the hall. "Every night," he said. "But now I count them for both of us."
