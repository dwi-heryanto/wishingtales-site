The key stuck, and June had to shoulder the door. It gave with a groan, swinging into a hallway that smelled of dust and something sweeter, like old apples. Her grandmother's house. She'd never been here before. Now she owned it, apparently, along with whatever else Gran had left behind.

Boxes lined the walls, but the hallway itself was bare except for a tall grandfather clock at the far end. Its face was pale as bone, and its pendulum hung still. June dropped her duffel bag and walked toward it. The clock's hands were wrong — both pointing straight up, at twelve. But it wasn't noon. It was nearly seven in the evening.

A small brass plate was nailed below the dial. Engraved words: For June, when she comes.

She touched the glass. Cold. The pendulum twitched, and she jerked her hand back.

"Hello?" she called. No answer. Of course not. Gran was dead. That was the whole reason she was here.

June found the light switch and flicked it. Nothing. She tried again, then pulled out her phone. The flashlight beam swept the hallway, catching a thin line of dust on the floorboards, leading from the front door to the clock. As if someone had walked that path many times.

She followed it. The clock's base was wider than it should be, and when she knelt, she saw a keyhole. No key. She straightened and noticed the hands had moved. Both now pointed at eleven fifty-nine.

"That's not possible," she whispered.

The minute hand clicked forward. Eleven fifty-eight. Then eleven fifty-seven. Counting down.

June backed away. Her heel caught the edge of a rug, and she stumbled, catching herself on a small side table. A framed photograph toppled. Gran, younger, standing in front of this same clock with a man June didn't recognize. Both were smiling. The clock behind them showed twelve exactly.

She set the frame upright. The ticking filled the hallway now, steady as a heartbeat. June pressed her palms against her ears, but it didn't help.

Then, from outside, a voice: "You shouldn't be in there after dark!"

***

June yanked open the door. A boy stood on the path, maybe her age, with a flashlight of his own and a jacket too big for him.

"Who are you?" she asked.

"Eli. I live down the road." He nodded at the house. "You're the granddaughter."

"June." She stepped onto the porch. The ticking followed her, faint but clear. "What do you mean, after dark?"

Eli shifted his weight. "The clock. It's counting down."

"You know about that?"

"Everyone around here knows." He glanced past her into the hallway. "Your grandmother tried to stop it. She couldn't."

June's stomach tightened. "Stop what? What does it count down to?"

Eli didn't answer right away. He walked to the edge of the porch and looked up at the darkening sky. "How much time is left?"

"I don't know. Eleven fifty-something."

"Then you have until midnight." He turned back to her. "It's not hours. It's minutes. When it reaches twelve, someone in this house dies."

June laughed, a short, ugly sound. "That's insane."

"Is it?" Eli pointed at the clock through the doorway. "Your grandmother wrote to my mom about it. She said the clock chose her when she moved in. It gave her three days. She spent them trying to break it."

"And she failed."

"She ran out of time." Eli's voice was quiet. "But she left something. A journal. My mom has it. She said to give it to you if you came."

June's hands were shaking. She shoved them into her pockets. "Why would she leave me a haunted house?"

"Maybe she thought you could finish what she started." Eli pulled a folded paper from his jacket. "This is a copy of the last page. My mom made it before the original fell apart."

June took it. The handwriting was cramped, urgent. A ritual, broken into steps. At the bottom, a single line: It will ask for a life. Give it one.

"A life," June repeated.

"That's what it says." Eli met her eyes. "The clock doesn't stop on its own. Someone has to take the countdown. Become the new keeper."

Behind her, the clock ticked. June turned and saw the hands: eleven forty-four. Sixteen minutes.

"We need that journal," she said.

Eli nodded. "Follow me. And hurry."

June followed Eli through the kitchen and down a narrow back stair. The air grew cold and thick, and the ticking of the clock followed her like a second heartbeat. At the bottom, a low door led into a cellar that smelled of damp earth and old wood. Eli pulled a flashlight from his jacket. "My mom said the journal was hidden somewhere down here. She never found it."

"Then how do you know it's here?" June whispered.

"Because your grandmother wrote her a letter. Said she'd hidden it where the house couldn't watch." Eli swept the beam across shelves of dusty jars. "Whatever that means."

June's skin prickled. She turned slowly, and the cellar was gone. She stood in a bright kitchen, sunlight streaming through a window. A woman in an apron hummed at the stove. June tried to speak, but her voice was gone. The woman turned, and her face was wrong—her eyes were clock faces, hands spinning. "You're late," she said, and the room dissolved into a roar of ticking.

June gasped and stumbled against a shelf. Eli caught her arm. "What happened?"

"I saw someone. A woman. She had clocks for eyes." June's voice shook. "She said I was late."

Eli's face paled. "The clock shows you things. My mom said it does that near the end. It shows you the ones who ran out of time."

June pressed her hands to her ears, but the ticking was inside her head now. Another vision flashed—a man clawing at a door, screaming as the clock struck. Then a girl no older than June, curled in a corner, whispering a name over and over. June blinked and the cellar returned. "How many?" she asked.

"I don't know. A lot." Eli's voice was tight. "Your grandmother tried to save them. She couldn't."

June looked at the paper in her hand. "The ritual. It has to work." She scanned the steps. "We need the journal. The copy is missing something—the last line is cut off."

Eli nodded. "Then we find it. Where would your grandmother hide something from the house?"

June thought of the vision—the woman's clock eyes, the man at the door. "Somewhere the clock can't see. Maybe outside." She looked at the cellar walls. "Or under it."

***

They searched the cellar for an hour, moving boxes and tapping stones. The ticking grew louder, faster. June's hands were raw when she found a loose brick behind a shelf. She pulled it out, and a small leather book fell into her palm. "Got it."

Eli hurried over. The journal was thin, its pages yellowed and fragile. June opened it carefully. Her grandmother's handwriting filled the first page. "If you're reading this, the clock has chosen you. I'm sorry. I tried to stop it, but the ritual needs a keeper. Someone must take the countdown so the house can rest. I couldn't do it. I was afraid. Maybe you are braver than me."

June's throat tightened. She turned the page. The ritual was written in careful steps. "It says we need to go to the clock at midnight and speak the words. But the last step—" She squinted. "The ink is smudged."

Eli leaned closer. "What does it say?"

"Something about a life. 'The clock will take what is offered. A life for a life.'" June looked up. "That's it. That's the sacrifice."

"But it doesn't say whose life," Eli said slowly.

June stared at the page. "No. It doesn't." She flipped to the back. A single line was scrawled there, almost illegible: "The keeper must be willing. The house will know."

"Willing," June repeated. "So I have to choose. Me or—" She stopped.

Eli met her eyes. "Or me."

"No." June shook her head. "I won't do that."

"Then we find another way." Eli took the journal from her. "There has to be something else. A loophole."

June looked at the clock on the cellar wall—a small one, ticking in time with the one upstairs. "We have until midnight. That's—" She checked her phone. "Six hours."

Eli nodded. "Then we read every page. We'll find something." He sat on an overturned crate and began to read aloud. June listened, but her eyes kept drifting to the smudged line. A life for a life. The house will know.

She thought of her grandmother, afraid and alone, running out of time. She thought of the girl in the vision, whispering a name. And she knew, with a cold certainty, that the clock was already counting down to hers.

At half past eleven, June stood in the hallway with the journal open in her hands. The clock above her ticked, loud as a heartbeat. Eli waited by the cellar door, a candle stub burning in his fist.

"You don't have to do this," he said.

"I know." June didn't look up from the page. "But the ritual needs two things. A name spoken, and a keeper willing to stay. If I name myself, the house takes me. If I name you—"

"Then it takes me." Eli's voice was flat. "You're not naming me, June."

"I wasn't going to." She finally met his eyes. "But there's another way. The journal says the keeper can be anyone who loves the house. My grandmother loved it. That's why it kept her." June swallowed. "I don't love it. Not yet. But I could learn."

Eli stepped closer. "You're talking about dying."

"I'm talking about staying." She closed the journal. "The house wants a guardian. Someone to keep the clock running, to feed it years instead of taking them. If I stay, it stops counting down. It just... is."

"And you're trapped here forever." Eli's candle shook. "That's not a loophole. That's the same thing."

June looked at the clock. Eleven forty. The hands hadn't moved. "Maybe. But at least you'd be alive."

"Don't." Eli grabbed her wrist. "We can burn the journal. We can tear the clock off the wall."

"And then what? It starts again. Some other kid finds it." June pulled free, gently. "I've seen them, Eli. The ones before me. They were scared and alone. I'm not alone. You're here."

He stared at her for a long moment. Then he set the candle on the floor. "Fine. But I'm staying with you. Whatever happens."

June opened the journal to the ritual page. Her hands were steady. "Then hold the candle. And don't let go."

***

At midnight, June knelt in the center of the hallway. The clock above her began to chime, each note slower than the last. She read the words from the journal aloud. The house creaked in answer.

A cold wind rushed through the hall, snuffing Eli's candle. He swore and fumbled for matches. June kept reading. The floorboards groaned. Shadows gathered in the corners and leaned toward her like listeners.

"June!" Eli's voice cut through. "The clock—it's speeding up!"

She looked up. The hands were spinning, faster and faster. The chimes overlapped into a single grinding note. And then, from the walls, she heard whispers.

*Stay. Stay. Stay.*

The journal said the keeper must be willing. June closed her eyes and thought of her grandmother—the smell of her kitchen, the way she hummed while she worked. She thought of Eli, who had come back for her. She thought of the house, cold and waiting.

"I'm willing," she said. "I'll stay."

The clock stopped. Silence fell like a dropped stone.

Eli lit the candle. Its flame burned steady. "June?"

She was still kneeling, but something had changed. The hallway looked different—softer, as if she were seeing it through a window. Her hands were pale. When she touched the floor, her fingers passed through.

"I can't feel it," she said. "The wood. I can't feel it."

Eli ran to her. He tried to grab her arm, but his hands went through her like smoke. "No. No, you said—"

"I said I'd stay." June's voice was quiet. "I didn't say I'd stay alive."

She stood. The clock above her ticked once, softly, and she felt it in her chest—a new heartbeat, slow and patient. The house settled around her. She could feel every room, every crack in the walls. She could feel Eli's fear, sharp and bright.

"You have to go," she said. "It's done. The clock won't count down again."

Eli shook his head. "I'm not leaving you here alone."

"I'm not alone." June smiled, and it was almost her old smile. "I'm the house now. And you're always welcome."

He stood there a moment longer. Then he picked up the journal, blew out the candle, and walked to the door. At the threshold, he turned. "I'll come back. Every day."

"I'll keep the light on," June said.

And she did.
