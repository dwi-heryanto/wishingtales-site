Tomas was three days burying his father and one hour learning what he had inherited. The mill stood where the river bent, its wheel turning slow and patient as it had turned for a hundred years. The villagers came to the funeral, and they came again the next morning to the mill door, not to buy flour but to look at him.

"Your father owed a debt," said Marta, the baker, folding her arms. "To the river. It comes due at the full moon."

Tomas laughed, because the alternative was to sit down on the step. "The river doesn't lend money."

"No," she said. "It lends water."

They told him then, all of them talking at once. His great-grandfather had built the mill without asking the river's leave. The water had risen, drowned the first wheel, and a bargain was struck: the family could keep the wheel turning, but every generation owed the river a payment. His father had paid it, every year, with the first sack of flour and a bowl of milk set on the stones at dusk. The milk was always gone by morning.

"And this year?" Tomas asked.

Marta looked at the water. "This year your father did not set it out. He was ill. The river knows."

That night Tomas went down to the millrace with a lantern. The river ran black and quick, and when he held the light over the water he saw no reflection of himself in it. Only the wheel, turning. Only the stones. The lantern shook in his hand, and he told himself it was the cold.

"Father," he said, because there was no one else to say it to. "You might have told me."

The water did not answer. But it was higher than it had been at noon, and the mark on the mill wall was a hand's width below the surface.

***

In the morning Tomas took the strongbox from under his father's bed. It held forty silver coins, worn thin with handling, and he carried them down to the river in a sack. He waded to the flat stone where the milk had always been set and poured them out. They rang against the rock and then slid, one by one, into the water. The river took them without a sound.

"There," he said. "Paid."

That night the water rose to the mark. The next morning he took the best grain from the loft, a full sack of it, and emptied it onto the stone. The river carried the grain away in a yellow stain. By evening the water was higher still, and the wheel was beginning to groan when it turned, as if something pressed against it.

Marta came at dusk with a bowl of milk, and Tomas took it from her. "It won't work," he said.

"It always worked for your father."

"My father gave it freely. I'm giving it because I'm afraid."

He set the bowl down anyway. In the morning it was still full, and the milk had soured, and the water had climbed past the mark and was lapping at the door of the mill.

That afternoon he tried again. He brought a silver cup, his mother's, and set it on the stone with a stone inside it to keep it from floating. The river took the cup but left the stone. He brought a lamb, and the lamb would not go near the water, and he had to carry it back up the bank with its heart hammering against his chest. He brought his father's boots, and the river pushed them back to shore, filled with silt.

On the third night he sat on the step and watched the water take the bottom stair. The moon was three days from full and already too bright. The mill wheel turned and turned, and every turn brought a little more water into the race, and Tomas understood that the river was not refusing his gifts. It was waiting for the one he had not offered.

"What do you want?" he shouted at the dark water. His voice came back off the far bank, smaller than he had made it.

The river said nothing, but the wheel stopped turning, and the silence was worse than any answer.

The ford lay a mile upstream, where the river ran shallow over a shelf of flat stones. Tomas walked there before dawn, because he could not sleep and because the mill had begun to speak in its timbers, a low complaint that followed him from room to room. Mist stood over the water. A figure sat on the near bank with a stick across her knees, and she did not look up when he came, though he was not quiet.

"You're the miller's boy," she said. Her voice was dry, like paper folded many times. "The one who owes."

"I'm the miller now," Tomas said. "And I've brought everything I have. Coin. Grain. My father's cup, my father's boots. The river won't take any of it."

The old woman laughed, a short sound with no warmth in it. "Course it won't. You're offering what you can spare. The river only takes what you can't." She turned her stick over in her hands. "Your father knew the price. He paid it every year, and the year he died he hadn't paid, and the river's been patient since. Patient as stone. Now the moon's coming full."

"What price?" Tomas asked. "He never told me."

"He wouldn't." She looked at him then, and her eyes were the color of the river in flood. "Your shadow. Given free, not stolen, not tricked. Walk to the water at the full moon and let it go, and the mill stands another lifetime."

Tomas stepped back. The mist moved between them. "That's not a price. That's a piece of me."

"It is," she agreed. "And the river will have it, or it will have the mill, and the bank, and the road, and then it will come for the houses. Your father gave his shadow when he was young. Did you never wonder why he cast none at noon?"

He had wondered. He had asked once, as a child, and his father had said the sun was behind him. "I won't do it," Tomas said.

The old woman shrugged and got to her feet, and her knees made a sound like a closing door. "Then you'd best learn to swim," she said, and she walked into the mist and was gone before he could think of an answer. On the bank where she had sat, the stones were dry. The mist had not touched them.

***

All that day Tomas worked, though there was nothing to do. He piled sandbags at the door. He carried the flour sacks up to the loft, one by one, and stacked them against the beam. The water in the race ran high and brown, and the wheel turned whether he wanted it to or not. By evening the bottom step was gone, and the second was wet.

He did not go inside. He sat on the bank with his back against the willow and watched the moon come up, three nights from full and already so bright that the river carried a white road down its middle. He thought about his father, which he had not let himself do since the funeral. The old man had ground flour for half the valley and never charged the widow at the crossroads, and when Tomas had asked why, he had said, "Because the river gives, and so do we." Tomas had thought that was a fine thing to say. He had not known it was a bill.

"You're not going to do it," he said aloud, to his father, to the water. "I know you'd tell me to. But I'm not." The willow creaked above him. Somewhere a heron called, harsh as a hinge. "I'll find another way."

The moon rose higher. The water crept up the bank an inch at a time, and Tomas watched it come, and did not move. He was afraid. He was afraid in his stomach and his hands, and he sat there anyway, because running would have been worse. He thought: a shadow is the shape of a man in the light. If I give it up, I'll be a man with no shape. I'll walk through the world and leave nothing behind me, and no one will know where I've been. He thought of his father at noon, casting nothing, and understood at last how tired the old man had been.

Near midnight the water stopped rising. It lay still and flat, and the wheel hung motionless, and the whole valley seemed to hold its breath. Tomas got up. His legs were stiff with cold and his boots were wet through. He walked down to the edge and stood where the second step had been, and the river looked back at him with its one bright eye.

"No," he said. "Not my shadow. I'll pay you another way."

The river did not answer. But the water did not take him, either, and that was something. He turned and went up the bank to the mill, and slept in his father's chair with the door open, and dreamed of wheels.

Morning came grey and low, and the river had not moved. Tomas woke in his father's chair with a ache in his neck and the taste of cold iron in his mouth. Outside, the water lay against the mill like a held breath. He washed his face at the pump, ate a heel of bread, and then he went down to the wheel.

The wheel was half sunk, its paddles green with weed. Tomas waded to the hub, the water cold as a knife, and put his hand flat on the wet wood. "I know you're there," he said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "You heard me last night. I said no to the shadow. Now hear the rest."

The river did not answer with words. But the water around his knees began to turn, slow and deliberate, as if something under it were considering him. A line of foam curled past his thigh.

"You want a debt paid," Tomas said. "My father owed you, and now I owe you. I won't pay in shadow. I won't pay in blood. But I'll pay in work. Every year, I'll clear your channel from the ford to the bend. I'll pull the snags and the dead branches. I'll keep the banks from caving in. And I'll keep this wheel turning true. As long as I live, the wheel turns, and the water moves, and the mill stands."

The water went still. Then it rose, quick as a hand closing, up to his chest. Tomas held onto the hub and did not let go. "That's my offer," he said, his teeth chattering. "You can take it or you can drown me. But you'll not have my shadow."

For a long moment nothing happened. Then the water pulled back, just a little, and a sound came up from under the wheel — a low, grinding hum, like a millstone turning far below. It was not a yes. It was not a no. It was the river thinking.

Tomas climbed out and stood dripping on the bank. His father's words came back to him, spoken on a summer evening years ago: The river doesn't want your grief, boy. It wants your hands.

***

All that day the water held steady. Tomas did not wait for an answer. He took the long pole from the shed and went down to the ford, and he began to clear the channel. The work was hard and wet and cold, and the river pushed against him the whole time, but he did not stop. He pulled a rotting branch from the shallows and dragged it up the bank. He cut the reeds that choked the flow. By evening his arms shook and his back was one long ache, but the water ran a little cleaner past the mill.

That night the river did not rise.

The next morning he greased the wheel's axle and patched the sluice gate. The old woman from the ford came by with a basket on her arm and stood watching him. "You're still here," she said.

"I'm still here," Tomas said. "I made an offer."

She looked at the water, then at him. "And what did the river say?"

"Nothing yet. But it hasn't taken anything, either."

The old woman nodded slowly. "That's how it is with rivers," she said. "They don't bargain like men. They watch. They wait to see if your word holds." She shifted her basket. "My father cleared this channel before yours did. He said the river remembers hands that keep their promise. It's the ones who promise and then go soft that it drowns."

Tomas wiped his forehead with his sleeve. "Then I'd better not go soft."

She laughed, a dry sound. "No," she said. "You'd better not." And she went on down the road.

By the third day the water had dropped below the second step. By the fifth, the wheel stood clear, and when Tomas opened the sluice the river pushed against the paddles and the wheel turned — slow at first, then steady, groaning like a thing waking up. He stood in the mill and watched the shaft turn and the stones begin to move, and something in his chest loosened that had been tight for weeks.

The river had not taken his shadow. It had taken his word instead, and found it good.
