The peddler came to the village on a day when the sun was too hot and the flies were too bold. He wore a coat of patched velvet and carried a cart full of odd jars. Each jar held a different kind of light, he said, and he smiled like a man who knew secrets.

Tomas was sweeping the step when the cart stopped. "You there," the peddler said, "you've got a fine shadow. Long and straight. I could use a shadow like that."

Tomas looked down. His shadow stretched across the dusty step, thin and ordinary. "It's just a shadow," he said.

"Just a shadow?" The peddler laughed. "Shadows are never just anything. But I'll tell you what—I'll trade you something better. Something that glows." He lifted a jar from the cart. Inside, tiny stars swirled like fireflies in honey. The light spilled out and made the dust sparkle.

Tomas had never seen anything so bright. "What would you want for it?"

"Your shadow. That's all." The peddler held out the jar. "A shadow for a star-jar. Fair trade."

Tomas thought of the dark evenings at home, when the candle was too short and the room grew dim. A jar of light would be wonderful. He nodded.

The peddler produced a pair of silver scissors and snipped at the air near Tomas's feet. Tomas felt a small tug, like a thread pulling loose. Then the jar was in his hands, warm and humming.

"Pleasure doing business," the peddler said, and his cart rolled on.

***

That evening, Tomas set the jar on the table. Its light filled the whole room, softer than a lamp and brighter than a candle. His mother gasped. "Where did you get that?"

"A peddler," Tomas said. "I traded something I didn't need."

He didn't mention the shadow. It didn't seem important. The jar glowed all night, and Tomas slept without dreaming.

When morning came, the sun was already high. Tomas stepped outside and waited for the warmth to hit his face. It didn't. The air felt cool, as if he were standing in a cave. He looked down at his feet. No shadow lay on the ground.

He ran to the well and looked in. His reflection stared back, but the dark shape that should have stretched behind him was gone.

His mother came out with a basket. "Tomas, fetch water."

"Mama, something's wrong. The sun isn't warm."

"It's warm enough for me," she said, and went back inside.

Tomas held his hand up to the light. It passed straight through, leaving no shade. The jar in his pocket felt heavier than before. He could still hear the peddler's voice: just a shadow. But without it, the world had gone strange and cold.

He needed to find that peddler.

Tomas waited until the light went long and orange. Then he followed the road the peddler had taken, past the mill and the last fence.

At the bend stood an elder tree he had never noticed before. Its branches hung so low they brushed the dust. Under it, a sign creaked on a nail: SHADOW MARKET, DUSK TILL DARK.

"Dusk till dark," Tomas said to himself. "That isn't long."

He sat and waited. The sun slid down. The moment its edge touched the hills, the road folded open like a door, and there was a market where no market had been.

Stalls stood in rows, lit by nothing. Lanterns hung with no flame in them. Shadows lay piled in baskets, heaped on tables, draped over poles like wet washing. They were every shape. A tall man's shadow. A dog's shadow. Shadows of birds with their wings half open.

"Looking for something?" said a woman with a basket of small dark shapes.

"My shadow," said Tomas. "A peddler took it."

She laughed softly. "Then you'll want the tall stall at the end. He keeps the good ones."

Tomas walked between the tables. His feet made no shade, and the other shadows leaned away from him as he passed, as if they smelled something wrong.

At the last stall, behind a jar of starlight just like his own, sat the peddler.

"Well," the peddler said. "The boy with the bright jar. Did you enjoy it?"

"It doesn't warm me," said Tomas. "The sun goes through me. I want my shadow back."

The peddler smiled. "Of course you do. Everyone does."

***

From under the counter the peddler lifted a folded shape. It was dark and quiet and exactly the size of Tomas. It wriggled like a fish in his hands.

"Here it is," he said. "Safe and sound. Now, what will you give me?"

"I have the jar," said Tomas. "You can have it back."

The peddler tipped his head. "The jar was the price for the shadow. You already paid it. To get the shadow back, you pay a new price."

"That isn't fair."

"Markets aren't fair," said the peddler pleasantly. "Markets are markets." He held the shadow up to the lantern light. "I'll tell you what I want. Give me the memory of your mother's lullaby."

Tomas's mouth went dry. "Her lullaby?"

"The one she sings when the fire is low. You know the one. Give it to me, and this shadow is yours." The peddler's voice was smooth as a stone in a river. "It's only a song. You'll still have your mother. You just won't remember her singing."

A woman nearby set down her basket to listen. A small shadow in a jar pressed itself to the glass.

Tomas thought about the song. He thought about how it started low and climbed, and how his mother's voice cracked on the high part, and how he always waited for the crack.

"No," he said.

The peddler's smile thinned. "No?"

"You can have the jar," said Tomas. "That's what I came to trade. But not the song. Not that."

The peddler looked at him for a long moment. Then he set the folded shadow down on the counter, and it lay there, waiting.

The peddler drummed his fingers on the counter. "A jar of starlight for a shadow," he said. "That is a fair trade, if a dull one. But you came to my market with nothing else to offer, boy. Why should I give you the easy way?"

Tomas looked past him at the rows of stalls. Shadows hung from hooks like dark washing. A woman was sweeping a walkway between them, slow and careful, and the broom looked very heavy.

"I could work," said Tomas. "I'm good at sweeping. I sweep our yard every morning before the sun's up."

The peddler laughed, and it was not a kind laugh. "Work? In my market?"

"Until you say it's enough," said Tomas. "The jar for the shadow, and me for the broom."

The woman with the broom stopped sweeping. She turned her head, and the small shadow in the jar beside her pressed its face to the glass again.

The peddler studied Tomas for a long moment. "One night," he said at last. "From dusk to the last dark. If the stalls are clean when the market closes, the shadow is yours."

"And the jar?"

"And the jar," the peddler agreed, as if the jar were the least interesting thing in the world.

So Tomas took the broom. It was taller than he was, and the handle was smooth with other people's hands. He swept the walkways between the stalls, and the dust rose up grey and fine. Somewhere behind him, the peddler began to hum.

The night went long. Tomas swept past jars of moonlight and crates of borrowed voices. He swept past a stall of lost buttons and one of forgotten names. His arms ached, and his feet ached, and the starlight in his pocket glowed warm against his leg.

When the market's lamps guttered low, the peddler came back. He looked at the clean stones. He looked at Tomas, dusty to the elbows.

"Well," he said. "You swept." He picked up the folded shadow and held it out. "Take it. And take your jar. The starlight is yours to keep, though I'll warn you it's a lonely sort of light."

Tomas took the shadow. It was cool and soft, like a folded shirt, and it fitted back onto his feet as if it had never left.

***

The road home was grey before dawn. Tomas walked with the jar under his arm and his shadow stretching out ahead of him, long and thin and his own. When the sun came up over the hill, he felt it on his face, warm as a hand.

His mother was at the gate. "You're up early," she said, and then she looked at the jar. "What's that?"

"Starlight," said Tomas. "I traded for it."

She raised one eyebrow and said nothing, which was how she asked questions she knew he would answer later.

That evening the neighbors came to see. Tomas set the jar on the table, and the light spilled out across the room, white and blue and trembling, brighter than any lamp. The children put their hands in it. Old Marta said it made her feel young.

But when everyone had gone, Tomas sat alone with the jar and found it was not as bright as he remembered. A jar of light, and no one to see it by, was a small and quiet thing.

So the next night he carried it down to the square and set it on the well. "Come and see," he called. "It's for everyone."

People came. They brought bread and chairs and a fiddle. The starlight shone on all their faces at once, and it did not dim. Tomas sat on the well's edge and watched, and his shadow sat with him, and neither of them felt the cold.

"You gave it away," said his mother, sitting down beside him.

"No," said Tomas. "I just stopped keeping it."

She smiled, and after a while she began to sing, low and climbing, and her voice cracked on the high part, and Tomas waited for the crack.
