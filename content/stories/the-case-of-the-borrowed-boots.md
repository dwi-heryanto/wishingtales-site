Rosa found the boots on the front step just after breakfast. They were big brown boots, caked in mud, and they were standing side by side like they were waiting for someone.

She looked up the street and down it. Nobody was there. A blue jay yelled at her from the fence, and then the street was quiet again.

"Mum," she called through the screen door. "There are boots on our step."

Her mother came out drying a bowl. "Whose are they?"

"That's what I'm going to find out," said Rosa.

She crouched down. The mud on the boots was still a little soft. Small bits of grass were stuck to the heels, and one toe had a smudge of something pale and yellow, like pollen. Mud is a clue, Rosa thought. Mud never lies about where it has been.

She fetched her notebook from under her pillow and wrote it all down in careful capitals. Big boots. Very muddy. Left on the step in the night.

Then she had a thought, and went round to the side of the house, where the grass was long and the gate creaked. A trail of muddy footprints led away from the step, dark and clear on the pale path.

"Aha," said Rosa, and her heart did a little skip.

She followed the prints as far as the corner, where they stopped by the garden wall. Whatever had worn those boots had come right up to her door in the dark and left them behind.

"Somebody's been here," she said to the blue jay. The jay said nothing useful.

Rosa tucked her notebook under her arm. She had a mystery now, a proper one, and mysteries were meant to be solved before lunch.

***

Old Mr. Penner was first, because he was always up before the sun and saw everything.

"Boots?" He leaned on his gate and thought about it. "Big ones, you say. Not mine. Mine are the size of a bread loaf."

"Did you hear anything last night?" Rosa asked.

"A dog," said Mr. Penner at once. "Barking down by the allotments. Woke me at midnight, the little nuisance."

Rosa wrote: Dog. Midnight. Barking.

Mrs. Okafor was next door to him, hanging washing in the sun. She shook her head when Rosa showed her the boots.

"I didn't hear a dog," she said. "But I did hear footsteps on the path. Quick ones. Like someone hurrying."

"Hurrying where?"

"Toward your house, I'd say. Then nothing at all." She pinned up a shirt and frowned at the sky. "I thought it was the wind."

The last house on the row had a new family in it, Rosa knew, though she hadn't met them yet. A boy had been carrying boxes in all week. When she knocked, nobody answered, only a curtain twitched.

Rosa stood on the step and studied her clues. A dog. Quick footsteps. Muddy boots left at her door.

"Somebody was out all night," she said slowly, "looking for something."

The blue jay landed on the wall and cocked its head at her, as if it knew the answer and wasn't telling.

Rosa followed the mud. It made a crooked line from her doorstep, across the path, and along the side of the house. The prints were small and neat, like a row of tiny full stops. A few steps on, the mud turned to smears. Then to flecks. Then to a single dark spot on the grass.

At the garden shed, the trail stopped. The shed door was not quite shut. Rosa put her ear to the gap and heard a sound like a small engine running. A growl. Then a whimper.

"Hello?" she said softly. "It's only me."

The whimpering stopped. Rosa eased the door open. Inside, between the rake and the flowerpots, a little brown dog was curled into a ball. Its tail was tucked under. Its eyes were very wide.

"Oh," said Rosa. "You're the midnight barker."

The dog thumped its tail once, uncertainly. Rosa knelt down and held out her hand. "It's all right. I'm not cross. You've had a long night, haven't you?"

Something was looped around the dog's neck. A piece of blue rope, frayed at one end. Rosa gave it a gentle tug, and the dog crept forward, sniffing her fingers. Its paws were caked in mud.

"You've been walking all over the village," she said. "No wonder you're tired."

The dog licked her hand. Rosa laughed. "Right," she said. "Let's find out who you belong to."

***

Rosa took another look at the muddy boots. They were smallish. Scuffed at the toes. One lace was snapped and knotted back together. She thought about the new boy next door, carrying boxes all week, and the curtain that twitched when she knocked.

"Those are his," she said out loud. "They have to be."

The dog followed her to the gate, close at her heels. Rosa crossed the path and knocked again. This time the door opened. The boy looked tired, with dark circles under his eyes and his hair standing up.

"Have you seen a dog?" he asked, before she could speak. "Small, brown, answers to Biscuit?"

Rosa stepped aside. The dog gave a yelp and shot through the door, and the boy dropped to his knees. "Biscuit! You came back!" He buried his face in the dog's fur. "I've been looking everywhere."

When he looked up, his eyes were wet. "Where did you find him?"

"In my shed," said Rosa. "He left his muddy boots on my doorstep."

The boy stared at her. "His boots?"

"Yours, I mean. I found your boots. That's how I knew."

He looked down at his bare feet and gave a shaky laugh. "I kicked them off in the garden last night. I was so worried I didn't even notice." He stood up and held out his hand. "I'm Sam."

"Rosa," she said. "I like solving mysteries."

Sam grinned. "Then you're exactly who I need."

Rosa followed Sam into the kitchen, where Biscuit was already drinking from a bowl as if he owned the place. The little dog's tail thumped against the cupboard doors. "He's been gone since last night," Sam said, leaning against the counter. "I didn't sleep at all. I just walked up and down the street calling his name." Rosa pulled out a chair and sat down. "You should have knocked on doors," she said. "I would have helped." Sam looked at his bare feet. "I don't know anyone here yet. We only moved in on Saturday." Rosa thought about that. A new boy, a lost dog, and nobody to ask. "Well," she said, "now you know me." Biscuit finished the water and trotted over to sniff Rosa's shoes. She reached down and scratched behind his ears. "He's very good at escaping, isn't he?" "He's the worst," said Sam. "Or the best. Depends how you look at it." Rosa laughed. Outside, the morning sun was coming through the window in long yellow bars. Sam found a biscuit tin and set it on the table. "My mum made these before she went to work," he said. "You can have one if you want." Rosa took one. It was still warm. "So," she said, brushing crumbs off her jumper, "how did he get out?" Sam sat down across from her. "That's the mystery," he said. "Because I checked every gate before bed. Twice."

***

Sam led Rosa out to the garden behind his house. The grass was flattened in one corner, and a low hedge ran along the back fence. "Look," he said, pointing. There was a gap in the hedge, just wide enough for a small brown dog. "I didn't see that last night," Sam said. "It was dark." Rosa crouched down and peered through the gap. On the other side, she could see her own garden shed. "He went straight to my shed," she said. "He must have smelled something good." Sam nodded slowly. "There were sausages in the bin," Rosa added. "My dad burned them on Friday." Sam put his hand over his mouth, but he was laughing. "He's a terrible dog," he said. "He's a clever dog," said Rosa. "He found the only warm shed on the street." They walked back around to the front, where the muddy boots were still sitting on Rosa's step. Biscuit followed, his nose to the ground. Rosa picked up the boots and held them out. "You'll want these back." Sam took one and looked at it. "They're ruined," he said. "Mum will go mad." "Or," said Rosa, "you could leave them with me. As evidence." Sam tilted his head. "Evidence of what?" "Of the first mystery we solved together." Sam grinned and put the boot back down. "Deal," he said. Biscuit barked once, as if he agreed. "Same time tomorrow?" said Rosa. "I'll find us a new mystery." Sam nodded. "I'll bring the biscuits." Rosa walked home with mud on her hands and a plan already forming in her head.
