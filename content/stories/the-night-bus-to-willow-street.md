The 11:40 bus was the last one. Priya had the timing of it down to a science. Ten minutes past her shift at the cinema on Wednesdays and Fridays, twelve on Tuesdays when the late film let out. She'd stand at the corner by the dry cleaner's, hood up against the wind off the river. Then she'd count the streetlights until the bus came grumbling around the bend with its single passenger already aboard.

That was the thing. The bus was never empty. There was always the old man.

He sat in the same place, three rows back on the right, next to the window. Grey coat, grey hair, a canvas bag on his knees that he never opened. He got on at the stop before hers and got off at hers. In between he watched the streets slide by as if he were reading them.

Priya didn't like it. She'd learned the rules the way every girl learns them. One of the rules was that a man who rides the same bus every night and gets off at your stop is a man you keep an eye on. So she sat at the front, by the driver, and she watched him in the mirror above the doors. He never looked back. He never looked at her at all.

Her mother worked the late shift at the care home across town. She came home smelling of disinfectant and other people's kitchens. "You got the bus all right?" she'd ask, every night, from the doorway of the kitchen, still in her coat.

"Got the bus all right," Priya would say.

Neither of them said the rest of it. The flat was dark when Priya let herself in, and stayed dark, and the fridge hummed like it was keeping somebody company.

***

It rained for a week in October, the kind of rain that comes sideways and gets under your hood no matter what you do. Priya stood at the corner with her bag over her head and her shoes full of water. When the bus finally came she climbed on dripping. The old man was in his usual seat. Every other seat was taken by people who'd given up waiting for a dry walk home.

The only empty place was beside him.

She stood in the aisle a moment, holding the rail, water running off her sleeves. The driver glanced back. The old man looked up from his window. Then, without a word, he gathered his canvas bag and slid over against the glass. He left her the aisle seat, the one further from him.

She sat down. "Thanks."

"It's a wet night," he said. His voice was quiet and a little rough, like a coat that had been hung up too long. "You'll want the window when it's like this. You can see the lights in the puddles."

She looked. He was right. The streetlamps came apart in the wet road and lay there in long yellow pieces. The bus rolled through them and put them back together again.

"You ride this bus a lot," she said, because it was the least strange thing she could think to say.

"Every night," he said. "Rain or not. My wife used to say I was a creature of habit. She meant it kindly. Mostly."

Priya almost laughed. "My mum says that about me. She says I've got a route and I stick to it."

"A route is a good thing to have," the old man said. "It's the knowing where you're going that matters. Some nights the only thing that gets me home is knowing this bus does."

The bus turned onto the high street and the shops went by, shuttered and quiet, their signs dark. He nodded at the bakery. "That used to be a shoe shop. Fellow in there would mend anything. Boots, bags, harness, anything. He had a parrot that knew the prices."

"You're joking."

"I never joke about the parrot." He said it so flatly that she did laugh, and he looked pleased, as if he'd been waiting all evening for that.

The rain drummed on the roof. The bus stopped, and nobody got on, and it pulled away again.

"I'm Priya," she said.

"I know," he said. "It's on your bag." He pointed at the little embroidered tag her mother had sewn on in September. "I'm not a spy. It's just a good bag."

She turned the tag over in her fingers and felt the small, ridiculous heat of having been afraid of nothing.

"I'm off at the next stop," she said.

"So am I," he said. "So am I."

***

The next night the rain had stopped, and the bus smelled of wet wool and diesel. Priya found him in the same seat, third from the back on the left, and she sat down across the aisle without being asked. He was looking out the window at the dark shapes of the houses.

"Which one was yours?" she asked.

He turned. "How did you know it was one of these?"

"You always look at them the same way. Like you're counting."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he pointed with two fingers, not quite touching the glass. "That one. The yellow one with the blue door. That was my mother's house. I grew up in it, and when she died I bought it from my brothers and sisters, and I raised my own children in it."

Priya looked. The house was small and narrow, wedged between a taller brick building and a shuttered shop. The blue door was peeling. A bicycle leaned against the wall.

"It's still there," she said.

"That's not the house I loved." He said it without drama, as if he were reading a label. "The house I loved had a garden out back. Apple tree, a plum, a shed my father built. The people who bought it from me tore it all out. Put in a patio and a hot tub. The tree's gone. The shed's gone. The garden's a square of grey stone now." He shrugged. "So I ride this bus. It goes past the front of it, and I can see the shape of the roof and the window where my daughter used to wave at me when I came home."

The bus turned a corner and the yellow house slid out of view. Priya watched it go.

"That's sad," she said.

"It's not sad," he said. "It's just what happened. Sad is when you stop looking."

She thought about that. Outside, a man was walking a small dog under the streetlights. The dog stopped to sniff a lamppost and the man waited, patient, hands in his pockets.

"My mum works late," she said. "That's why I'm on this bus. She's a nurse. She does the evening shift three times a week. She gets home about midnight, and by then I'm usually asleep."

He nodded slowly. "And your dad?"

"Not around." She said it the way she always did, flat and quick, like closing a door. "It's fine. I'm fourteen. I can make toast."

"I'm sure you can." He looked at her. "But that's not the same as not being lonely."

Priya stared at the back of the seat in front of her. The fabric was worn in a pattern, little diamonds of thread rubbed thin by years of shoulders. She hadn't said the word lonely to anyone. Not to her mother, who would have felt guilty, and not to her friends, who would have made it a joke. The old man had said it for her, plainly, and now it sat in the air between them like something she could pick up.

"The flat's quiet," she said. "When I get home, I turn on the TV before I take my coat off. Just for the noise. I don't even watch it. I just need something in the room."

He didn't say anything for a while. The bus stopped and a woman got on with a bag of groceries, and the doors hissed shut, and they pulled away again.

"I know the feeling," he said at last. "After my wife died, I used to leave the radio on in the kitchen all night. Just so I'd hear a voice. It didn't matter what it said."

Priya looked at him. His hands were folded on the top of his cane, and his knuckles were big and pale.

"Did it help?" she asked.

"A little," he said. "Not much. But a little is something."

The bus turned onto her street. The lights were on in the flat above the laundrette, and she knew the one beside it would be dark. She stood up and pulled the cord.

"Goodnight," she said.

"Goodnight, Priya."

She stepped off into the cold and stood on the pavement, watching the bus pull away. The old man's silhouette stayed in the window until the bus turned the corner. Then she went up the stairs to her flat, unlocked the door, and turned on the TV before she took off her coat. The screen lit the empty room with a blue glow. She sat on the sofa and thought about the yellow house with the blue door, and the garden that wasn't there anymore, and the old man riding the bus every night to see the roof of the house he'd loved. It made her own quiet feel less like a wall and more like a room, with a door in it, and someone else on the other side of the door, listening for the same noise.

***

The next week she started sitting next to him instead of across the aisle. He didn't comment on it, but he moved his cane to the other side without being asked. She learned that his name was Albert, though he said everyone called him Bert, and that he had been a printer before he retired. She learned he could still name every street on the route in order from memory.

"You should be a tour guide," she said.

"I'm too old to be a tour guide. I'd fall asleep in the middle of the tour."

On Thursday it rained again, hard, and the bus was crowded with people who didn't usually take it. Priya had to squeeze past three wet coats to reach her seat. Bert was already there, his collar turned up, a folded newspaper on his knee. He looked tired. More tired than usual, she thought, though she couldn't say exactly how she knew.

"Long day?" she asked.

"Long week." He smiled, but it took him a moment. "Don't worry about me. I'm made of old boots and stubbornness."

The bus crawled through the rain. The windows fogged, and Priya drew a small circle in the condensation with her finger. Through it she could see the yellow house with the blue door, the bicycle still leaning against the wall.

"Do you ever want to knock?" she asked. "On your old house. Just to see inside."

He considered it. "I did once. About a year after I sold it. A young woman answered, and she was very kind, and she let me stand in the hallway. But it wasn't my hallway anymore. It smelled of her cooking, not my mother's. The stairs creaked in different places." He shook his head. "I didn't go back again. Some things you should remember the way they were."

The rain hammered on the roof. Priya thought about her own flat, which she had lived in for three years, and which still felt like a place she was staying rather than a place she was from. She wondered if that would ever change.

"Bert," she said. "Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask me anything. Doesn't mean I'll answer."

"Why do you get off at my stop? There's nothing there for you."

He was quiet for a long moment. The bus stopped, and the doors opened, and the cold wet air came in. Nobody got on. The doors closed again.

"Because it's the end of the line," he said. "And I like to see someone get off and go home. It's a small thing. But it's a thing."

Priya didn't know what to say to that. She looked at her hands in her lap. The bus turned onto her street, and the lights of the laundrette came up, and she stood.

"Goodnight, Bert."

"Goodnight, Priya."

She stepped off into the rain and ran for the door. Halfway up the stairs she stopped and looked back. The bus was still there, its engine idling, and she could see him through the wet glass, a dark shape in the window, watching until she was safely inside. Then the bus pulled away. The street was empty. She went up to her flat and turned on the TV and sat in the blue light. For the first time in a long time the quiet didn't feel like it was waiting for something.

***

The rain had stopped by Thursday, which made the bus feel different, all its windows clear and the wet streets shining like something polished. Priya took her usual seat near the back and watched the doors. At the hospital stop a nurse got on, and a boy with a cello case, and at the corner by the laundrette a woman with too many bags. Bert wasn't there.

She told herself it was nothing. People missed buses. Old men especially, she thought, though she had no idea if that was true. She watched every stop anyway, and at each one the doors opened onto nobody, and the bus pulled away with a small disappointed hiss.

By the time they reached the end of the line her chest had gone tight. She stayed in her seat as the last passengers filed off, and the driver turned around and raised his eyebrows at her.

"You getting off, love?"

"Is the old man not on tonight?" She said it too fast. "The one who sits at the front. Bert."

The driver's face changed, softened into something careful. "Bert. He's not been on since Tuesday. He's poorly, I heard. His daughter rang the depot and asked us to keep his pass for him."

Priya stared at the empty seat by the window, at the little glass shelf where he used to rest his cap. "I don't know his other name," she said. "I only know Bert."

"That's more than most know," the driver said. "He's at the hospital down the road. Not far. If you were thinking of it."

She walked home slowly, and the flat was dark, and she did not turn on the TV. She sat at the kitchen table with a glass of water and thought about a man who rode a bus to watch a stranger go inside, and how she had never once asked him anything that mattered.

***

The hospital smelled of toast and bleach, and the corridors were longer than they looked from the road. Priya had written his name as Bert on the visitor sheet and the woman at the desk found him anyway. "Third floor, bed by the window. He'll be glad of it. He's been asking after the bus."

He looked smaller in the bed, the way people do, but his eyes were the same. "Priya," he said, and then, "You came on the bus?"

"I came on my feet. The bus doesn't stop here." She sat in the plastic chair and put her schoolbag on the floor. "You weren't on it. The driver told me."

"Ah. Well." He shifted against the pillow. "It's the heart. They say it's mending. They say a lot of things." He looked out the window at the car park and the grey sky. "I've been lying here thinking about the bus. About that seat. It's a silly thing to miss."

"It's not silly." Priya said it louder than she meant to. "You were there every night. It mattered."

Bert was quiet a moment. Then he reached to the little cabinet beside his bed and took out a key on a string, small and brass and worn smooth at the bow. "Willow Street," he said. "There's a garden plot at the back of the allotments, number nine. I've had it forty years. My wife grew roses in it, and then she didn't, and I kept the roses anyway." He held the key out. "I can't get down there now. The weeds will be winning. Someone should be winning against the weeds."

Priya looked at the key in his palm. "You don't know me. Not really."

"I know you get off at my stop," Bert said. "I know you looked back once. That's enough to start."

She took the key. It was warm from his hand.

"It needs work," he said. "Go and see it. Tell me what's still alive."

"I will," she said. She stayed until the nurse came with the tea. She told him about the bus, and the rain, and the boy with the cello case. He laughed, and it turned into a cough, and then into a laugh again.

***

The allotments were behind the terraced houses on Willow Street, through a gate that stuck, and number nine was the last plot before the fence. Priya stood at its edge on Saturday morning with her mother's trowel in her bag and felt her stomach drop. Grass had gone over everything in long pale waves. The roses were still there, though, three bushes against the far fence, leaves dark and glossy, and one of them had a single bud the colour of a bruise.

She went in through the little gate and started pulling. It took hours. Her mother came at noon with a flask and stood at the edge watching her.

"You've been quiet all week," her mother said. "And now you're gardening. In someone else's allotment."

"It's Bert's. The man from the bus." Priya sat back on her heels and wiped her hands on her jeans. "He's ill. He gave me the key."

Her mother looked at the roses, then at her daughter. "You'll want gloves," she said. "There's a pair in the shed at home."

By evening Priya had cleared a path to the roses and found a watering can under a collapsed shelf. There was a bench, and a stone with a name carved into it that she did not read for very long. She filled the can at the tap by the gate and watered the roses the way Bert had not been able to. The water went dark on the soil and the leaves lifted.

She took the bus home that night and sat in the seat by the window. She thought she would tell him about the bud, and about the grass, and about the stone. The flat was still empty when she got there. It was still quiet. But the key was in her pocket, and the roses were alive. She stood in the kitchen with the light on and heard the bus pull away down the street, and she did not feel like she was waiting for anything at all.
