The lamp room smelled of hot brass and paraffin. Nell stood on the stool with the wick trimmer in her hand, squinting at the flame. It needed to be even, or the light would smoke and the glass would blacken by midnight.

"You do that every single night," Tessa said from the window. "It's not going to change."

"It changes if I don't." Nell scraped a curl of charred wick into the tin. "That's the whole point."

Tessa pressed her forehead to the cold glass. "There's a storm coming. Look at the sea."

Nell looked. The water beyond the pier was the colour of slate, and the waves were short and sharp, the way they got before a blow. "Wind's from the nor'east," she said. "Dad said to watch for that."

"Dad said a lot of things." Tessa turned away. "He also said he'd be back before dark."

Nell climbed down and opened her notebook. Oil, one and a half cans. Wick trimmed at six. Weather, nor'east rising. She wrote it all in her careful hand.

A thump came at the window. Both girls froze.

A gull was clinging to the sill, wings spread flat against the glass. Its feathers were plastered dark with water. One wing hung at a wrong angle.

"Oh," said Nell. "Oh, no."

***

The gull watched them with a bright, unblinking eye. Its beak was slightly open, and it made no sound at all.

"We have to bring it in," Nell said. She was already reaching for the latch. "It'll die out there."

"No." Tessa put her hand flat on the window. "We can't. If it panics in here it'll knock the lamp and then where are we?"

"It's not going to knock the lamp. It's half dead."

"It's a wild bird, Nell. You can't just pick it up like a kitten." Tessa's voice was sharp. "Haven't you ever seen what a trapped bird does? It throws itself at walls."

Nell's hand stayed on the latch. The gull shuddered, and a drop of water ran down the glass from its breast. "Then what do we do? Leave it out there to be blown off the sill?"

"It's got wings. It'll go somewhere."

"It can't. Look at it." Nell's voice cracked. "Its wing's broken, Tessa."

Tessa was quiet for a moment. The wind pushed at the tower and the whole room seemed to lean. "Fine," she said at last. "But not near the lamp. And if it starts flapping, it goes back out."

Nell nodded quickly. "The coal box. It's dark and low and away from the glass."

"And you're the one handling it," Tessa said. "I'm not getting pecked."

Nell unlatched the window. The wind came in first, cold and full of salt, and the gull turned its head toward the dark and did not move.

The bird weighed almost nothing. Nell lifted it with both hands and it lay still, one wing stuck out at a wrong angle, the other folded tight. Its heart knocked against her palms like a knuckle on a door.

"Easy," she said. "Easy now."

Tessa held the lamp high and watched from the far side of the room. "Coal box's behind you. Don't put your hand in the black bits."

Nell knelt and pulled the rags out of the basket by the stove, old ones, soft ones. She made a hollow in the coal box, away from the glass, and set the bird in it. The gull turned once and settled, its good wing over the broken one, and closed its yellow eye.

"There," Nell whispered.

"It's still a wild thing," Tessa said, but she said it quietly.

Outside, the wind had stopped playing. It was working now, shoving at the tower in long pushes, and the rain came sideways at the glass. The flame in the lamp leaned over and came back, leaned and came back, like someone walking a deck.

Nell watched it. "Tessa."

"I see it."

***

A gust hit the tower broadside. The shutter on the seaward window tore loose and slammed, and the flame in the lamp bent flat and went out.

Dark came down on the room like a lid.

"Shutter!" Tessa shouted. "Get the shutter!"

Nell was already moving. She got both hands on the swinging board and hauled it back against the frame, and the wind fought her for it, and she leaned her whole weight and held. Rain came through the gap and ran down her wrists.

Behind her she heard the scrape of the matchbox, then a small bright sound, then nothing.

"Wet," Tessa said. "The first one's wet."

"Try another."

"I am trying another."

Nell could not turn to look. Her arms were shaking and the board kept trying to pull free. "Tessa. It's got to be lit."

"I know it's got to be lit." A match struck. A long breath of quiet. "Hold still, hold still."

Then the light came back, small and yellow, and grew, and the room was a room again. Tessa set the glass down over it with both hands and stood there a moment with her palms flat on the brass, as if she were keeping it from flying off.

From the coal box, the gull had not moved. Its eye was open and it was watching them, calm as anything, its good wing still over the broken one.

"It never made a sound," Nell said.

"No." Tessa looked at the bird. "It never did."

The wind leaned on the tower all night. Nell sat on the coal box with her back to the wall and her knees drawn up. The gull watched her from its nest of rags. Its eye was bright and black and did not blink.

"You're not frightened," she said. "Or you are, and you're being polite." The bird turned its head. "That's what I thought." She pulled her cardigan tighter. "The light's still on. Tessa saw to that." The gull shifted, and the broken wing stayed tucked. "She's better at some things than she lets on."

The hours went by slowly. Nell talked to fill them. She told the bird about the tally in her notebook, and the boats she could name, and the way the harbour looked at low tide with the weed hanging off the stones. The gull listened, or seemed to. Once the wind hit the tower so hard the glass shook, and she put her hand flat against it until the shaking stopped.

Near midnight the trapdoor lifted and Tessa came up with two mugs. Steam curled off them. "Tea," she said. "The last of the milk."

Nell took hers with both hands. "You should sleep."

"So should you." Tessa sat down on the floor with her back against the coal box, close enough that their shoulders touched. She nodded at the bird. "It's still here, then."

"It's still here."

Tessa drank her tea and looked out at the dark. "I'm glad it's here," she said, quietly, as if she hoped the wind would carry it off. "It's company. Don't tell anyone I said that."

"I won't." Nell smiled into her mug. "I'll put it in the tally."

***

The wind dropped before the light came. Nell noticed it the way you notice a noise when it stops. The tower was quiet, and the glass was clear, and out past the headland the sea was turning from black to grey.

The gull stood up in the coal box. It stretched its good wing wide, then folded it, then stretched it again. The broken one it held carefully, like a hand it did not want to use yet.

"You're going," Nell said.

She carried the box to the window and set it on the sill. The gull looked at her a moment, head on one side. Then it stepped up onto the edge and opened both wings, the broken one low and stiff, and dropped into the air. It caught the wind and rose. Nell watched it until it was a small grey shape over the water, going where gulls go.

Tessa came up with the last of the tea in one mug and handed it over. "Gone?"

"Gone." Nell drank, then opened her notebook and wrote in her careful hand: one gull, returned. She closed it.

They stood together at the window. The sun came up over the harbour. Down on the pier, their father's lamp was moving, a small yellow dot, coming home.

"Kettle's still hot," Tessa said.

"Then we'd better use it."
