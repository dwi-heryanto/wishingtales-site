Otis wrote the evening entry the way he always did, with the lamp pulled close and the window open a crack. The wind came off the hill and turned the page corners, and he wrote: Wind, west, strong. Rain after dark. He did not add anything else. For a week he had left questions in the ledger and set traps and watched the saucer of milk go still. None of it had made the hill any closer. Tonight he just wanted the line written so he could sleep.

The pen went back in the tin. Then he saw it. A line sat under his own, in that other hand, small and upright and sure of itself. It did not answer a question. It said: The frost will come late this year. Otis read it three times. His neck felt cold, though the window was barely open.

"Right," he said, to nobody. He looked at the hill through the glass. It was only a black shape with the last grey light behind it. Nothing moved on it. He put his hand flat on the page, and the ink was dry.

***

Mira came up the lane the next morning with the post in her basket and her brakes squealing. Otis showed her the ledger on the kitchen table before she had taken off her coat.

"The frost will come late," she read out loud. "That's not a question. You didn't ask anything."

"I know."

"So it's a trick." She tapped the page with one finger. "Someone's got a key. Or your grandfather wrote it in his sleep."

"He can't hold a pen."

"Then someone else." Mira folded her arms. "How do you know it's not just weather? Wait long enough and the frost comes late or it doesn't."

Otis had thought of that. It was the sensible thought, and he had not been able to put it down. "Then we watch," he said. "Till the lamp goes low."

Mira looked at him for a long moment. "Fine," she said. "But I'm not sitting in the dark for nothing."

They sat by the window as the evening went blue. The hill held the last light and then let it go. Mira counted the seven houses and named them, and Otis wrote the evening entry with the pen moving slowly. Nothing else appeared. The lamp burned down to a low orange coin, and the page stayed as blank as a held breath.

Mira went home at nine, and the lamp was only a coin of light by then. Otis sat a while longer with the pen in his hand. He was tired of experiments. The saucer, the ink, the careful questions: none of it had got him anywhere he could stand on.

So he wrote a true thing instead, because it was true and because his hand wanted to write something.

*Mira counted the houses and got to seven and then counted again.*

He set the pen down. The lamp ticked. Outside, the hill was a black shape with a thin edge of stars above it.

Then the ink moved.

Not a new line under his. His own line, copied out again in the other hand, the patient one, the one with the old-fashioned loops. *Mira counted the houses and got to seven and then counted again.* The same words. Twice on the page, one under the other, like a boy and his echo.

Otis put both hands flat on the table and breathed. "Right," he said, to nobody. His voice came out small in the kitchen.

He read it again. It had not answered him. It had not asked him anything. It had simply taken what he gave it and given it back, the way you might hand a person a cup of tea and have them hand it back warm, to show they were listening.

He turned the page and wrote underneath, in his own careful hand, very small: *Did you like that better than the questions?*

He waited. The lamp ticked. The page stayed still, and the hill stayed outside, and Otis sat with his chin on his arm until the coin of light went out.

***

He woke in the chair with a stiff neck and the first grey in the window. Grandfather's room was the next one along, and the door was open, and there was a sound in it that was not sleep.

Otis went in. Bram was sitting up against his pillows with his hands on the blanket, looking at the window as if it had said his name.

"Grandad." Otis sat on the edge of the bed. "It's early. Go back down."

"The hill's got a face on it tonight," said Bram, quite clearly. "Always did, at this hour. Your grandmother saw it first. She said it was like a man lying down to listen."

Otis's neck went cold, and then warm. "She said that?"

"She had the eyes for it." Bram turned his head slowly. For a moment his look came all the way back from wherever it went. "You writing it down, boy?"

Here was the choice, and it was a small one, and it was not small at all. The ledger was for weather. Wind, rain, frost, sun, one line each, a hundred years of evenings. It was not for grandmothers, and it was not for faces on hills.

"I could," said Otis.

"Then do," said Bram, and closed his eyes, and was asleep or nearly, and his breathing evened out like weather settling.

Otis went back to the kitchen and opened the ledger at the new page and held the pen over it a long time. Then he wrote, in the space under the last entry, small and straight: *The hill has a face on it at this hour. Grandad says it is a man lying down to listen.*

He set the pen down. His hand was shaking, a little. The page waited, blank as a held breath, and the morning came up grey over the seven houses.

Morning came up grey, and Otis was at the window before the kettle. The line he had written about the hill sat there, plain and true. Under it, nothing. No answer, not yet. He made tea for Bram and left it by the bed, then went out to the wall and waited for Mira.

She came up the lane standing on her pedals, postbag slapping her hip. "You look like you slept in your boots," she said.

"I wrote something that wasn't weather," said Otis. "Grandad told me to."

Mira propped her bike and read the page over his shoulder. Her mouth went flat. "That's a story, Otis. Stories don't go in ledgers."

"I know." He turned the page. "But it's true. He said it."

They stood in the cold a while. Then Mira said, "If it's true, it'll still be true tonight. Go and do your post round. I'll come back at lamp-lighting." She said it like a dare, and Otis said, "Right," and went.

***

By evening the wind had dropped and the hill stood up black against a lemon sky. Mira arrived with her coat buttoned wrong and her chin up. "I'm here to watch," she said. "Not to believe."

"That's fair," said Otis. He lit the lamp and set it on the sill.

He opened the ledger to the new page. Under his own line about the hill, he wrote again, slower this time: *The hill is a man lying down to listen. That is what Grandad says. I am writing it because he asked me to.*

He put the pen down. For a long moment nothing happened. Then the ink on the page seemed to draw breath.

A new line appeared beneath his, in that old, formal hand, each letter upright as a fence post:

*You have been asking what it is. Ask instead what it hears.*

Otis stared. Mira leaned in, close enough that her hair brushed the page. "That's not a trick," she said quietly. "A trick would be cleverer than that."

Otis read the line again. All those nights of questions. What are you. Who writes here. And the hill had been listening the whole time, and he had never once asked what it heard.

***

He wrote the evening entry first, because that was the work. *Wind light from the west. No rain. Frost coming.* Then he sat with the pen and thought about what a hill might hear. Seven houses. A kettle. Bram's breathing through the wall. Mira's bicycle bell. His own pen, scratching.

He wrote, small, under the weather: *Tonight it heard a boy and a girl arguing, and then not arguing. It heard a kettle and an old man snoring. If that is what it wanted, it can have it.*

He did not ask for an answer. The page stayed quiet. But out on the hill a light moved, low and slow, like someone shifting to get comfortable, and then went still.

Mira let out a breath. "I still don't believe it," she said. "But I'll come back tomorrow."

"Right," said Otis. He closed the ledger and turned down the lamp. The hill stayed outside, patient and dark, and for once he did not want it to go away.
