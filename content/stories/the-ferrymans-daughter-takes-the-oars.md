The fever had come on fast. By dusk, Ila's father could not lift his head from the pillow, and his hands shook when he tried to hold the cup she brought him. The ferry bell would ring at moonrise, and the crossing to the far bank took an hour on good water.

"You have to go," he said. His voice scraped like a hull on gravel. "The night crossing does not wait, and the people on the other side are expecting the boat."

Ila stood in the doorway with the lamp in her hand. "I have never taken her out alone."

"You have watched me every night for six years." He turned his face toward her, and his eyes were too bright. "You know the river."

"Knowing and doing are different things." She set the lamp on the stool beside his bed. "What if I run her aground? What if the fog comes?"

"Then you row slow and you listen." He caught her wrist. His fingers were hot and dry. "Swear it to me, Ila. Take the ferry out alone tonight, and never let a passenger steer. Not for any reason. The river is mine to answer to, not yours."

She looked at the window. The sky above the water was the color of old iron, and the first star had already opened its eye.

"I swear it," she said.

He let go and sank back. "Good. The coin box is under the seat. One fare each way, no more. And if the water talks to you, do not answer."

"The water does not talk."

"Not to you," he said, and closed his eyes.

Ila took the lamp and went down to the landing. The ferry lay against the dock, black and patient. She untied the rope with fingers that felt too large, pushed off, and the river took the hull without a sound.

The far landing was a slab of stone with a post and a bell. Ila rang the bell once, as her father always did, and waited. The river moved beneath her, breathing against the boards. No one came.

She was about to ring again when a figure stepped out of the dark beyond the post. A hood covered the face, and the cloak hung to the ankles, gray as the water at dusk. The passenger did not speak, only held out a coin.

Ila took it. The metal was cold, colder than the night air, and it seemed to pull at her palm like a weight. She dropped it into the box and heard it land wrong, a flat sound with no ring.

"One fare," she said. "Sit in the middle. Do not touch the oars."

The passenger sat. The hood tilted toward her, and she felt the gaze like a hand on the back of her neck.

Ila pushed off. The pole went into the water, and the river moved away from it. She felt the oddness in her arms, a hesitation in the current, as if the water had decided not to touch the hull. The ferry drifted out on its own, and the landing fell behind.

"You are not the ferryman," said the passenger. The voice was low and wet, like stones under a stream.

"He is sick," Ila said. "I am his daughter. I am taking the crossing."

The hood turned toward the far bank. "Then row."

Ila rowed. The oars bit the water, and the water let them pass, but the boat moved as if it were sliding over glass. Behind her, the passenger sat perfectly still, and the cold from the coin seemed to have spread into the wood.

Halfway across, the river began to whisper.

The whispering came from everywhere at once, from the hull and the oar blades and the flat black water ahead. Ila could not make out words in it, only a patient murmur, like a crowd waiting for something to be decided.

"You hear it," said the passenger.

"The river always talks at night."

"Not like this." The hood did not move, but the voice seemed to come from closer now, though he had not shifted. "It is talking to you because you are not the one it knows. Turn your head. Look at the far bank."

Ila looked. Past the marked crossing, where the ferry lantern always turned her back toward home, the far shore ran on into the dark. There were no lamps there, no landing, no path up to the village. Only reeds and the low black line of trees and something pale that might have been a pier, or might have been nothing at all.

"There is no crossing there," she said.

"There is a bank." The passenger's hand came out of the cloak, and the coin turned over on his palm, catching the lantern light. "Row me to it and I will pay you twice. Two coins for one night's work. Your father would take it."

The oars went still in Ila's hands. A thin film of cold worked up through the wood into her palms, and she set her jaw against it. "My father would not. The crossing is marked. That is where the ferry goes."

"He has gone past the mark before." The voice was almost gentle. "Once, when the water was high. He told himself it was the same river. It was not."

A breeze came off the water, and the lantern swung on its hook, and for a moment the pale thing on the far bank seemed nearer than it had been. Ila dragged her eyes away from it and pulled the oars back into her chest, ready.

"I do not know what you are," she said. "But I know the rule. A passenger does not steer, and a ferry does not wander. Sit down."

The hood tilted. "You are very like him," said the passenger. "He said the same thing, once."

***

The passenger stood up.

The boat should have rocked. It did not. He rose as though the water were a floor beneath him, and the cloak hung straight down, and the lantern flame guttered sideways as if a wind were pulling at it from below.

"Sit down," Ila said again, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"Row," he said.

The river rose with him. It did not surge or splash; it simply climbed, quietly, up the sides of the hull, and the dark water closed over the gunwales and lay there, trembling, higher than any river should stand. Ila felt the boat go heavy and slow. The current took hold of the stern and began to swing it, gently at first, then harder, turning her bow away from the marked crossing and toward the unlit bank.

She drove the right oar down and pulled. The blade went into the water and the water shoved it back. She tried the left, and the current caught it and twisted it in her grip, and the handle burned cold against her fingers.

"You cannot hold against it," said the passenger. "It is older than your arms."

"Then it will have to be patient." Ila braced both feet against the crossboard and pulled again, and the boat shuddered and came a little way around, and the water climbed her oar blades like hands.

The lantern swung wild now, throwing her shadow across the deck and across the standing figure, who had not moved. His hood was still up, but the cloth had gone dark and heavy with wet, and beneath it there was no face she could name, only a shifting, as of water finding its level.

"You are frightened," he said.

"Yes." She said it plainly, because there was no use in lying to a river. "I am frightened and I am not turning. My father set the mark, and I will hold to it."

She pulled. The boat crept forward, an inch, a hand's width. The current pushed back, and her shoulders ached, and the cold ran up her arms to the elbow, but the bow held its line.

"Why?" said the passenger, and for the first time the wet voice sounded almost curious.

Ila set her teeth and rowed. "Because he is sick in his bed," she said, "and I promised him."

The hood fell back.

Ila had braced for a face, some drowned thing with a mouth. What she got was worse and simpler. Inside the hood there was water, a small column of it, standing upright and holding the shape of a head. It moved as the river moved. When the current swirled past the bow, something in that face swirled too, a mouth that opened and closed like a ripple.

"Ila," it said. Her name came out of it in her father's voice, and then in her mother's, and then in her own.

She stopped rowing. Her hands would not obey her, so she let them rest on the oars and looked at it. "You know my name."

"I have carried it for eleven years." The water-face tilted. "Your father promised me a coin at every crossing. He gave me one at the first. After that he gave me a story, and a story is not a fare."

Ila's stomach dropped. She remembered her father counting coins at the kitchen table, frowning, moving one from the tin to his pocket and then, later, back again. She had thought it was worry about money. It had been a debt he could not face.

"Why did you let him cross?" she said.

"Because the boat was full of people who had paid." The river's voice was flat as a stone skipping. "I take what is owed. I do not take what is not. But the debt sits in the water, and it grows, and tonight it sent me up the bank to collect it. Your father could not come. You came instead."

The lantern guttered. Ila thought of her father's face on the pillow, grey and sweating, and of the coin he pressed into her hand without explaining, the old one with the worn edge. She had put it in her pocket and forgotten it. Now she felt it there, cold through the cloth.

"If I pay you," she said slowly, "the crossing is clean."

"If you pay me," the river said, "the crossing is clean, and the oars are yours, and I go back to being a river and not a creditor. But hear me. Pay me the wrong coin and I take the boat instead. A boat is a fare. A daughter is a fare. I am not cruel. I am only owed."

The water in the hood leaned toward her, and every wave on the river leaned with it, and Ila understood that she had one coin and one chance and no way to know which one the river would take.

***

She rowed.

Not the way she had been rowing, fighting the current inch by inch. This time she turned the bow downstream, gave the river the angle it wanted, and let it carry her while she pulled just enough to keep the mark in sight. The crossing was not a straight line. Her father had told her that, and she had not understood until now. It was a curve, a long slow bend around the deep water, and the mark at the far end was not the bank but a black post standing in the shallows, older than the landing, older than her father.

"There," she said. "That is the true end."

"It is," the river said. "No one has rowed to it in a long while. They stop at the near bank and call it done."

The boat slid past the post, and the current let go all at once, like a hand opening, and the water went smooth and quiet under the hull. Ila shipped the oars and stood, and her legs shook, and she did not care.

She took the coin from her pocket and held it out. It was warm now, warm from her own body, and the worn edge caught the lantern light. The river's face came close and looked at it for a long moment.

"That is the first coin," it said. "The one he gave me and then took back in his heart. He has carried the shame of it eleven years."

"Then take it now," Ila said, "and let him sleep."

The water rose from the hood and wrapped around her hand, cold and quick, and the coin was gone. For a breath there was nothing but the river and the dark and the small light of her lantern. Then the hood was empty cloth, and the boat was only a boat, and somewhere behind her the black post stood in the shallows like a thing that had always been there.

She rowed back across the smooth water, and the crossing took no time at all, and the landing came up out of the dark with the lamp still burning.

Her father was sitting up in bed when she came in, and his face was not grey anymore. "You went out," he said. "You took the boat."

"I took the boat," Ila said. "And I paid what was owed."

He was quiet a long while. Then he lay back and closed his eyes, and his breathing went slow and easy, the way it used to. Ila hung the lantern by the door and set the oars in the corner, where they belonged, and they were hers now.
