Tess learned the rule the way she learned everything in Bellwether: by watching her father do it wrong. He came in from the evening route with his coat still on, and set the brass hour-key on the kitchen table beside the bread. The key was a small thing, the size of her thumb, warm from his pocket. Every lamp in town held one hour of borrowed light, he said, and every lamplighter had to bring his own hour back by midnight or the lamp would take it from somewhere else. He said it like a joke. Then he went to bed and forgot.

At eleven, Tess found him asleep in his chair, the key still on the table. She shook his shoulder. “Dad. The key.”

He mumbled something about morning and turned his face to the cushion. Outside, the lamps along Market Street burned steady and gold, each one a small held breath. Tess stood at the window and counted them. Forty-one lamps. Forty-one borrowed hours, and one of them sitting on the kitchen table, getting cold.

She had never walked the route alone. But the rule was the rule, and the clock on the mantel was already leaning toward half past eleven. She put on her coat, pocketed the key, and stepped out into the blue dark.

The first lamp on the corner took the key easily. She slid it into the little brass slot beneath the glass, felt the click, and the flame inside leaned toward her like a dog recognizing a voice. One hour returned. The lamp sighed and dimmed to its ordinary light.

“Good,” she said, and her breath made a small cloud. “One down.”

She worked her way down Market, then turned onto Cinder Lane, where the houses leaned close and the lamps were older. Most of them took their hours without complaint. But the lamp at the end of the lane, the one outside the shuttered house with the ivy, would not take anything at all.

She tried the key. The slot was warm, but nothing clicked. She tried again, pressing harder, and the flame inside guttered and went thin, then steady, then thin again, as if it were listening for something that wasn't there.

“Come on,” Tess said. “You're supposed to take it.”

The lamp did not answer. It only burned, faint and stubborn, and somewhere behind her a clock began to strike twelve.

***

The clock finished striking, and the lane went very quiet. Tess stood with the key in her hand and the lamp burning low above her, and she understood, in a slow cold way, that this lamp had already spent its hour. Someone had borrowed it and not brought it back.

She knocked on the ivy-covered door because she could not think of anything else to do. The knock sounded small and rude in the dark. For a long moment nothing happened. Then a bolt slid, and the door opened on a man so old he seemed to be made of the same grey dust as the lane.

“Mr. Vell,” Tess said. She knew him by sight, the way you know everyone in a town this size. “Your lamp won't take the hour. It's already spent.”

He looked past her at the lamp, and his face did something complicated. “I know,” he said.

“The timekeeper will come,” Tess said. “That's what Dad says. If a lamp's hour isn't returned, the timekeeper comes and collects it from the borrower.”

“I know that too.” Mr. Vell opened the door wider. Inside, a single candle burned on a side table, and beyond it, in a dim room, Tess could see a bed and a shape in it, very still. “My wife,” he said, and his voice cracked on the word. “She had hours left. Not many. The doctor said a night, maybe two. I borrowed the lamp's hour so I could sit with her a little longer. Just one more hour of her.”

Tess looked at the candle, at the bed, at the old man's hands, which were shaking. “You can't keep it,” she said. “The lamp's dark. The whole lane's darker for it.”

“I know,” he said again, and then, quieter, “I couldn't bring it back. Not while she was still breathing.”

Behind them, at the mouth of the lane, a figure appeared in the fog. Tall, thin, carrying a lantern that gave no light at all. The timekeeper walked slowly, and the fog moved out of its way.

“Tess,” Mr. Vell said. “Go home.”

But Tess did not move. The key was still warm in her pocket, and the dark lamp hung above them both, waiting.

The timekeeper stopped at the gate and did not open it. Up close, the lantern gave off no light and no heat, but the fog would not come near the glass. Under the tall hat, the face was ordinary: a woman's face, tired, with a line between her brows like a seam.

“Mr. Vell,” she said. “You have something of mine.”

“I know it.” He stood in the doorway with his cap in his hands, twisting it. “I'm not going to pretend I don't.”

“The lamp on this lane went dark at dusk. The lane has been dark since. People walk slower on a dark lane, Mr. Vell. Some of them stop walking altogether.” She said it without anger, which was worse. “An hour is not a thing you can hold in your hand and put in your pocket. It has to be somewhere. Yours is missing, so the town is missing it.”

“It was for her.” He nodded back toward the dim room, the bed, the shape in it. “One hour. I sat by her and held her hand and she knew me. She hasn't known me in three days. For one hour she knew me.” His voice went thin. “You'd have done the same.”

The timekeeper looked past him into the house, and something in her face shifted, like a door opening a crack and closing. “I might have,” she said. “But I would have brought it back.”

“I tried.” Mr. Vell's hands opened and closed. “I got as far as the lane and I couldn't make my feet go up the ladder. My wife was in that bed and the hour was still hers.” He looked at the dark lamp overhead. “Take it from me now, then. Take whatever's left of me. I'm old. I haven't got much.”

“It doesn't work that way.” The timekeeper set her lantern down on the gatepost. “An hour spent is spent. What I collect is what's owed. The lamp needs an hour to burn, and someone has to give one.”

Tess heard her own voice before she decided to use it. “Take mine.”

Both of them turned. The key was in her fist, and she made herself keep her eyes up. “My hour. The one I'd have borrowed tonight. I don't need it. I'll walk the route in the dark and I'll be home before the bell.”

“Tess,” Mr. Vell said, “no.”

“You said go home,” she said. “I'm not going home. The lane's dark because of us, and my father's out there somewhere, and if I take a light out of the town to keep for myself, what does that make me?” Her voice wobbled on the last word, and she let it. “Take it.”

The timekeeper studied her for a long moment. Then she picked up her lantern and held it out, and the glass, which had been dead, was full of a soft grey glow, like the sky just before rain.

***

“Hold out your hand,” the timekeeper said.

Tess did. The air above her palm grew warm, then heavy, then cold, and something left her: not a pain, not a pulling, just a lessening, the way a room feels after someone has carried out the last chair. She thought of summer evenings and could not quite remember the smell of them. She thought of her father's laugh and heard it, but from a distance, as if through a wall.

“That's enough,” she said, and her voice sounded strange to her.

“It is done,” the timekeeper said. The grey light in her lantern had gone gold. She turned to Mr. Vell. “Your debt is paid. Go back inside. Sit with her. Whatever hours she has left are hers, and they are real, and they will not be borrowed.”

Mr. Vell looked at Tess with his mouth working. “Child,” he said. “What have you done?”

“I gave an hour,” Tess said. “You gave one too. Only I gave mine back.”

He went in and shut the door, and through the window Tess saw him sit down by the bed and take his wife's hand. The timekeeper lifted her lantern to the dark lamp on the pole, and the lamp drank the gold light and came on with a small clean sound, like a match struck in a quiet room.

“The rest of your route is unlit,” the timekeeper said. “I can't help you there. I only collect.”

“I know.”

“It's a long walk in the dark.”

“I know that too.”

“Then walk it.” The timekeeper picked up her hat and settled it. “And Tess, an hour is not the same as a light. Someone who gives an hour can still be seen.” She walked off down the lane, and the fog closed behind her, and she was gone.

Tess stood alone under the one burning lamp. The key was cold now. Ahead of her the lane ran black between the hedges, and past the lane the crossroads, and past the crossroads the square and the pier and the whole unlit town. She put the key in her pocket, and she started walking.

The lane was longer in the dark than she had ever known it. Tess walked with one hand out, counting hedges, and the key in her pocket knocked against her thigh with each step. Fog beaded on her coat. Somewhere a dog barked twice and then thought better of it.

At the crossroads she stopped. The signpost creaked, and beyond it the square lay flat and black, the lamp posts standing like fence posts in a field. She could turn back. No one would know. Her father would still be out there, and the town would wake to a dark morning and blame the fog.

“No,” she said, out loud, because the word had to go somewhere. Then she walked into the square.

Halfway across, a door opened. Light spilled out in a yellow wedge, and a woman in a dressing gown stood in it, holding a jam jar with something glowing inside.

“Tess? Is that you?” It was Mrs. Alder from the bakery.

“It's me.”

“Where's your father?”

“Late,” Tess said. “I'm doing the route.”

Mrs. Alder looked at the dark lamp above her door, and then at the jar in her hands. “I've been saving this,” she said. “For a rainy night. Or a bad one.” She held it up. “It's not much of an hour. But it's mine.”

“You don't have to.”

“I know.” Mrs. Alder carried the jar to the lamp post, unscrewed the little door at its base, and tipped the light in. The lamp came on, soft and gold, and the square had a centre again.

Across the square another door opened. Then another. People came out in coats over nightclothes, carrying jars and lanterns and one old tin with a handle. Tess watched them walk to their own lamp posts and feed the light in, one by one, and the town lit up around her like a sentence being written.

Old Mr. Vell stood on his step with his hands in his pockets. He didn't have a jar. When Tess passed, he said, “I gave mine already.”

“I know,” she said.

“It was worth it.”

“I know that too.”

The pier lamps came on last, out over the water, and the fog turned gold above them. Tess stood at the end of the route with the empty key in her hand and the whole town burning behind her, and she did not feel the cold at all.

***

The walk home was short and strange. Every window she passed had a light in it, and every light had a person behind it who had chosen to spend an hour on the town instead of on themselves. Tess kept her head down and her hands in her pockets. Her own hour was gone. She could feel the place where it had been, a small hollow ache, like a tooth that had come out.

At the gate she stopped. The kitchen lamp was on.

She pushed the door open and her father was sitting at the table with his boots off and a cup of tea going cold in front of him. He looked up, and his face did something complicated.

“Tess.”

“You're back.”

“I'm back.” He stood. “I got turned around in the fog past the mill. Took me half the night to find the road. When I came in and you were gone…” He stopped. “The whole town's lit.”

“They did it themselves,” Tess said. “I just walked.”

He looked at her for a long moment. “You gave your hour.”

“How did you know?”

“Because I know what it costs.” He came around the table and put his hands on her shoulders. “You didn't have to do that.”

“Yes,” Tess said. “I did.”

Her father nodded slowly, and then he did something he had not done since she was small. He pulled her in and held her, and she stood there with her face against his coat and listened to the town breathing outside, lamp by lamp by lamp.

“I'll take the route tomorrow,” he said.

“We'll both take it,” she said.

He laughed, once, tired and warm. “All right.”

Outside the window the lamps burned on borrowed time, every hour of it freely given, and Tess sat down at the table and drank her father's cold tea and let the night go on without her.
