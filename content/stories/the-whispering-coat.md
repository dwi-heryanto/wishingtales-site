Nell found the coat on a Tuesday, at the bottom of the lost-and-found bin behind the school office. It was heavy blue wool, too big for her, with a collar that smelled of salt and rain.

"That old thing?" said Mr. Pettigrew, the caretaker, when she asked. "Been in there since spring. Nobody's claimed it. Take it if you want."

So Nell took it home. She hung it on the hook by the back door, and at supper it was quiet. But when the house went dark and the stairs stopped creaking, the coat began to whisper.

It was a soft sound, like wind under a door. Nell sat up in bed and listened. The whisper came from downstairs, steady and patient, and it was not a sound she could ignore.

"Mum," she said at breakfast, "does the coat make a noise?"

Her mother buttered toast. "Coats don't make noises, love. You're imagining it."

But that night it was louder. Nell crept down in her socks and stood in the kitchen. The moon made a pale square on the floor. The coat hung on its hook, and from its folds came a hush, hush, hush, like someone turning pages in a very old book.

She reached out and touched the sleeve. It was warm, as if someone had just been wearing it.

"Who are you?" she whispered.

The coat said nothing. But in the morning, when she pulled it on to test it, she felt the collar settle against her neck like a hand, and the whispers seemed almost glad.

***

The next night the whispers changed. Nell lay still and let them come, and slowly the sounds sorted themselves into words.

Come. Come down. Come along.

She got up. The floorboards were cold. The coat was waiting, and when she lifted it off the hook it folded itself around her shoulders without a fuss, as if it had been waiting all evening.

"Where?" Nell asked.

Down. Along. To the bell.

The old bell tower stood at the end of the lane, above the harbour. Nobody rang it now. Nell had walked past it a hundred times and never once seen its door open. But the whispers pulled her along like a rope, gentle and certain, past the shuttered shops and the sleeping boats.

The tower door was shut and bolted. Nell stood in the dark and felt foolish.

Then the coat tugged. Her feet turned to the left, along the wall, to a place where ivy hung in a thick green curtain.

Behind the ivy was a door. It was small and low, made of grey wood, with no handle that Nell could see.

"Hello?" she said.

The whispers answered, soft as breath. Push.

She pushed. The door swung inward, and a cold smell of stone and salt came out to meet her. Somewhere above, in the dark of the tower, something moved.

Nell looked back at the sleeping houses. Then she stepped inside, and the coat hummed against her back, warm as a hand.

Inside, the tower was a cold stone tube. Steps curled up into darkness. Nell stood still, and the coat went quiet. "Hello?" she said again, and her voice went up and up and got lost.

Then a boy stepped out from behind the stairs. He was thin and grey, like a drawing left in the rain. "You brought it," he whispered. "My coat."

Nell held the collar tight. "Is it yours?"

"I lost it," he said. "On the sand. A long time ago. Please take it to the sea."

His voice was soft as dust. He didn't come closer. "If I take it back, the sea will remember me," he said. "And I can rest."

Nell looked at the coat. It felt warm and heavy on her shoulders. "Why didn't you come for it yourself?" she asked.

"I tried," he said. "The coat wouldn't let me. It wants to be worn." His grey hand lifted, then fell. "Please."

Outside, the sea was breathing in and out. Nell nodded. "I'll try."

***

The harbour steps were slick with weed. Nell stood at the bottom, the coat still on her back. The whispers had gone quiet. She took a breath and pulled at the sleeve.

The coat clung. It wrapped tight around her arms like a hug that wouldn't let go. "Off," she said, and pulled harder.

Then the whispers came back. They weren't soft now. They hissed, and they pushed words into her ears. Stay. Stay. The sea is cold.

Nell stumbled on the wet stone. "No!" she shouted. "It's not mine!"

The coat pulled her toward the water. She dug her heels in. Behind her, the shadow boy waited at the top of the steps. He didn't speak. He only watched.

Nell's heart banged. She could let the coat win. It was warm, and it wanted her. Or she could fight it, and maybe lose it forever.

"Help me," she whispered.

But the boy only shook his grey head. "I can't," he said. "It's your turn now."

Nell closed her eyes. She thought of her grandmother's voice, soft and low. And she began to sing.

Nell sang the lullaby her grandmother used to hum when the house was dark and the wind was loud. It was slow and small, about a boat rocking on a quiet sea, and a light that never went out. Her voice shook at first. The coat pulled harder, and the whispers hissed like water on hot stones.

"Hush now," Nell sang, because that was the next line. "Hush now, the boat is small. Hush now, the sea is wide." The pulling eased. She took a step back from the water, and the coat loosened its grip on her arms.

The shadow boy came down the steps. His grey face was close now, and she could see he was smiling, just a little. "That's the one," he said. "My mother used to sing that."

"My gran taught me," Nell said, between verses. The coat softened against her shoulders. The whispers turned to a low murmur, like the sea in a shell. Nell kept singing, and the boy stood beside her, his grey hand hovering near the sleeve.

"You can let go now," she told the coat. "I'll take you home." The coat slid from her shoulders and lay in her arms, heavy and warm, like a sleeping cat.

The boy's edges began to blur. "Thank you," he said. "I waited so long." His smile stayed as he faded, until there was only the grey light of the tunnel and the sound of the sea.

***

Nell carried the coat up the stone steps, and the whispers were quiet. Outside, the night was clear and the sea was calm. She walked to the edge of the old harbour wall, where the water was deep and dark.

"Here we are," she said, and she held the coat out over the water. It didn't fight her. It hung there, heavy and soft, and she let it drop.

The coat floated for a moment, then sank slowly, like a leaf in a pond. The whispers faded to a hum, low and steady, like a bee in a jar. Nell stood and listened until the hum was only the sea.

On the way home, the street was quiet. Her mother was at the door. "You're late," she said. "I was worried."

"I found something," Nell said. "And I gave it back."

Her mother looked at her for a moment. "You look different," she said. "Taller, somehow."

Nell smiled. That night, she lay in bed and listened. Far away, under the sea, the hum was still there. It was a small, kind sound, like a lullaby half remembered. She closed her eyes and slept without dreaming.
