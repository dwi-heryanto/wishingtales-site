Cora's father buttoned his coat and set his cap straight. "I'll be back on the evening tide," he said. "Keep the fire in and the door bolted." He looked up at the tower. "And don't climb it alone. Not for anything."

"I won't," Cora said. She had heard it a hundred times.

He rowed out past the breakwater. Cora watched until his boat was a dark speck on the gray sea. Then she went inside and latched the door.

By supper, the fog had come. It rolled in thick and soft, swallowing the harbor lights one by one. Cora stood at the kitchen window and could not see the tower's base.

Up in the lamp room, the great light flickered. Once. Twice. Then it went out.

Cora's heart thumped. She climbed onto the window seat and pressed her nose to the cold glass. "Papa," she whispered, "where are you?"

The fog pressed against the panes like a face. Somewhere out in it, a bell buoy clanged. Then another. The sea was full of boats that could not see.

She ran to the door, then stopped. The tower steps were dark and narrow. Her father's voice was in her head: not alone, not alone.

But the light was out. And the sea was full of boats.

***

Cora lit a candle and stepped onto the path. Fog curled around her ankles and hid the tower until she was almost touching it. The door at the base was unlocked.

As she pulled it open, she heard the voice.

It came from everywhere and nowhere, thin and dry as old rope. "One," it said. "Two. Three."

Cora froze. "Who's there?"

No answer. Only the counting. "Four. Five."

She climbed the spiral stairs, one hand on the cold wall. The voice kept counting, steady as a heartbeat. At the top of the stairs, she stopped.

"Nine. Ten." A pause. Then, softer, "Eleven."

Cora knew the boats in the harbor. She had counted them herself that morning. There were ten. Ten boats, and now the voice had counted eleven—and then stopped.

"Eleven," it said again, and this time the word hung in the air like a question.

Cora's mouth went dry. Her father was the eleventh boat. He was out there in the fog, and the voice had counted him, and then it had gone quiet.

The lamp room door was above her. She had to relight the lamp. She had to show her father the way home.

"Hold on, Papa," she said, and her voice shook. "I'm coming."

The stairs went up and up. Cora took them two at a time, then one at a time, her breath scraping her throat. The fog pressed against the tower windows, thick as wool. At the top, she grabbed the lamp room door and pulled.

It didn't move.

"No," she whispered. She pulled again, harder. The door held fast. A brass plate gleamed near the handle, and when she bent close she saw the keyhole. Locked. Her father never locked it. He said a locked door was a door that couldn't save anyone.

She ran back down to the keeper's room. The spare key hung on a nail by the stove—except it didn't. The nail was bare. She checked the hook, the shelf, the floor. Nothing.

"Where is it?" she said, her voice cracking. "Papa, where did you put it?"

The fog answered with a soft, dry sound. Like paper turning.

Cora yanked open the desk drawer. It was full of old charts, a broken pencil, a tin of matches, and a key. A brass key, green at the edges, heavier than it looked. She had never seen it before.

The stairs seemed longer going up. Her legs burned. The fog pressed cold against the windows. At the top, she slid the key into the lock. It turned with a click that sounded like a door exhaling.

***

The lamp room was dark and cold. The great lamp sat in its glass cage, and the wick was black. Cora struck a match. It flared, then went out. She struck another. The flame bent away from the wick as if a wind had pushed it.

"Come on," she said. "Please."

The match died. The cold in the room deepened. Then the voice came again, close and dry, right beside her ear.

"Eleven," it whispered. "Eleven."

Cora's hands shook. "Stop it," she said. "He's not lost. He's coming home."

The fog pressed against the glass. The voice said nothing more, but the cold stayed, patient as a held breath. Cora struck a third match, and this time she remembered her father's words. He had told her once, late at night, that a lamp fed on oil alone could fail. A lamp fed on something true would always burn.

"True," she whispered. "Something true."

She looked at the dark wick. She thought of her father out there, counting boats in the fog. She thought of the empty nail by the stove. She thought of how loud the tower was when she was alone in it.

And then she knew what to say.

Cora set the burnt match down on the brass rail. The fog breathed against the glass, and the cold stayed close, waiting. She put both hands flat on the cold iron of the lamp stand and spoke into the dim room.

"I'm scared," she said. Her voice sounded small, but it did not shake. "I'm alone up here and I'm scared. That's true."

The wick flickered. Not a flame, just a shiver, like a held breath let go.

"I'm scared the fog will keep him," she went on. "I'm scared I'm too small to fix this. I'm scared of the counting."

Something in the glass changed. The black wick glowed a deep red, then orange. A small flame stood up straight, steady as a candle in a still room. Cora stared at it. The cold loosened its grip on her arms.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's it."

She turned the wick up. The flame grew, filled the glass, and threw a long golden arm out into the fog. The beam swung and swept and swung again. Outside, the white wall thinned. The counting voice did not speak again. Where the light touched, the fog turned grey, then pearl, then let the dark through.

Cora leaned her forehead against the cool glass. Below, the sea moved in long, quiet breaths. Somewhere out in the grey, a gull called once, and then the fog began to lift like a blanket pulled back from a bed.

***

Cora stayed in the lamp room until the beam found the harbor mouth. She watched the fog peel away in strips. First the near rocks showed their wet shoulders. Then the buoy, bobbing. Then, low and slow, a dark shape came through the gap in the breakwater.

Her father's boat.

She pressed both hands to the glass. The little boat rocked in on the swell, its lantern swinging at the bow. Her father stood at the tiller, hat pulled low, one hand raised toward the tower. Cora raised hers back, though he could not see her.

By the time she climbed down, the kitchen was warm with the smell of wet wool and coffee. Her father came in shaking water from his coat. He looked at the burning lamp in the tower window, then at her.

"You lit it," he said.

"I did." Cora held up the brass key. "The spare was gone. I found this one."

He was quiet a moment. "That was my father's key," he said at last. "I thought I'd lost it."

"I fed it something true," Cora said.

Her father nodded slowly, as if that made perfect sense. Then he found a piece of string in the drawer and tied the key around her neck. The brass sat warm against her chest.

"Next fog," he said, "you'll know what to do."

Cora looked up at the tower, dark now except for its steady light. "Next fog," she agreed.
