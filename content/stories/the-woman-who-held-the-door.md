Maren kept her watch where the moor met the sky, at a door that stood alone in the heather. Iron, it was, black and seamed with rust, set into a stone frame with no wall around it. The wind came off the hills and worried at her cloak, but the door never moved. It had not moved in three hundred years.

She had a stool, a lantern, and a stone bottle of water. That was the whole of her house. The village was a mile down the valley, and once a day, if the weather allowed, someone climbed the path to bring her bread and news. Mostly it was Tam.

He came up the track now, a lanky boy of thirteen with a sack over his shoulder and mud to his knees. "Maren," he said, setting the sack down. "Bread. Bit of cheese. The miller's wife sent an apple."

"Tell her thanks." Maren took the loaf and broke it. The crust was still warm. She ate slowly, watching the light go gold over the hills.

Tam sat on a flat stone and pulled his knees up. He always stayed a while. "You never go down," he said. "Not once. Not even at Midwinter."

"Someone has to watch."

"Watch what? It's a door in the middle of nothing." He squinted at it. "There's no house. No hill behind it. It just stands there."

Maren chewed her bread. "That's the point of it."

"But why you?" Tam picked at a loose thread on his sleeve. "You're not old. You could have a house. A garden. A—" He stopped, embarrassed.

"A life?" Maren smiled. "I had one, once. Before I took the post."

"What happened to it?"

The wind dropped. In the quiet, the heather rustled, and Maren heard the small sounds of evening: a lark, a stream somewhere under the ground, Tam's breathing. She looked at the door. It was just iron, and it was the most important thing in the world.

"I gave it up," she said. "That's all."

Tam opened his mouth to ask more, but the sun touched the horizon and the first star came out, and he forgot. They sat together while the moor turned blue and the lantern made a small yellow circle on the grass.

***

At moonrise, the knocking began.

Three soft taps, polite as a visitor. Maren set down her cup and stood. Tam scrambled up beside her.

"Who's there?" he called.

"Hush," Maren said. She did not move toward the door. She never did.

The taps came again, slower. Then a voice, low and pleasant, as if it were smiling. "Maren. I know you're there."

Tam's eyes went wide. "It knows your name."

"It always does." Maren's voice was flat. "Sit down, Tam. Don't answer it."

But the voice went on, warm as a fire on a cold night. "Three hundred years, Maren. Don't you want to know what's on the other side?"

Maren said nothing. Her hands were steady. She had learned to keep them steady.

Tam tugged at her sleeve. "What is it? What's behind the door?"

"Nothing you want to meet." She sat back down on her stool, facing the door, and after a moment Tam sat too, close to her. The lantern flickered. The voice did not knock again, but it did not go away either. It waited, the way a cat waits at a mouse hole, patient and certain.

"Tell me," Tam whispered. "Tell me why you're here."

Maren looked at the iron door. The rust had made patterns like old writing. "The first keeper was a woman named Ilsa," she said. "She lived in the village when it was smaller. One winter the door appeared on the moor, and things came through it. Bad things. Crops failed. Children got sick. The village was dying."

Tam was very still.

"Ilsa made a bargain. She would keep the door shut, and watch it every night, and in return the door would stay closed. But the bargain had a price. The keeper does not age, and the keeper does not leave. Not until someone else takes the watch."

"So you—" Tam's voice cracked. "You've been here three hundred years?"

"No," Maren said. "Ilsa was. I came after. There have been seven of us. Each one took the watch from the last." She looked at him. "And none of us opened the door."

The voice behind the iron laughed, soft and fond. "Not yet," it said.

Tam left at first light, the way he always did, and Maren watched him go until the moor swallowed him. Then she sat with her back to the door, as she had for three hundred years, and waited for the dark. It came early that night, and with it came the voice again.

"Maren," it said. Not through the mouse hole this time. Through the whole door, low and warm, like a hand on her shoulder. "I know what you want. I've always known."

She didn't answer. She never answered.

"You want to see the sea," the voice said. "You told Ilsa once, when you were a girl. You said you'd never seen it. Three hundred years, Maren, and you've never seen the sea."

Her hands were cold. She put them in her lap.

"I can show you," the voice said. "Open the door, and I'll take you there. Not a trick. Not a bargain. Just the sea."

Maren stood up. She didn't mean to. Her body stood the way a body does when it has been waiting too long. The iron was warm under her palm, warmer than it should have been. Her fingers found the latch. It was stiff with rust, but it moved. It moved.

Behind her, a boot scuffed on stone.

"Maren?" Tam's voice, small and tight. "What are you doing?"

She turned. He was standing at the edge of the torchlight, his face white, his bread sack forgotten on the ground. He had come back. He had come back early, and he was looking at her hand on the latch.

"Nothing," she said. "I wasn't—"

"You were going to open it."

"No." She pulled her hand away. The latch was cold again, and her palm ached. "I wasn't. I wouldn't."

Tam didn't move. "You said none of you ever opened it. You said that."

"And none of us did." She stepped away from the door, and her legs felt like they belonged to someone else. "Go home, Tam. It's late."

He didn't go. He stood there, breathing hard, and then he said, "I heard it. I heard what it said. About the sea."

Maren closed her eyes. "It lies."

"Does it?"

She had no answer for that. The voice behind the door laughed, soft and fond, and Tam flinched like he'd been struck.

***

Tam came back at midday, and he did not come alone. Three village elders walked behind him: old Renn, who kept the mill, and his sister Greta, and a thin man named Colm who had never once spoken to Maren in all her years at the door. They stopped well back from the iron, and Renn folded his arms.

"The boy says the door spoke," Renn said. "He says you nearly opened it."

"The door always speaks," Maren said. "It's been speaking for three hundred years. I've never opened it."

"He says you put your hand on the latch."

She looked at Tam. He wouldn't meet her eyes. "I did," she said. "And then I stopped."

Greta stepped forward. "How do we know it's stopped? How do we know you won't try again tonight, when there's no one here to see?"

"Because I've held it for three hundred years."

"That's not proof," Colm said. It was the first time she'd heard his voice, and it was flat and tired. "Open it. Open the door and show us there's nothing behind it."

"No."

"Open it," Renn said, "and we'll believe you. Open it and we'll bring you food again, and leave you be."

Maren looked at the iron. The rust had made patterns like old writing, and she had read them a thousand times. "If I open it," she said, "you won't have a village by morning. You won't have a moor. You'll have whatever came through, and it won't stop at the door."

Renn's jaw tightened. "Then you leave us no choice."

They took the bread sack. Greta picked it up off the ground and held it against her chest, and Tam stood very still and did not say a word. When they walked away, he went with them.

Maren sat down against the door. The voice behind it was quiet for a long time. Then it said, gently, "They'll be back. They always come back. And next time, they'll bring torches."

The first day without bread, Maren ate the last of her oats. The second day she boiled nettles. On the third she sat with her back against the iron and did not bother to get up.

Hunger was a different kind of cold. It started in the hands and worked inward. She watched the sun cross the moor and tried not to count the hours.

Behind her, the voice had been quiet since the elders left. That was worse than the talking. Silence meant it was thinking.

On the fourth night, it spoke.

"Maren."

She didn't answer.

"Maren, it's me." The voice was Tam's. High and cracking at the edges, the way it went when he was frightened. "Maren, please. They've gone. The elders went home and I came back alone and there's something on the moor behind me. Maren, open the door."

Her hand was on the latch before she knew it had moved. The iron was cold and rough under her palm.

"I can hear you breathing," Tam's voice said. "I know you're there. Please. It's right behind me."

She had heard Tam frightened before. Once a dog had cornered him by the standing stone and he had shouted for her exactly like that, and she had come out with her stick and driven the dog off. He had cried then, a little, and pretended he hadn't.

This was not Tam.

She made herself listen past the voice. The wind moved over the moor. A curlew called twice and stopped. There was no running, no breath, no boots on stone.

The voice tried again, softer. "Maren. I'm sorry about the bread. I should have—"

"Stop," she said.

The voice stopped.

Then it laughed, and it was not Tam's laugh, and it was not any laugh she had heard in three hundred years. It was low and pleased and it went on too long.

"You're learning," it said, in its own voice now. "I wondered how long it would take."

Maren sat down against the door and put her hands over her ears. It didn't help. The voice didn't need her ears.

"Open it," it said, "and I'll give you back the boy. Not a copy. The real one. He's out there somewhere, cold and alone, and you're the only one who could let me fetch him."

She could not tell if that was a lie. That was the worst of it. She could not tell.

She stayed against the door.

***

The night went on. The voice tried everything.

It offered her food, and it was not lying about the food; she could smell bread, hot and new, and her stomach turned over so hard she had to press her hand against it. It offered her sleep, and a bed, and the sound of rain on a roof, and a hundred other small kind things she had not had in three hundred years.

She held the latch and did not turn it.

When offering failed, it tried other voices. Greta calling her name. Renn shouting that the torches were lit. Colm saying, we were wrong, open it, we were wrong. Each one was perfect. Each one was a little wrong somewhere, in a way she could hear if she listened hard enough.

Near dawn it went quiet for a long time. Then it said, in its own voice, almost gently, "You'll die out here. You know that. Starve against a door and no one will ever know what you held shut."

"I'll know," Maren said.

It did not answer.

The sky went grey, then pink, then gold. The voice did not speak again. The knocking had stopped some time in the dark, and she had not noticed when.

She sat with her back against the iron until the sun was fully up and the moor was warm. Then she let go of the latch, and her hands were shaking, and she was so hungry she could hardly stand.

That was when she heard boots on the path.

She got up. She got her stick. If it was the voice trying again, she would not be fooled twice.

But it was Tam. He came over the rise at a run, and behind him came a girl she did not know, tall and thin, with a pack on her back and a set to her jaw that Maren recognised.

Tam stopped short when he saw her. "You're alive," he said. "I thought— when they took the bread—"

"I'm alive," Maren said. Her voice came out rough. "Who's this?"

The girl stepped forward. "My name's Sera. Tam came to my village and told me what you do here."

Maren looked at Tam. He looked at the ground and then at her.

"I couldn't leave you out here alone," he said. "So I found someone who'd come."

Sera held out her hand, and in it was a heel of bread. "Eat," she said. "Then tell me what I need to know. I'm not afraid of a door."

Maren took the bread. It was stale and it was the best thing she had ever eaten.

"You will be," she said. "But that's all right. It's the ones who aren't afraid that open it."

She looked at the iron door, and the pattern of rust that she had read a thousand times, and then at the girl who had come to take her place.

"Sit down," she said. "I'll tell you everything."
