The bell rang at midnight, and Wren was already awake. She had been lying in the dark, listening to the house settle, when the sound came: one clear note, thin as a wire, from the empty house on Harrow Lane. It rang again. Then silence. Wren sat up, her heart knocking. The house had been empty for years, its windows boarded, its garden a tangle of dead roses. Nobody rang a bell in there.

She went to the window. Across the street, Sam's light was on. Sam was fifteen, two years older, and usually asleep by ten. But there he was, standing at his window, looking toward the empty house. Wren tapped on the glass. Sam saw her and raised a hand.

She pulled on her coat and slipped outside. The night was cold and smelled of rain. Sam met her on the pavement between their houses.

"You heard it," he said. It wasn't a question.

"A bell. Twice."

"Three times. The first one was at eleven fifty-eight. I've been counting."

They stood together, looking at the house. It leaned slightly, as if listening. The boards over the downstairs windows were grey with age. The gate hung open.

"Nobody's lived there since I was small," Wren said. "Mrs. Abernathy died, and her nephew put it up for sale. That's what Mum said."

"Then who's ringing the bell?"

A cloud moved off the moon, and the house seemed to brighten. Wren thought she saw a shape at the attic window, a pale blur that could have been a curtain. Then it was gone.

"We should tell someone," she said.

"Tell who? The police? A bell rang. They'll laugh."

Sam was right. But the sound had been so deliberate, so regular. Three rings, then nothing. As if someone was sending a message.

"Tomorrow," Sam said. "We look in daylight. Properly."

Wren nodded. They stood a moment longer, and the house stood with them, dark and patient. Then they went back inside, and Wren lay awake until the sky turned grey, listening for a fourth ring that never came.

***

By ten the next morning, the house on Harrow Lane looked almost ordinary. The sun had burned off the mist, and the dead roses were just dead roses. Sam brought a torch, though they didn't need it yet.

"We start at the front," he said. "Then the sides. Then the back."

The gate creaked when Wren pushed it. The path was cracked and tufted with weeds. The front door was solid oak, its paint peeling, and a heavy padlock hung from a hasp. No sign of forced entry.

They walked the perimeter. The downstairs windows were boarded from the inside, but one board had warped, leaving a gap. Wren peered through. Dust, a shape that might have been a chair under a sheet. Nothing moving.

"Cellar door," Sam said, pointing.

It was set into the side of the house, half hidden by a rhododendron. The door was wood, low and wide, and it had a padlock too. But this padlock was different: newer, shinier, and hanging open. The hasp had been oiled.

Wren crouched. "Someone's been here."

"Could be the estate agent."

"With an open padlock?"

Sam didn't answer. He was looking at the ground. In the dust by the cellar door, clear as a signature, was a footprint. Small, narrow, the tread worn smooth. A woman's shoe, or an old person's.

"Fresh," Sam said. "The edges haven't crumbled. This is from last night."

Wren felt a chill that had nothing to do with the wind. "So someone is living here."

"Maybe. Or someone visits."

They tried the cellar door. It wouldn't budge, though the padlock was open; something was wedged from inside. Wren put her ear to the wood. She heard nothing, but she felt it: a faint vibration, like a held breath.

"We should come back tonight," she said. "See who's ringing the bell."

Sam looked at the attic window, still boarded, still blank. "All right. But we don't go in alone. We wait, and we watch."

They walked home without speaking. The footprint stayed in Wren's mind, small and neat, pointing toward the cellar door. Someone was there, in the empty house, ringing a bell at midnight. And tonight, she would find out who.

Wren waited until the house had gone quiet. Her parents' bedroom door clicked shut at eleven, and by half past the only sounds were the fridge humming and a car passing on the main road. She pulled on her coat, eased the back door open, and slipped out into the cold.

Sam stood at the corner, hands shoved in his pockets. "You're late."

"I'm not late. You're early." She fell into step beside him. The streetlights made pools of yellow on the pavement, and between them the dark was thick. Harrow Lane smelled of wet leaves and old brick.

The house loomed at the end of the road, its windows black. They stopped across from it and waited. Ten minutes. Twenty. Wren's feet went numb. She was about to say they should give up when Sam touched her arm.

"There," he whispered.

A light moved behind the attic window. Small, warm, bobbing gently, like a candle carried from room to room. It crossed the boards that covered the glass and vanished, then reappeared at the far end.

"Someone's up there," Wren breathed. "Right now."

"We said we wouldn't go in alone."

"We're not alone. We're together." She was already crossing the road. Sam muttered something behind her, but he followed.

The front door stood ajar, just an inch, the paint cracked along its edge. Wren pushed it with one finger and it swung inward without a sound. Inside, the air was warmer than outside, and it smelled of dust and something else, something sweet and faintly burnt, like a candle snuffed too recently.

***

The hallway was narrow and lined with boxes, stacked so high they leaned against the walls. Wren stepped over a rolled rug and a stack of newspapers tied with string. Sam stayed close behind her, his breathing loud in the silence.

The stairs creaked under her weight, and she froze. Above them, the light had stopped moving. A floorboard groaned. Then, very softly, a voice said, "Who's there?"

Wren's heart hammered. She could run. She could drag Sam out the door and pretend they'd never come. But the voice was thin and old, and it shook.

"My name's Wren," she said. "I live on the next street. I heard the bell."

A long pause. Then the attic door opened, and an old woman stood in the gap, holding a candle in a brass holder. Her hair was white and pinned back, and she wore a cardigan with a button missing. Behind her, the attic was tidy: a bed, a chair, a small stove, and a shelf of books. The bell hung from a beam near the window, a dark shape in the candlelight.

"You heard it," the woman said. Not a question.

"Every night at midnight," Sam said from behind Wren. "For a week."

The woman's mouth tightened. "I didn't know it carried that far." She stepped aside, gesturing them in with the candle. "You'd better come up. I'll put the kettle on. It's cold out, and you've come a long way."

Wren climbed the last steps. The attic smelled of wool and tea. The old woman set the candle on a table and lit a second one, and the room filled with a soft, wavering light.

"My name is Mrs. Aldridge," she said. "I've lived in this house for forty years. And I'm not supposed to be here."

"Why not?" Wren asked.

The woman looked at the bell. "Because my nephew sold it," she said. "And he doesn't know I never left."

Sam sat down heavily on the edge of the chair. "So you ring the bell to... what?"

"To make the house sound occupied," Mrs. Aldridge said. "The buyers came once, and they heard it and went away. I thought if it kept ringing, they'd think it was haunted." She smiled, but her eyes were tired. "It was the only thing I could think of."

Wren looked at the bell, then at the woman's worn cardigan, at the kettle beginning to whisper on the stove. "You can't stay hidden forever," she said.

"No," Mrs. Aldridge agreed. "But I can stay hidden until Friday. That's when the new owners come to clear the house."

"And then?"

"Then I suppose I'll have to go." The old woman lifted the kettle and poured the water, and the steam rose between them like a small ghost. "Unless someone can think of something better."

Wren met Sam's eyes across the candlelit room. Neither of them said a word, but she knew exactly what he was thinking. They had until Friday. And they were not going to let her go without a fight.

On Thursday afternoon, a silver car pulled into the gravel drive of the Harrow Lane house, and Wren watched from her bedroom window as a man in a pressed shirt climbed out. He carried a clipboard and a ring of keys. Her stomach dropped. Friday had come early.

She ran to Sam's house, and they hurried down the lane together, keeping to the hedge. The man had already unlocked the front door and was standing in the hallway, frowning at the dust. "Hello?" he called. "Any squatters?" His voice echoed off the bare walls.

Wren pushed the door open wider. "Excuse me," she said. The man turned, surprised. "This is private property," he said. "I'm the nephew. I'm here to clear it out."

"We know someone who lives here," Sam said. The nephew's eyebrows shot up. "Lives here? Nobody lives here. My aunt moved to a care home months ago."

"She didn't," Wren said. "She's upstairs." She pointed at the ceiling. As if on cue, the bell in the attic gave a faint, rusty clang. The nephew's face went pale. "What was that?"

"The bell," Wren said. "She rings it every night. To keep people away." She led him up the stairs, Sam close behind. At the attic door, she knocked softly. "Mrs. Aldridge? It's Wren."

The door creaked open. The old woman stood there, smaller in daylight, her cardigan buttoned wrong. "Oh," she said, seeing her nephew. "You've come."

"Aunt Margaret?" The man's clipboard lowered. "You're supposed to be in Meadowbrook."

"I ran away," she said simply. "I hate Meadowbrook. The food is grey, and nobody rings bells." She gestured at the little brass bell on the table. "This is my home. I was born here."

The nephew rubbed his jaw. "I'm selling it, Aunt Margaret. The new owners are coming tomorrow. I've already signed." He looked at Wren and Sam. "You two shouldn't be here."

Wren stepped forward. "She's been living here all along," she said. "She's not hurting anyone. She just wants to stay."

***

The nephew, whose name was Mr. Collins, stood in the attic with his arms crossed. "I can't just call off the sale," he said. "There are contracts."

"Then change the contract," Wren said. "She's your aunt. She's family."

"I know she's family," he said, and for a moment his voice softened. "But she can't live here alone. No heat, no running water."

"There's a tap in the garden," Mrs. Aldridge said. "And I have a blanket."

Wren looked at Sam, who nodded. "What if she wasn't alone?" Wren said. "What if she was the caretaker? The house needs someone to look after it. You could write that into the sale. The new owners get a caretaker who knows every inch of the place."

Mr. Collins stared at her. "A caretaker." He looked at his aunt, at the bell on the table, at the quilt on the floor. "She'd have to sign something."

"I can sign," Mrs. Aldridge said. "I'm not senile, David. I just don't like being told what to do."

For a long moment, the attic was quiet. Then Mr. Collins sighed. "I'll make some calls," he said. "But you can't ring that bell anymore. No more haunting."

Mrs. Aldridge smiled, a real smile this time. "Then I'll ring it at noon," she said. "To call the children in for lunch."

Wren laughed. Beside her, Sam let out a breath. Downstairs, the house settled with a creak, as if it had been holding still and finally let go. The bell sat on the table, polished and waiting. Tomorrow the new owners would come, and Mrs. Aldridge would answer the door. The house on Harrow Lane would be quiet at midnight, but it would not be empty.
