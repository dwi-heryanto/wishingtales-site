Every evening the night market woke up slowly, like a cat stretching. First came the lanterns, then the smells of hot bread and cinnamon, then the voices of people calling out their wares. Mira loved all of it from her spot behind her family's honey stall.

Tonight, though, something was wrong. The lanterns were lit, but they swung the wrong way. Stalls leaned into each other. A juggler dropped three oranges in a row and stared at his own hands as if they had betrayed him.

"Mira," said her mother, frowning at the crooked rows. "Run and see if the guiding lantern is lit. The market can't find its way without it."

Mira ran. Past the cheese cart, past the woman who sold buttons, past the old man with his sleepy parrot. The guiding lantern hung on a tall pole at the market's edge, and it was supposed to burn all night with a steady gold light.

The pole was empty. Just a hook, a rope, and a small dark space where the light should have been.

"It's gone," Mira whispered.

An old woman with a basket of plums stopped beside her. "Gone? Then the market will wander off by morning. It happened once before, you know. Whole place turned up in a field, and nobody could find the honey."

Mira's stomach went tight. Her family's honey was in this market. Her whole life was in this market.

She looked down at the muddy ground. There, glowing faintly like spilled moonlight, was a footprint. Then another, and another, leading away from the pole toward the dark trees.

"I'll find it," she said. "I'll bring it back."

***

The footprints were small and bright, and they did not fade. Mira followed them to the edge of the woods, where the market's music grew thin and strange behind her.

A rough nuzzle bumped her elbow. She turned to find Pip, the market's goat, who wore a red collar and ate anything left unattended. His eyes were calm, as if he had already decided to come along.

"You can't come," Mira told him. "It's dark in there."

Pip chewed the end of her sleeve and stepped onto the first glowing print.

"Fine," she said. "But stay close."

The woods were not as dark as she expected. The footprints glowed brighter here, and the trees leaned back a little, as if making room. Pip's hooves clicked softly on the roots.

"Who would take the guiding lantern?" Mira said, mostly to hear a voice. "It's not valuable. It only shows the way."

Pip let out a low, thoughtful bleat.

"You're right," she said, though she wasn't sure he was. "Something took it. Something that wanted to hide."

Ahead, the footprints ended at the base of a wide oak, then started again on the other side, higher up, as if whatever made them had climbed. Mira's heart knocked against her ribs.

"Pip," she said. "I think we're being watched."

Above them, two round eyes blinked open in the leaves, golden and slow, like small lamps waking up.

The eyes blinked again, and then the owl leaned out from the leaves. It was grey and soft as a moth, and it looked as if it had been woken from a very good dream.

"Who goes there at this hour?" the owl asked, yawning so wide that Mira could see its small pink tongue.

"I'm Mira. This is Pip. We're looking for the night market's guiding lantern."

"Ah." The owl ruffled its feathers and settled back. "I saw a light go by. It was in a hurry and it was grumbling."

"Grumbling?"

"Lanterns grumble when they are carried upside down," said the owl. "Here is what I know, and then I am going back to sleep. It is not where the water runs, but where the water cannot go. It is caught where the wind scratches, and only a gentle hand can set it free."

Mira repeated the words to herself. Pip nibbled a fern and looked no wiser than she felt.

"Thank you," she said. "But what does it mean?"

The owl's eyes were already closing. "It means you must think, little one. Owls sleep. That is our whole job."

Up ahead, between the trees, Mira heard water. "Where the water cannot go," she whispered. "That's away from the stream, not toward it." She turned to Pip. "Come on. We go along the bank, but we stay dry."

Pip trotted after her, and the two of them followed the sound of the water through the dark.

***

The stream was wider than Mira expected. It ran fast and black between the roots, and the stepping stones were spaced too far apart for a small girl to jump.

"We'll wade," she said, mostly to be brave.

Cold water climbed her legs to the knee. Pip splashed behind her, unbothered, his little beard dripping. Halfway across, Mira's foot slipped on a slimy stone, and the small lantern she carried on a cord around her wrist swung out and struck the water.

The cord snapped. The lantern bobbed once, then slid away downstream, its little glow turning over and over until the dark swallowed it.

Mira stood still in the current. Her hands were empty. The woods pressed close around her, and for a moment she could not see her own feet.

"Pip?" Her voice came out thin.

A warm nose bumped her elbow. He was right there, solid and breathing, his coat rough under her fingers.

"All right," she said, and made herself say it slowly. "All right. I don't have my lantern anymore." She took a breath. "But I have you, and I have the riddle, and I have my feet."

The far bank was only a few steps on. She waded to it, climbed out dripping, and wrung the water from her sleeves. The dark was still dark, but her eyes had grown used to it, the way eyes do.

"Where the wind scratches," she said, and pointed into the trees.

The trees thinned, and the wind came through them in a long, scratching whisper. Mira stopped and listened. "That's it," she said. "Where the wind scratches." Pip's ears swivelled forward.

Ahead, a bramble grew thick as a hedge, and something in it was glowing. Not brightly. Just a steady amber shine, like a coin held up to a candle.

"There!" Mira ran the last few steps and dropped to her knees. The market's lantern hung deep in the thorn bush, its little door wedged shut between two branches. It glowed anyway, patient as a sleeping cat.

She reached in and yelped. A thorn caught her sleeve. Another pricked her thumb.

"Careful," she told herself. "Careful is faster."

Pip pushed his head into the bush beside her and snorted. Branches creaked. One snapped clean through, and a gap opened.

"Good goat," Mira said. She pressed the two biggest branches apart with both hands and slid the lantern free. Its handle was warm.

Something had gnawed the cord that tied it. A fox, maybe, or the wind itself. "Poor thing," she said. "You were lost too."

The lantern's glow steadied, and the thorns around it seemed smaller now. Mira looped the handle over her wrist and stood up. "Ready?"

Pip bleated once, which meant yes.

***

The way back was shorter than the way out. Mira held the lantern high, and its light laid a gold road over roots and stones. Behind her, Pip's hooves went click, click, click.

At the edge of the woods, the night market waited in the dark. Stalls stood silent. People sat on crates with their bundles in their laps, and nobody was talking.

Then a woman by the bread stall lifted her head. "Look," she said. "Look there."

Mira stepped into the square, and the lantern swung at her wrist, and light spread out across the cobbles like spilled honey. A cheer went up. Somebody laughed. Somebody else started singing.

The stall keeper with the striped awning hurried over. "You found it. You actually found it."

"It was in a thorn bush," Mira said. "Something chewed the cord. Pip broke the branches."

"Then Pip gets the first bun." The stall keeper handed the goat a warm roll, and Pip ate it in one bite.

Mira hung the lantern from its hook on the tall pole, and the market came awake around her. Lamps were lit. Voices rose. The night smelled of bread and woodsmoke again.

"You led us home," the stall keeper said.

Mira looked at her empty hands, then at Pip. "I didn't have a light for half the way," she said. "I just kept going."

She stayed until the last stall folded its awning. Then she walked home with Pip beside her, and she did not mind the dark at all.
