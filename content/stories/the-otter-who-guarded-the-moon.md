Sedge the otter came out of the river with a fish in his teeth and a splash of silver on his whiskers. The night was warm and the water was slow, and he liked the way the shallows tugged at his paws. Then he saw it. A round white light lay on the riverbed, glowing up through the water like a lantern dropped by someone careless.

Sedge dropped the fish. He paddled closer, slow and careful. The light was the moon. It was lying in the shallows, half sunk in the mud, and it pulsed gently, dim as a candle at the end of its wick.

"You are not supposed to be down here," Sedge said. He put both paws under the moon and pushed. It was lighter than he expected and heavier than he hoped. It rolled, wobbled, and settled back into the mud with a soft squelch.

He tried again. He braced his back legs on a stone and shoved with his shoulders. The moon rose a little, then slid sideways and plopped down again. Water lapped over its face, and for a moment the whole river went dark.

Sedge sat back in the shallows, breathing hard. "Up there," he told it, pointing his nose at the black sky. "That is where you belong. Everyone knows that."

The moon said nothing. It only glowed, patient and dim, like a creature waiting to be helped.

Sedge looked at the empty sky and the dark hills and the river running quietly past. Somewhere above, the stars were out, but they seemed smaller without their friend.

"All right," he said. "I will think of something."

***

The older otters gathered on the flat rock where the river bends. Sedge told them everything, all in a rush, his tail thumping the stone.

"The moon is in the shallows," he said. "I tried to push it up. It will not go."

Old Bracken, who had grey in his fur and a long scar on one shoulder, blinked slowly. "You tried to throw the moon?"

"I tried to push it," said Sedge.

The other otters murmured. A young one named Tilla put her paw over her mouth.

"The moon cannot be thrown," said Bracken. "It must be carried. Up to the top of the tallest pine, where the sky is closest."

Sedge looked at the pines on the ridge. The tallest one stood black against the stars, its top branches thin as whiskers. The path to it was dark and long, all roots and stones and rising ground.

"That will take all night," said Sedge.

"It will," said Bracken. "And the path is dark. But you have found it, so you must carry it."

Tilla crept closer. "Will you be scared?" she whispered.

Sedge looked at the great dark hill. His fur prickled. "A little," he admitted.

Bracken nodded. "Good. Scared is honest. Now fetch a basket. A good one, woven tight. And go."

Sedge sat still for a moment, watching the tall pine sway. Then he stood up and shook the water from his fur.

"I will need a strong basket," he said.

Sedge wove the basket himself, from reeds he pulled at the water's edge. He crossed the strands over and under, the way his mother had shown him, and pulled each one tight. The moon sat beside him on the bank, glowing soft and patient, a pale round stone that was not a stone.

"Tighter," said Bracken, watching. "If it falls through a gap, we will never find it again."

Sedge pulled the last reed hard and tucked the end in. The basket was round and deep, with a handle woven across the top. He lifted the moon and set it inside. It filled the basket like a bowl of light.

"Oh," said Tilla. "It looks happy in there."

Sedge lifted the handle over his shoulder. The basket was heavier than he expected, warm against his back. He started up the bank, and the other otters watched him go without a sound.

The river lay between him and the hill. Sedge crossed by the stepping stones, one paw at a time. The stones were wet and slick with weed. Halfway across, his hind paw slipped, and he sat down hard on the stone with a splash.

"Careful!" called Tilla from the bank.

"I am being careful," Sedge called back. He stood up, shook his whiskers, and felt the basket still safe on his shoulder. The moon glowed inside it, lighting the next stone so he could see where to put his feet. Stone by stone, he crossed. On the far bank, the path began to climb.

***

The path wound up through the roots of the pines. Sedge climbed until his legs ached. The basket bumped against his back with every step, and the moon's glow lit the ground before him, pale and steady.

Then the wind changed. It came down off the ridge, cold and sharp, and the pines began to hiss. Sedge looked up. Clouds were rolling across the stars, fast and black.

"Not now," he said. "Please not now."

A gust struck him sideways. The basket swung out from his shoulder, and Sedge grabbed the handle with both paws. Rain came all at once, hard as thrown pebbles. The path turned to mud under his feet. He crouched low and held on.

Then a branch cracked above him. Sedge flinched, and in that one moment the wind tore the basket from his paws. It tumbled down the slope, bouncing and rolling, and splashed into the rapids below.

"No!" Sedge shouted. He scrambled down the bank, sliding on wet needles. The river was white and roaring. Far out in the churn, a small glow bobbed and spun.

Sedge did not stop to think. He dove. The cold hit him like a wall, and the current shoved him sideways. He kicked hard, fighting upstream, his eyes on the glow. Water filled his ears and his nose. The glow dipped under, then rose again, and he lunged.

His paw closed on the basket handle. The moon was still inside, still glowing, still whole. Sedge hugged the basket to his chest and let the current carry him to the shallows. He crawled out, shaking and gasping, and lay on the wet stones with the basket pressed against his heart.

"Got you," he whispered. The moon glowed back at him, calm as ever.

Sedge lay on the cold stones until his breath came back. Then he sat up and looked at the tall pine at the top of the bank. It rose into the dark like a ladder with no end.

"All the way up there," he said. "Of course it is."

The moon glowed softly in the basket. Sedge tucked the handle in his teeth and began to climb. The bark was rough and sticky with sap. His claws found small holds, and his tail wound around the trunk for balance.

Halfway up, the wind shook the branches. Sedge pressed flat against the trunk and squeezed his eyes shut. The glow from the basket warmed his chin.

"You are not heavy," he told the moon. "You are just bright."

He climbed on. Higher up, the branches grew thin and swayed. A gust knocked him sideways, and one hind paw slipped. Sedge hung by three paws, heart pounding. Below him the river was a black ribbon with silver threads.

"Don't look down," he said. "Look up."

So he did. The top branch pointed straight at the empty sky where the moon belonged. Sedge gripped the basket tighter and pulled himself up the last few feet. The branch bent under him, creaking. He sat in the crook of it, swaying, and lifted the basket onto his knees.

The wind died. The whole valley seemed to hold its breath.

***

Sedge opened the basket lid. The moon filled it, round and white and humming like a sleepy bee. He cupped it in both paws and lifted it above his head. It was lighter than he expected, light as a bubble, light as a breath.

"Go home," he said.

He pushed it upward. The moon rose out of his paws, slow at first, then faster, climbing the dark the way Sedge had climbed the pine. It floated up and up and settled into the empty place in the sky. Light spilled out over the water and the trees.

Below, the otters began to cheer. Their voices came up through the branches, small and wild.

"Sedge did it!" someone shouted.

"Look at the river!" called another. "I can see every stone!"

Sedge clung to the top branch and looked down. The whole valley glowed. The shallows were silver, the pine needles were silver, and the faces of the otters were bright as lamps. His mother stood at the bank with her paws over her mouth.

Carefully, Sedge climbed down. When his feet touched the stones, the other otters crowded around him, nudging and chattering. His mother pressed her nose to his.

"You carried the moon all that way," she said.

Sedge looked up at the sky. "It was heavy at first," he said. "Then it wasn't."

He sat on the bank with his family and watched the moon float over the river. From then on, whenever the night was dark, Sedge would find the highest rock and look up, and the moon always seemed to glow a little brighter for him.
