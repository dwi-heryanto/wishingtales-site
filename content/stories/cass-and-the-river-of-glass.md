The heat had been wrong for days. Not the honest heat of July, which smells of tar and cut grass, but a dry, still heat that made the dogs lie flat in the shade and the swallows fly low. Cass felt it on her skin as she walked the towpath, and she felt something else too: a cold that came up through her boots from the ground itself.

Then she saw the river.

It was frozen. Not a skin of ice at the edges, not a few floes spinning in the current, but solid from bank to bank, a grey-white plain that ran upstream and vanished around the bend. The willow branches hung down and touched it. A dragonfly was stuck in the surface near her feet, wings spread, perfectly still.

Cass crouched and put her palm against the ice. It was cold the way a window is cold in winter, and under her hand she could feel a faint vibration, like a struck tuning fork held to a table.

"That's not possible," she said, to no one.

She stood and looked upstream. There was a path on the ice, a smooth pale line that wound between the frozen ripples, and it led away from her, north, toward the old bridge. The air above it shimmered slightly, as if the path were warmer than the rest.

She tested the ice with her heel. It held. Then she stepped onto the river.

It did not crack. It did not even creak. Her boots made no sound at all, and the vibration under her feet grew stronger, a low hum that she felt in her teeth. The path drew her forward, and she followed it, because the river should not be like this, and someone ought to find out why.

The banks fell away. The town shrank behind her. The frozen surface stretched ahead, and the hum deepened, and somewhere far upstream a bell began to ring, slow and muffled, as if from underwater.

***

The path ended at a wide pool of black ice, and in the middle of the pool stood a figure.

He was an old man in a coat that dripped, though there was no water on the ice. His hair was wet and plastered to his head, and his eyes were the pale grey of the frozen river. He held a lantern that gave no light.

"You shouldn't be here," he said. His voice was hoarse, as if he had been shouting for a long time. "The ice is thin where you stand."

Cass looked down. The ice under her boots was clear as glass, and below it, far below, she saw the dark shape of a tower, and a bell hanging silent in its frame.

"Who are you?" she asked.

"I kept the bell," he said. "The river bell. It rings the thaw. Every spring I rang it, and the ice went out, and the water ran warm again. But this year I went down to ring it and the river closed over me, and now the bell is drowned, and the river is frozen, and it will stay frozen until someone rings it again."

"Then ring it," Cass said.

"I can't." He held up his lantern, and she saw that his hands were transparent, and that the lantern was not lit. "I'm drowned. The bell needs a living hand. And it must be rung before dawn, or the river will never thaw. Not this year, not ever. The cold will spread."

Cass looked at the tower under the ice. It was deep, and the water around it was black, and the bell hung at the bottom like a stone.

"Dawn is soon," the keeper said. "You have until the sky turns grey. After that, the ice will be too thick to break, and you'll be trapped down there with me."

Cass took a breath. The cold air tasted of iron.

"How do I get down?" she asked.

The keeper showed her the gap where the ice had thinned above the tower door, a pale circle like a looking-glass laid flat on the river. "Go down feet first," he said. "Don't fight the cold. It'll take your breath either way. Let it."

Cass knelt, and the ice bit through her knees. Below the glassy skin the water was green going to black, and the tower stood in it, its door half-swallowed by silt. She had never been under water in her life except the shallow end of the mill pond, where you could stand.

"Count," the keeper said. "Count and don't think."

She broke the skin. The cold went through her like a nail. Her body tried to gasp and there was no air to gasp, so she clamped her mouth and let herself sink. The green closed over her head. The tower door gaped to her left. Her chest began to burn. Silt rose in slow clouds from her kicking. She found the doorframe with her hands and pulled herself through. Her shoulder caught on something soft that gave way and was only weed.

Inside, the dark was total. Her lungs were a fist now. She felt along a wall and found stone, then stairs, and she went up them blind. Her knee struck a step and she tasted blood and kept going. A light bloomed behind her eyes that was not light. Her arms went heavy. She thought, I am going to breathe in, and she clamped her teeth and crawled. The stairs ended. There was a room, and in the room there was a rope.

It hung from the dark above her like a root. Her fingers closed on it. The burning in her chest burst into stars, and she thought of the keeper's face, and she pulled.

***

The bell spoke. Underwater it was not a sound but a pressure, a shove against her ribs that knocked the last air out of her. The rope went slack, then taut, and the bell spoke again, and the stones around her began to move.

A crack opened in the wall beside her hand, and water came through it in a bright thread, then a jet, then a torrent. The floor tilted. She was thrown against the stairwell, and the current took her, and she clawed at the doorframe as the whole tower shrugged and the steps behind her folded down into the dark. Dust and silt blinded her. Somewhere above, the ice groaned like a ship's hull.

The rope was still in her fist. She hauled on it, and the bell swung and struck, and struck again, and each time the tower shook harder. She understood, then, with the water roaring past her ears: the bell was not just ringing. It was waking the river, and the river was coming up through the cracks to answer.

The current lifted her off her feet and carried her out through the door, tumbling, and she saw the pale circle of the gap far above her, and the ice across it breaking into white plates. Her lungs were empty. Her arms would not obey. She reached up anyway, the way you reach for a light switch in a dark room, and her hand closed on nothing, and the water took her down.

Then something caught her collar and pulled.

The keeper's voice was close to her ear, thin as a wire. "Not you," he said. "Not this one." The pull dragged her up through the boiling green. The ice plates slammed together around her shoulders. She was through. The cold air hit her face. She coughed river water onto the frozen bank and could not stop coughing. The whole river behind her was splitting apart with a noise like a hundred doors thrown open at once.

Cass lay on her side on the frozen bank, coughing until her ribs ached. Each breath came in a raw scrape. Behind her the river roared, a sound she had never heard it make—not the summer chuckle over stones, but a deep, breaking groan, like a barn coming down.

The keeper knelt beside her, his coat dripping. Water ran off his chin and pooled in the grass. "Breathe," he said. "Slow." His hand was cold on her shoulder, but steady.

She pushed herself up on one elbow. The ice was gone. Where the solid white plate had stretched bank to bank, black water churned and boiled, carrying shards of ice the size of doors. The far bank was chewing itself apart, mud sliding into the flood. Mist rose off the surface in columns.

"You did it," the keeper said. He sounded surprised, as if he had not truly believed it until now. "I have stood at that bell for a long time, girl. I have asked. Nobody came."

"You were under there," Cass said. Her voice came out cracked. "All that time."

"I was where the bell was." He looked at the water, and something in his face loosened. "Now the bell is rung, and I am not."

She saw then that the mist was gathering around him, not around her. It curled at his boots and up his coat like smoke from a wet fire. He did not seem to mind.

"The tower," Cass said. "It fell."

"It was already gone. You rang what was left." He stood, and his knees made no sound, and the mist came up to his waist. "Go home, Cass. Walk south and do not look back at the water. It will be warm by morning."

"Will you—" she started, and did not know how to finish.

"I will be fine," he said. "Better than fine. I have been cold a long while." He smiled, and it was a tired, ordinary smile, the kind a man gives at the end of a long shift. "Thank you for the ringing."

The mist closed over him. There was no drama in it, no cry, nothing to frighten a person. One moment he was a man standing on a bank, and the next the mist had thinned and there was only river.

Cass got to her feet. Her legs shook. The flood was already easing, the roar dropping to a rush, the rush to a mutter. Ice plates knocked together and drifted downstream, and between them the water ran clear and dark and alive.

***

She woke because the sun was on her face.

Cass did not remember lying down. She was on the bank, above the waterline, her jacket bunched under her head. Grass pressed into her cheek. Her clothes were stiff with river silt, and her hair had dried in a salty knot, but she was warm—actually warm, the way you are warm in July, and she lay still a moment just to feel it.

The river ran past below her, full and easy, chuckling over the stones the way it always had. Steam lifted off it in soft ribbons. The banks were scoured bare where the flood had gone through, roots showing, a fence post leaning. Somewhere upstream a bird was already arguing with the morning.

She sat up. Her whole body complained. There was a bruise the size of an apple on her forearm and a cut on her palm she did not remember getting, and her boots were full of grit.

"Cass!"

Her father was coming down the path at a run, still in his slippers, his coat flapping. He reached her and grabbed her by both shoulders and then, instead of shouting, just held on.

"I woke up and your bed was empty," he said into her hair. "The river was frozen. The whole river, Cass. I thought—"

"I know," she said. "I know. I'm sorry."

He pulled back and looked at her, at the silt and the bruise and the wet clothes. "What happened to you?"

Cass looked at the water. It ran bright and ordinary, and there was nothing under it but stones and light.

"I rang a bell," she said.

He waited for more. When it did not come, he shook his head slowly, the way he did when he had decided to be angry later. "Come on," he said. "Let's get you home and dry before your mother wakes up."

They walked up the path together. Cass did not look back at the water. But she listened—to the mutter of it, to the small knock of a stone turning over, to the honest, careless sound of a river that had somewhere to be—and it followed her all the way home.
